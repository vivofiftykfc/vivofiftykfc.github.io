A Review of Data Fitting and Interpolation

Abstract

This article presents two fundamental approaches to transforming discrete observations into continuous functional models: data interpolation and data fitting. Interpolation requires the resulting curve to pass strictly through every data point. It is therefore suited to local estimation and exact reconstruction when the available data are highly accurate. The discussion begins with classical Lagrange and Newton polynomial interpolation, examines the Runge oscillations that arise when high-degree polynomials are used, and then turns to spline interpolation, which evolved from piecewise strategies. Cubic splines in particular have become the standard means of balancing global smoothness with local fidelity.

Data fitting, by contrast, seeks to remove the influence of random errors and reveal the broad patterns within the data. Taking least squares as its theoretical foundation, this article explains the optimization principle of finding the best trend function by minimizing the sum of squared residuals. It covers linear models, polynomial models, and nonlinear models that can be linearized, while stressing the importance of objectively evaluating goodness of fit through such measures as R² and adjusted R².

Through comparison, the review clarifies the essential differences between interpolation and fitting in their underlying assumptions, appropriate applications, and technical implementation. Its purpose is to help researchers make a sound methodological choice in response to different levels of data quality and different analytical aims, thereby supporting quantitative analysis that is both more precise and more revealing.

1. Overview

In scientific research and engineering practice, raw data usually take the form of a series of discrete observations, (x_i, y_i). To conduct deeper analysis, predict unknown values, or uncover an underlying pattern, however, we must construct a continuous function f(x) that represents those data. Data fitting and interpolation are the two principal ways of doing so. They rest on different assumptions and serve different analytical purposes.

  • Interpolation: its central assumption is that the data are perfectly accurate. The constructed curve must pass strictly through every given data point; in other words, f(x_i) = y_i must hold for every i. Interpolation is primarily used to make precise, local numerical estimates between known observations. In essence, it “fills in the gaps.”

  • Fitting or approximation: its central assumption is that the data contain noise. The curve need not pass through any particular observation. Instead, it should be smooth and reflect the overall trend of the data as closely as possible. Fitting seeks to separate random errors from the data and capture the fundamental relationship between variables. In essence, it “looks for the rule.”

2. Interpolation: Constructing an Exact Path

2.1 Polynomial Interpolation

An n-1 degree polynomial is used to pass exactly through n data points.

  • Lagrange interpolation:

    • Idea: this is a constructive method. For each data point (x_i, y_i), it carefully designs a basis function L_i(x). That function equals 1 at x_i and 0 at every other x_j (j ≠ i). The final interpolation polynomial is the weighted sum of these basis functions, with the corresponding values y_i serving as weights.

      $$ P(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} y_i L_i(x) \quad , \quad L_i(x) = \prod_{j=0, j \neq i}^{n-1} \frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j} $$

    • Comment: the method is theoretically elegant but computationally limited. Whenever a data point is added, all basis functions must be recalculated, so previous calculations cannot readily be reused. More seriously, it cannot avoid the inherent risk of high-degree polynomial interpolation.

  • Newton interpolation:

Idea: the polynomial is constructed iteratively. By introducing divided differences, the method allows each new data point to be accommodated by appending only one additional term. It therefore offers good inheritance and computational efficiency.

$$ P(x) = f(x_0) + fx_0, x_1 + \dots + f[x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}]\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}(x-x_i) $$

Comment: it is computationally superior to the Lagrange method, but it still faces the following problem.

  • Runge’s phenomenon:

This is a danger that must be taken seriously in high-degree polynomial interpolation. When a high-degree polynomial is fitted to equally spaced nodes, violent oscillations may appear near the boundaries of the interval even if the original function is smooth, producing substantial prediction errors. The lesson is clear: blindly forcing a single high-degree polynomial through every point is a hazardous approach.

2.2 Piecewise and Spline Interpolation

A more robust way to avoid Runge’s phenomenon is to abandon a single global polynomial and adopt a piecewise, divide-and-conquer strategy.

  • Piecewise linear interpolation:

Idea: this is the simplest piecewise method; adjacent data points are connected successively by straight lines.

Comment: it is simple and fast, and it never produces oscillations. The cost, however, is the presence of sharp corners at the data points. The first derivative is discontinuous, and the curve as a whole lacks the smoothness required by most physical laws.

  • Spline interpolation:

Idea: spline interpolation is a major improvement on elementary piecewise methods and is designed to ensure global smoothness. It connects low-degree polynomial pieces, usually cubic ones, and imposes continuity conditions on their derivatives at the junctions, which are called knots.

Cubic splines are the most widely used. They ensure not only that the function itself is continuous, but also that its first and second derivatives remain continuous at every knot. Consequently, the curve has no sharp corners, while its curvature also changes continuously; it is highly smooth both visually and mathematically.

Comment: spline interpolation is now the most extensively used and consistently reliable interpolation method in scientific computing. It balances exactness and smoothness remarkably well, avoids Runge’s phenomenon, and has become the standard choice for interpolation tasks.

3. Fitting: Capturing the Underlying Pattern of Data

3.1 The Method of Least Squares

Least squares is the foundational method of fitting. It turns the idea of a “best fit” into a measurable criterion.

Central idea: define the residual between a data point (x_i, y_i) and the fitted function f(x) as e_i = y_i - f(x_i). The best fitted function is the one that minimizes the sum of squared residuals, or SSR.

$$ SSR = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - f(x_i))^2 \rightarrow \min $$

Squaring is chosen not only because it has convenient mathematical properties, including differentiability and convexity, but also because it assigns a greater “penalty weight” to large errors, or outliers.

3.2 Selecting and Implementing a Fitted Model

  • Linear fitting:

    • Model: the functional relationship is assumed to be linear.

    • Solution: take partial derivatives of SSR with respect to all unknown parameters, such as a, b, and so forth, and set them to zero. This produces a system of linear equations known as the normal equations. Solving that system yields the best parameter estimates.

  • Polynomial fitting:

    • Model: the function is assumed to be a polynomial, such as f(x) = a_nx^n + \dots + a_1x + a_0.

    • Solution: its least-squares solution can likewise be reduced to a system of normal equations.

  • Nonlinear fitting:

    • Model: when theory indicates that variables have a nonlinear relationship, such as an exponential, logarithmic, or power relation, nonlinear fitting is required.

    • Strategies:

      1. Linearization: a suitable change of variables can transform a nonlinear relationship into a linear one. For example, taking logarithms on both sides of y = ae^{bx} gives ln(y) = ln(a) + bx, after which linear fitting can be applied to (x, ln(y)). This is a common and efficient technique.

      2. Iterative optimization: when a complex model cannot be linearized, numerical optimization algorithms, such as gradient descent or the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, must search iteratively and gradually approach the parameter combination that minimizes SSR.

3.3 Evaluating Goodness of Fit

Once a model has been fitted, its quality must be evaluated quantitatively.

  • Coefficient of determination, R-squared or R²:

    • Definition: it measures the percentage of the total variation in the dependent variable that the model can explain. Its value lies between 0 and 1; the closer it is to 1, the stronger the explanatory power of the model.

      $$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - f(x_i))^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2} = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST} $$

  • Adjusted R²:

    • Purpose: R² has a weakness: increasing model complexity, for example by raising the degree of a polynomial, can only increase its value. Adjusted R² therefore introduces a penalty for the number of model parameters. When models of different complexity are compared, it provides a fairer measure.

4. Conclusion

Interpolation and fitting are both foundations of data analysis. Interpolation follows a philosophy of “trusting the data” and constructs exact bridges between one point and the next. Fitting follows a philosophy of “questioning the data” and searches for an underlying rule amid noisy observations. In practice, if the data source is reliable, the error is very small, and the aim is local estimation, interpolation—especially spline interpolation—is the better choice. Conversely, if measurements contain error and the aim is to explore a global pattern or make predictions, fitting, with least squares at its core, is necessary. A clear understanding of their differences and appropriate applications is a prerequisite for effective quantitative analysis.
