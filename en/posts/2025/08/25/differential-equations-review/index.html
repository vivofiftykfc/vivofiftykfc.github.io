A Review of Differential- and Difference-Equation Methods

Abstract

This article reviews two important methods for modelling dynamic processes: differential equations and difference equations. It first introduces the background of dynamic-process modelling and the choice of methods from a broad perspective, before examining the fundamental ideas and modelling grounds of each kind of equation. Applications include changes in populations, the diffusion of pollutants, and the sales of new products. Difference equations are suited to processes that change in distinct stages over discrete time, whereas differential equations describe continuous and smooth development. Particular attention is given to analytical solutions, numerical solutions, and qualitative analysis, especially the identification of equilibrium points and the judgement of stability. Their practical value for prediction and intervention is also emphasized. Finally, examples such as epidemic models show how increasingly complex models can incorporate impulses, time delays, and different dimensions, including both time and space, and how such models may be improved through repeated revision.

1. Overview of Dynamic-Process Modelling

1.1 The Concept

Many models discussed earlier, such as linear regression, analyse the internal composition of an object statically. They can clearly display its internal structure and relationships at a particular moment. The world, however, is in motion. What matters more often is how an object evolves through time or space. When the characteristics of the object under study are continually changing, dynamic-process modelling has its part to play.

1.2 Choosing a Modelling Method

Route One: Abundant Data but an Unclear Mechanism—the “Black-Box” Approach

Methods: data fitting and statistical regression.

We may not know how a machine works internally, yet possess many sets of input-output data. Our task is then to find a mathematical function that imitates the machine’s behaviour as closely as possible. Whether the function itself has a physical meaning is not our chief concern; it is enough if the imitation is convincing.

Common methods:

Polynomial fitting is the simplest and most direct tool, though pursuing apparent goodness of fit may lead to overfitting.

Other forms of fitting should be selected according to the “shape” of the data:

  • If the growth rate becomes progressively slower, like someone who is nearly full, try logarithmic fitting.

  • If the data grow ever faster, like a snowball rolling downhill, try exponential fitting.

  • If the data form a bell shape, high in the centre and low on both sides, try Gaussian fitting.

  • If the data fluctuate periodically, Fourier-series fitting is the natural choice.

Time-series analysis is needed when the data appear disordered but seem to possess a kind of inertia—today’s observation is related to yesterday’s. More advanced time-series models, such as ARIMA, can then be employed.

Statistical regression is also a form of fitting, but it more “honestly” acknowledges the presence of an error term e and provides a complete theoretical framework for testing the significance of both the model and its coefficients.

Route Two: A Well-Understood Mechanism but Insufficient Data—the “White-Box” Approach

Methods: differential or difference equations and random processes.

Although we may lack abundant data, we understand the rules governing the internal operation of the system. The task is to express its dynamic behaviour in equations derived from physical, chemical, or biological laws.

Models constructed in this way are generally more robust and are particularly suitable for long-term prediction, since they capture the essential laws of development rather than merely reproducing superficial patterns in the data.

2. Modelling with Difference Equations

2.1 Basic Concepts

Appropriate situation: use difference equations when change occurs step by step, in jumps, and is divided into distinct stages.

We do not care about what happens between individual frames. We care about the relationship between frame n and frame n+1, or perhaps several previous frames. For example, population may be counted by year, stock closing prices updated by day, and population size calculated by reproductive cycle.

2.2 Basis of a Difference-Equation Model

The central principle is to link what came before with what comes next: describe the present state by means of past states.

  • xn+1​=f(xn​): the simplest model, in which “tomorrow” depends only on “today.”

  • xn+2​=f(xn​,xn+1​): a slightly more complicated model, in which “the day after tomorrow” depends not only on “tomorrow” but also on “today,” thus allowing for “inertia.”

2.3 Applications of Difference Equations

2.3.1 A Population Model: A Gradually Refined Line of Thought

  1. Model V1.0: The Utopian Model, or the Malthus Model

    • Idea: assume a “utopia” with unlimited resources, where population growth depends only on fixed birth and death rates.

    • Problem: this model is too naive. It predicts either exponential explosion or exponential extinction, neither of which can continue for long in reality.

  2. Model V2.0: The Real-World Model, or the Logistic Model

    • Idea: introduce resource constraints and competition. When the population becomes too large, food grows scarce and living space contracts, causing the birth rate to fall and the death rate to rise.

    • Conclusion: this is a much more advanced model. It predicts that the population will approach a stable upper limit imposed by the carrying capacity of the environment, which agrees closely with natural processes. Yet it also reveals more complicated behaviour: depending on the parameters, a population may settle at the limit, fluctuate periodically around it, or even enter an unpredictable state of chaos.

  3. Model V3.0: Allowing for Generational Differences

    • Idea: introduce a time lag. Many organisms cannot reproduce immediately after birth and require a period of growth. Today’s birth rate may therefore be determined by the number of adults k cycles earlier.

  4. Models V4.0 and V5.0: Incorporating More Realistic Factors

    • Sex: only females can give birth, so introducing the female proportion s makes the model more precise.

    • Age structure, or the Leslie model: this is a major advance. The population is no longer treated as a single number but as a vector, each element of which represents the population in one age group. A state-transition matrix M can then describe precisely how the age structure of the entire population changes from one generation to the next.

  5. Model V6.0: Allowing for Sudden Shocks, or Impulse Effects

    • Idea: the real world is full of unexpected events. Some occur regularly, such as fishing during a fixed season each year, while others are triggered by the state of the system, such as large-scale pesticide spraying once the locust population exceeds a threshold.

    • Modelling: by introducing a switching function, T(n) or S(x), we can simulate such “impulsive” interventions in the equation. This is essential when modelling matters such as epidemic control and economic regulation.

2.4 Stability of Difference Equations

  • Central question: as time approaches infinity, where will the system eventually go? Will it settle at one point, grow without limit, or oscillate back and forth?

  • Equilibrium point: the state at which the system remains motionless, namely x* = f(x*). It is a possible “destination” of the system.

  • Judging stability:

    • In plain language: if we give the system a gentle push near its equilibrium, will it roll back by itself, which means it is stable, or move ever farther away, which means it is unstable?

    • Mathematical tool: the effect of this “push” is described by the derivative |f'(x*)|.

      • |f'(x*)| < 1: the disturbance is gradually “absorbed,” and the system converges back to the equilibrium, so it is stable.

      • |f'(x*)| > 1: the disturbance is “amplified,” and the system moves away from the equilibrium, so it is unstable.

3. Modelling with Differential Equations

3.1 Overview

Appropriate situation: use differential equations when change is smooth and continuous.

3.2 Principal Tasks

  1. Finding an analytical solution: this resembles obtaining a universal formula from which the state at any time can be calculated. Such solutions are rare, however, and are available only for a small number of simple equations.

  2. Qualitative analysis: this is the next-best alternative. Although we have no universal formula, we can analyse the equation itself, draw a “sketch” of its solutions—a phase portrait—and understand the system’s long-term behaviour, equilibrium points, possible periodicity, and so forth. This can offer more insight than a particular numerical solution.

  3. Finding a numerical solution: this is the most practical and powerful method. Given initial conditions and parameters, a computer “iterates” step by step to obtain an approximate solution at any time. We may not know the exact equation of a rocket’s trajectory, for example, but we can calculate its positions at 1 second, 1.01 seconds, 1.02 seconds, and so on. Joining these points gives the trajectory.

3.4 Basis of a Differential-Equation Model

  • The central task is to identify the relationship between the rate of change and the state.

  • Classic example: dx/dt = kx. In ordinary words, “the speed at which something x changes is proportional to its own size.” This relationship can describe unrestricted population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest, and many other phenomena.

3.5 Applications of Differential Equations

  1. A Model of New-Product Sales, or the Logistic Model:

    • Mechanism: sales growth is driven by two factors. The first is word-of-mouth promotion by existing buyers, which is proportional to the quantity already sold, x. The second is market demand among potential buyers, which is proportional to the unsold quantity, K-x. Multiplying them gives the classic Logistic model.

  2. An Age-Structured Population Model:

    • This is the continuous counterpart of the Leslie model. Instead of updating once a year, we examine at every moment how many people move from “youth” to “middle age” and from “middle age” to “old age.”

  3. The Influence of Exceptional Factors: like difference equations, differential equations can incorporate impulses, delays, and other complex factors to bring the model closer to reality.

  4. Mathematical-Physics Equations, or Partial Differential Equations, PDEs:

    • When the object under study varies not only with time but also with spatial position, partial differential equations are required.

    • In plain language: we care not only about how the total amount of a pollutant in a pond changes over time, but also about how its concentration changes over time at different positions, (x, y, z), in that pond. This requires partial derivatives.

3.6 Qualitative Analysis of Differential Equations

  • Central idea: as with difference equations, we find equilibrium points and judge their stability.

  • Method: for a two-dimensional system, stability is judged by analysing the eigenvalues of the linear approximation’s Jacobian matrix at an equilibrium point. The real part of an eigenvalue determines whether the system converges or diverges in the corresponding direction. The equilibrium is stable when the real parts of all eigenvalues are negative.

3.7 Continuing Improvement and Deeper Discussion: An Epidemic Model

This is an excellent example of modelling as a process of repeated iteration towards reality.

  1. Begin with the classic SIR model: it provides the simplest “skeleton.”

  2. Add population movement: turn it into SIRS by allowing recovered individuals to lose immunity again.

  3. Add real-world constraints: incorporate births and deaths so that the total population is no longer fixed.

  4. Add temporal features: include an incubation period, as in the SEIR model, and impulse effects during holidays.

  5. Add social interventions: include vaccination and quarantine.

  6. Add biological characteristics: include viral mutation and vertical transmission.

  7. Add randomness: use stochastic differential equations to describe random disturbances in such parameters as the infection rate due to environmental noise.

By applying one “patch” after another, we can turn a rough toy model into a powerful tool capable of informing public-health decisions.
