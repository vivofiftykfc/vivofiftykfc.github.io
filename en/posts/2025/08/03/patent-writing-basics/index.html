Patent Writing: First Notes

Overview

We had previously written an initial report. Later, the teacher came back to us and, in rather polite terms, explained that it was a pile of rubbish: it read like a mid-term inspection report and did not satisfy the requirements. He then guided me through part of the proper form by hand—although by now I have forgotten a fair amount—and sent us several granted patents from earlier cohorts to use as models. I spent the following week alternating between fooling around and working, eventually producing a second preliminary version of roughly ten thousand Chinese characters and several diagrams in Word and Visio. These are the lessons I took from that process.

Before anything else, one point must be stated clearly. This article merely explains the reasoning with which I wrote my own draft and my present understanding of patent writing.

Do not rely on these notes alone. Before writing, compare your work against the documents of patents that have actually been granted and examine how they organize their arguments. What I provide here is only one approach that seems reasonable to me.

First, a diagram borrowed from a creator on Bilibili:

I will not discuss the general categories of patents—those for inventions, utility models, and designs—in detail. For students in information-related engineering, invention patents are normally the relevant kind. Utility models are of limited use, while designs are even further from our work.

The following three requirements were copied from another source.

  1. Utility or industrial applicability:

    An invention must be capable of being made or used in industry and must produce a positive effect or solve a particular technical problem. It cannot remain a purely theoretical or abstract concept, still less a fantasy contrary to the laws of nature; it must have practical value. More precisely, a person skilled in the relevant field should be able to implement the technical scheme from the disclosure in the specification, and the invention should produce the anticipated beneficial effect in practice. A perpetual-motion machine, for example, cannot be realized because it violates conservation of energy and therefore lacks utility.

  2. Novelty:

    Before the application date, or priority date, the invention must not form part of the prior art. “Prior art” means technology made publicly known anywhere in the world before that date. Disclosure may occur through publications such as patent documents, academic papers, books, and product manuals; through public sale, display, or use; or through such other means as oral reports, public demonstrations, and online publication. The central question is whether the claimed technical scheme is completely identical to a scheme recorded in any single item of prior art, or constitutes a direct and equivalent substitution. Any such prior disclosure destroys novelty.

  3. Inventive step or non-obviousness:

    Compared with the prior art existing before the application date, an invention must possess prominent substantive features and represent notable progress. A utility model must at least possess substantive features and progress. The creation cannot merely combine existing techniques or make a minor alteration; it must go beyond what an ordinarily skilled person in the field could readily derive through routine reasoning or experimentation. The central question is whether, to a person possessing ordinary knowledge and technical ability in that field, the invention is non-obvious in view of the prior art as a whole, which often requires several documents to be considered together. It should also normally produce a beneficial technical effect.

The Specification

I begin with the specification because my own approach is to write the detailed specification first. It generally contains the title, technical field, background, summary of the invention, explanation of the drawings, and detailed embodiments. The summary of the invention, drawings, and embodiments should occupy most of the document.

Title

The title is simple. A patent normally begins with an idea; take the name of that idea, revise its wording, and something like “A Method for XXX Based on XXX” can serve as the title.

Technical Field

This section also requires little anxiety. State the field to which the patent belongs. A common template is: “The present invention relates to the field of XX and, more particularly, to an XX method.”

Background

The background resembles the review of domestic and international research in a paper. It explains the fundamental reason for making the invention and should describe the defects of existing technology in detail. A typical sequence begins with a broad setting: as some social problem becomes increasingly serious, the demand for something grows; this creates a more specific demand; existing products fail to satisfy it; and a new method, apparatus, or system is therefore required.

Summary of the Invention

This is one of the principal sections.

It can broadly be divided into three parts, although that does not mean three further subheadings must actually be inserted.

Purpose: explain in two sentences which defects and problems in the background the invention is intended to solve.

Technical scheme: describe the actual operating procedure of the invention. In our patent, this meant explaining the relations among its modules and its basic processing flow. Much of my phrasing came from a senior student’s patent: first state the process in broad terms, then place the details beneath expressions such as “Optionally, wherein…”

As far as I can tell, this section should also remain substantially consistent with the claims. That deserves attention.

Beneficial effects: state what the invention achieves. There is plenty of room for generous phrasing here, so I will not dwell upon it.

Explanation of the Drawings

Preparing the drawings remains an important part of the process. For my draft, the teacher pointed out that we lacked a TOC—here meaning an overall diagram rather than a table of contents. Such a figure presents the processing flow or basic structure of the principal functional units.

A flow mentioned in the text needs a flowchart as an accompanying figure. Hardware requires a schematic or structural diagram. These are the most basic means of explaining one’s own patent.

The figures should preferably be vector graphics. Every stage of a process should carry a label such as S1 so that it can be referred to conveniently in the text. We followed the teacher’s recommendation: draw the figures in Visio, convert them to .svg, and then submit them.

The figures themselves are appended at the end. In the written explanation, describe each through a sentence such as “Figure X is an XX diagram of an embodiment of the present invention.”

Detailed Embodiments

My understanding is that this section provides an example based on the technical scheme and includes every technical point and feature needed to implement it. In practice, I further expanded the process given in the summary and explained each stage with reference to the drawings.

Abstract of the Specification

The teacher did not trust us to write the abstract and sent us his own version. It should include:

  • a sentence stating that the invention discloses an XX method and belongs to a particular field;
  • a description of what the method includes; and
  • its advantages and the problem it solves.

One representative figure should also be selected from the drawings as the abstract figure.

Claims

The claims define the scope of protection. They are especially important when a company is using a patent to protect its property.

Claims are divided into independent claims and dependent claims.

If the patent is intended to earn money, its scope should be made as broad as reasonably possible so as to prevent infringement. Think of Russian nesting dolls: the independent claim covers the largest area, thereby protecting the intellectual property effectively, and the dependent claims narrow it step by step. The independent claim might cover a fork; dependent claim 1 a wooden fork; dependent claim 2 a sandalwood fork; and dependent claim 3 a sandalwood fork made in China. If the patent office rejects the first application, move the substance of dependent claim 1 into the independent claim. If it is rejected again, move dependent claim 2 into it, and so forth. The broader the defensible scope, the better.

If the aim is instead to satisfy a professional-title requirement or strengthen a résumé, and the priority is simply to obtain a grant, the independent claim can begin with a detailed description. This sacrifices breadth but may increase the probability of allowance.

More generally, a patent must demonstrate novelty, inventive step, and utility. These three qualities should repeatedly be visible in the claims.

That is roughly all I have for now.
