2025-11-03 — A Brief Note

I have spent almost all of the past few days lying in my dormitory. I have done nothing, like a piece of plastic waste abandoned by time and left to drift with the waves. Even so, I still want to write something—probably out of that instinct to atone in words for the guilt of having wasted my time.

There was, in fact, nothing noble about my original reason for building this blog. To put it plainly, it was merely a by-product of my anxiety and incompetence, a mental toy designed to make up for the fact that I could accomplish nothing. The dignified and elegant phrases in the introduction were only meant to give this walking corpse a respectable shell, so that I could prove to an imaginary reader that I still appeared to be a person with thoughts, warmth, and some recognisable shape.

Yet beneath this false shell, I unexpectedly recovered some of the thinking I had lost. Those technical notes, those pieces of self-examination, and those wild thoughts disguised as professional writing somehow allowed me, in an almost absurd manner, to make peace with myself for a while.

That peace, however, did not last. Later, everything changed its flavour again. My notes became increasingly formalistic. I would spend an entire day writing one set of notes on Principles of Communication—carefully, or so I told myself, while knowing perfectly well that this was merely procrastination in a more sophisticated form. I did learn something, but the painfully slow pace kept reminding me that, instead of dawdling like this, I might as well kill myself. Later still, I simply pasted in a few screenshots and muddled through. Even copying someone else’s homework would have made me think more.

This reminds me of a conversation with my cousin. I told him that, on the first reading of a book, one should construct a complete framework of knowledge: see what each chapter is doing, grasp the whole picture, and then use exercises to fill the gaps. Through repeated correction, one eventually forms a system of one’s own, after which one may consult other people’s systems to make up for what is missing. Looking back now, I am saddened to discover that I already understood these principles three years ago, yet I sold them to a recycling station, together with my piles of old paper, for a few dozen yuan.

What, then, have I been doing for the past three years? Can I continue scraping by? Or is suicide the best option now?

So I have begun to suspect that all my “note-taking” and “blog-writing” may simply be another way of wasting my life. Notes ought to be useful, but if the act of writing slows down one’s growth, they become decoration. Perhaps I should return to the method I used in high school: take no notes, but build the skeleton of knowledge in my mind, until the system becomes part of the way I think.

Let this piece, then, serve as a declaration that I am going to set things right.

My recent condition, politely described, has been “relaxed”; more honestly, it has been “rotten.” My schedule has collapsed completely. I live on food delivery, take no exercise, and do no thinking. My head feels stuffed with fog, and I cannot be bothered to wake it up. If the person I was three years ago could see me now, he would probably go back and repeat his final year of high school.

The problem is that I know perfectly well what I ought to do. Why, then, can I not move? Perhaps the answer is simple: my life is too comfortable.

Here, in the manner of an examination essay, let me first scatter a generous handful of famous quotations across the page.

As Mencius said, when Heaven is about to place a great responsibility upon a person, it first causes suffering in his mind, exhaustion in his muscles and bones, hunger in his body, and poverty in his circumstances; it frustrates whatever he attempts, thereby stirring his heart, strengthening his endurance, and increasing what he was previously unable to do. I am not trying to claim that suffering creates talent, nor am I repeating the slogan that failure is the mother of success. I merely want to make one point: when survival itself becomes difficult, people can actively mobilise their own initiative and accomplish remarkable things. Song Lian likewise wrote that the students of the Imperial Academy received daily provisions from the government and seasonal clothes from their parents; they suffered neither cold nor hunger, studied the classics in great halls without the hardship of travelling, had instructors and learned scholars who answered every question, and could find every necessary book collected before them, without having to copy texts by hand or borrow them from others as he had done. To put it bluntly, a rich or powerful family’s child who enjoys an entirely smooth life, faces no struggle at home, and feels no pressure to survive can never work as hard as Song Lian did—because there is no pressure of survival.

Without a genuine crisis, there is no necessity for change. People usually relearn how to run only when hunger makes them panic and fear makes them tremble. Ideals? Ambitions? Those are luxuries. For a low-energy, lonely, desireless wreck like Zha Ze, ideals stopped working long ago. The only thing capable of driving me is naked pressure to survive.

I wonder whether I should manufacture a sense of hunger for myself. I might, for example, link my spending to my schedule and make disorder carry a price; or I might simply leave the familiar environment and allow comfort to collapse altogether.

This may be the only way out. At the very least, it offers the possibility of waking up.

As for human relationships, the movement from the freshness of first acquaintance to the dullness of familiarity with certain friends has unquestionably been a failure. This is not particularly surprising. I really am a boring person. There is no resonance, no passion, only awkwardness whenever I try to socialise.

The bonds between people probably amount to only a few kinds: shared interests, work, a common hometown, exchange, and the rare category of “one of us.” I, however, seem to belong to none of them.

Some people say that loneliness is an unavoidable path for a thinker. But I am no thinker. I am merely an ordinary person who is not even competent at social interaction. Perhaps that is what truly deserves reflection.

A few days ago, I went to see an EVA film. When the lights went out, something in me trembled for no apparent reason. That long-forgotten sense of the sublime in a work of art fell like cold water upon my numb brain and woke it up.

I once again found that lingering feeling which follows an excellent work. A great work is like thin air: it makes one suffocate, yet also makes one alert. It forces us to face the parts of ourselves that have long gone numb and, for a brief moment, to believe that life may still be illuminated by art. It reminds us that what is beautiful must eventually pass away, so that, when the experience is over, we realise there is now one fewer work in the world capable of striking directly at the soul. Perhaps this is what is meant by mono no aware: a tender mourning for the disappearance of beauty, and a quiet submission to the brevity of existence.

After returning, I found some vague belief burning again at the bottom of my heart—I could continue living by the light of such art. Even if I merely drag out this existence, it still counts as a way of living.

And so I wrote this piece as well.

Perhaps writing cannot make me better. At least, however, it allows me to hear my own heartbeat amid the ruins.

Sometimes I wonder whether “self-reflection” is itself a sweet form of self-deception.

A person falls into the void, yet still needs language to sustain the illusion that “I am still thinking.”

And it is precisely within this illusion that I continue to scrape by.

Late at night, the breeze in the dormitory gently moves the curtain. A delivery scooter passes downstairs, its light drawing an arc through the darkness.

I watch it leave and suddenly remember a sentence:

“In this world, the hardest thing is not to stay alive, but to live like a human being.”

I do not know whether I can still manage that. But at least at this moment, perhaps I do.

Original Version

I have spent the past few days playing dead in my dormitory and have accomplished almost nothing. Even so, I still want to write something. Perhaps this comes from a desire to compensate for the guilt of having done nothing at all.

First comes a reflection on this blog. To put it bluntly, I created the thing in the first place as worthless filler meant to relieve the anxiety of achieving nothing. It was a product for venting resentment, a substitute designed to conceal the emptiness inside me and my lack of human warmth. To put it even less politely, it was something I made to show off. The high-sounding claims in the introduction merely wrap a vaguely human skin around me, concealing the thoughts actually written here and fabricating, for imaginary readers who do not exist, a childish geek persona that would collapse under the slightest examination.

There was, however, one side effect: I discovered that this space really could contain some of the thoughts I once had but no longer possess—reflections, apparently technical posts written to fill the site, and some of my early notes.

Gradually, though, the whole thing changed. The clearest example was the notes. At first I took great pains over my Principles of Communication notes, but a single entry consumed an entire day. After all, I am now in an extremely dispirited state, and only a small part of each day can be used effectively. There was indeed some benefit: the subject’s framework was constructed in my mind. Yet the cost was an extreme loss of speed. Worse still, many later sections were brushed aside; screenshots and AI were thrown together, and the material was muddled through. Does that still mean anything?

This again reminds me of a conversation I once had about studying with my cousin, who was still in high school. First, speed through the entire book. A book is a complete structure of knowledge, is it not? Understand what it is talking about, see what each part is doing, and grasp what the particular procedures for solving problems are meant to accomplish. Then think it through and classify it according to the table of contents. If you have truly understood the book, you should be able to follow that table and explain what every section discusses, what kinds of questions it contains, and how they should be interpreted. This is the first step: forming the outline of your own prior network of knowledge. Next, solve problems and dig deeper. Carry your framework into those exercises, using a large data set to fill, improve, and correct it. Finally, once the framework has become mature—once practice has made it almost effortless—you can widen its scope. In high school, that meant recalling the textbook; at the present stage, it means comparing your framework with those constructed by different experts in the same field and then refreshing your own system.

Then I suddenly realised that I already knew this method thoroughly three years ago, before entering university. I could not help wondering: what exactly have I been doing for the past three years? Can I still survive?

Looking at my present note-taking practice, I admit that notes can be useful. Yet writing while learning slows progress far too much, whereas using AI to write them puts the cart before the horse. What reason, then, do I have to preserve this habit? Perhaps the proper course is to study as I did in high school: take no notes, but write the relevant framework directly into my mind.

Let this passage, this article of more than a thousand Chinese characters, serve as the general programme for setting things right.

Next comes a reflection on my current condition. My days and nights are reversed, my schedule is chaotic, I survive on food delivery and refuse to exercise, and brain fog has left me without motivation. Honestly, if judged by the standards I held three years ago, I am now a piece of rubbish. The only thing I can do is occasionally produce a little reflection and a few thoughts—then continue sinking.

Do I really not know what should be done? Why, then, am I unable to do it? I do not want to discuss meaning or meaninglessness here, so for the moment I will borrow a pragmatic analysis: the pressure of survival imposed by the outside world is simply too weak. Refusing to change will not make me unable to survive, at least not in the immediate future. Change, meanwhile, carries even its small cost. The desire to change therefore never appears, and I willingly allow myself to decay.

Let us now analyse human beings simply as organisms, without invoking metaphysical notions of romance, Mr Democracy, or Mr Science. If we are organisms, we have only two needs: survival and reproduction.

Set reproduction aside for now. Twenty years of sexual repression have already worn that source of motivation flat. Let us discuss the motives and fears connected with survival instead.

Here, as in the days of examination-oriented education, let me begin by scattering a grand pile of famous quotations across the page. As Mencius said, when Heaven is about to place a great responsibility upon a person, it first causes suffering in his mind, exhaustion in his muscles and bones, hunger in his body, and poverty in his circumstances; it frustrates whatever he attempts, thereby stirring his heart, strengthening his endurance, and increasing what he was previously unable to do. I am not claiming that suffering creates talent, nor am I saying that failure is the mother of success. I merely wish to make one point: when people are close to being unable to survive, they can actively mobilise their initiative and accomplish remarkable things. Song Lian similarly wrote that the students of the Imperial Academy received daily provisions from the government and seasonal clothing from their parents; they suffered neither cold nor hunger, studied the classics in great halls without the hardship of travel, had instructors and learned scholars who answered every question, and could find every necessary book collected before them, without having to copy texts by hand or borrow them from others as he had done. To put it bluntly, in such an environment, the child of a rich or powerful family who enjoys an entirely smooth life, faces no struggle at home, and feels no pressure to survive can never study as hard as Song Lian did—because that child faces no pressure of survival. Everyone speaks about willpower and stresses the function of ideals. But plainly speaking, ideals have no effect whatsoever upon a dispirited, low-energy person like me, who needs to feed only himself, is empty to the point of death every day, and has no company and no one to love him. The simplest and most direct source of motivation is pressure to survive.

I have been considering several possible ways to turn survival pressure into the motive force of action. The first is to link spending with the regularity of my daily schedule and the amount of work I complete. Of course, this means placing pressure upon myself, which I have tried before, and there remains a risk that I will simply give up. The second is to leave my present environment, remove the existing restraints, and physically escape the boundaries of comfort.

Some corresponding action has indeed begun. Let us see how it works out. I shall leave this part here and return to it later.

As for my relationships during this period, they seem to have undergone an examination of their own. The process moved from the freshness of first acquaintance to the familiar stage, in which each person’s actual situation had become roughly known. The result was unquestionably a failure: I could feel that I was not interested. This was hardly unexpected. On the basis of my present observations, the bonds between people amount to only a few kinds: companions connected by shared interests; colleagues and classmates connected by work or study; parties connected by exchange; people from the same place connected by geography; and close friends connected by inner monologues, embarrassing histories, and secrets—in other words, one’s own people.

Regrettably, where colleagues and companions with shared interests depend more heavily upon personal ability, even I find myself rather boring. It seems entirely normal that, once the novelty fades, we become ordinary friends. As for ambiguous relationships driven by hormones, there is truly no point in imagining them, nor is there any need to exhaust my mind and body over them. That would only deepen my internal friction. I would be better advised to persuade myself to improve the hardware first. And the possibility of finding someone attractive to sleep with while going out to have fun? That, too, is a privilege reserved for good-looking men and women. An ordinary person’s attention should be directed towards becoming such a person, rather than fantasising that so-called depth or conversational skill can compensate for present deficiencies in the hardware. There is therefore no reason to spend my already limited energy upon it.

At the same time, however, I can see that I am unable to make people feel at ease in conversation and know little about social skills. This is a weakness in performance, and I think it deserves serious consideration.

Finally, something about works of art. An EVA film opened in cinemas a couple of days ago, so I bought a ticket. It gave me a great deal to think about. First came the familiar response after an excellent work; the same thing happened after I finished Fate, and I could not calm down for a long time. I know that excellent works are more often encountered than sought, and that every one you finish leaves one fewer still to experience. This begins to lead towards the Japanese aesthetics of mono no aware, which I will not discuss further here. More importantly, the film once again awakened my memories of cinema, animation, and music. Yes—I really can live reasonably well by relying upon these forms of modern art.
