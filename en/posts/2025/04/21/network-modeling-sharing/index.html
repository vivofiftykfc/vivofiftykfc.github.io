Notes from a Mathematical Modeling Workshop

An Overview of Mathematical Modeling

Mathematical modeling is, in my view, a category of competition well worth entering. It teaches the basic process and techniques of academic writing, traditional methods of modeling and analysis—which matter all the more in an age dominated by machine learning—and the use of scientific reasoning to examine real problems. That last part can make one’s thinking more rational. From a more practical perspective, the competition may also contribute to comprehensive assessment, postgraduate recommendation, and interview performance. Our university has developed a fairly mature sequence: three rounds of campus competition introduce the basic models, after which students alternate between the Mathematical Contest in Modeling and China’s national contest. I would recommend it to first-year BIT students. Programming, advanced mathematics, and mathematical analysis are studied in the first semester, and the contest arrives in the second. The timing is rather good.

This article organizes the subject according to my own way of thinking. I am an engineering student, so the account may lean toward familiar engineering procedures. Deriving models from differential equations and similar work is left aside.

I also omit machine-learning methods. Many are black boxes that cannot readily be explained, and such contests were not originally designed as machine-learning exercises. Some competitions simply ban those methods.

This post follows a 2025 mathematical-modeling workshop held by the Network Association, and much of it is copied directly. My thanks go to the speaker, Guo Wentao.

Modeling and Programming

The following broad categories can guide model selection. Beginners may as well start from nothing and learn while competing—“learn warfare through warfare,” as Chairman Mao put it.

My recommended route is to enter a campus contest, use CSDN, Zhihu, and Bilibili to find methods, verify them in SPSS, and ask GPT, DeepSeek, or whatever AI is currently popular to explain them. Understanding grows gradually through use.

The prerequisite, of course, is knowing which methods exist. For each major direction, understand the available models, their inputs and outputs, their central ideas, and the situations and characteristics for which they are suitable.

Optimization

Optimization seeks a maximum or minimum. More precisely, it consists of variables, constraints, and an objective function. Anyone familiar with the basic idea of a neural network will notice a resemblance to placing an objective function before the model and training toward it. I tell you which variables exist and what final quantity I want—the shortest path, for example—and you construct a model that produces it.

Points to note include:

  • Linear route-finding and similar problems may have a unique optimal solution.
  • Simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and particle-swarm optimization produce simulated results and may become trapped in local optima. Run them several times.
  • Differential and difference equations often appear in biological or population problems, with competition and other conditions added over repeated simulations. They are common in Problem A of the MCM.
  • The speaker did not know much graph theory, and neither do I. It is rather theoretical.

Classification

Classification assigns existing objects to classes and may involve large amounts of data. It seldom forms an entire problem by itself, but often appears inside an evaluation task.

K-means, SVM, and XGBoost are particularly important.

Evaluation

Evaluation compares the quality of alternatives through an appropriate judgment method. Problem B in the first round of the 2025 campus contest belonged to this category. Entropy weighting combined with TOPSIS worked well, while PCA first removed several variables and also made it possible to draw attractive heat maps. If nothing else comes to mind, a purely subjective method such as AHP can at least produce a complete answer.

The danger is that these highly conventional procedures produce homogeneous papers. The final choice still deserves careful thought.

Forecasting

Forecasting estimates future changes.

  • Input: variables arranged over time
  • Output: future values of variables arranged over time
  • Principal methods: linear regression and ARIMA\GM
  • Several variables: LSTM may be attempted, or a non-temporal model may make judgments from specified factors

Evaluation is sometimes optional. When several models are available, compare them; when an indicator looks good—for example, an excellent fit—mention it. When the result is poor, perhaps do not. But let me repeat: always verify feasibility before investing effort in a method.

The Paper

Document Structure

  1. Abstract
  2. Contents
  3. Introduction to the problem
    • Background
    • Restatement
    • Analysis
  4. List of symbols
  5. Basic assumptions
  6. Model construction and solution
    • Parts one, two, and three
  7. Strengths and weaknesses, evaluation, and sensitivity analysis

Main Body

Introduce the method + present formulas + use figures + give results.

  • Explain why it is used: identify the category of model and the purpose it serves.
  • Explain how it is used: provide the formulas and, where useful, a process diagram.
  • Explain the result: show the result graph and analyse the error.

Points to Note

  • Pay attention to the position of each task. The first may be handled more lightly; the third usually demands much more work.
  • Always verify feasibility and avoid wasted effort.
  • Polish every graph. In practice, many teams use similar models, so attractive figures matter enormously.
  • The abstract is also important. The modeler should revise it after all other work is complete.
  • Remember to add the list of symbols.
  • Whatever the detail or completeness of the modeling, address every requested task and figure and fill every part of the template.
  • The writer should begin on the first day, working on background research and similar material.
  • Figures need patient refinement. A slide from the workshop appears below.

  • The modeler and writer must communicate properly.
  • The modeler should assess strengths and weaknesses and design a sensitivity analysis. According to the workshop, SHAP may be useful for this? I have never used it anyway.
