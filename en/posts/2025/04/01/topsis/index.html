TOPSIS: The Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to an Ideal Solution

TOPSIS: Distance from Ideal and Worst Solutions

I. Modeling

A TOPSIS model can be constructed in roughly three stages:

  1. Convert the original matrix into a benefit-oriented matrix.
  2. Standardize the converted matrix.
  3. Calculate the scores and normalize them.

To my mind, this procedure has much in common with the entropy weight method. The two should, in fact, be capable of being used together.

Converting the matrix into benefit-type indicators

Four kinds of indicators frequently occur in practice:

Type of indicator Principal feature Examples
Benefit-type The larger the value, the better Examination scores, GDP growth, corporate profit
Cost-type The smaller the value, the better Expense, defect rate, pollution
Intermediate-type The nearer the value is to a specified point, the better pH in water-quality assessment
Interval-type A value within a specified interval is best Body temperature, plant nutrients in water

TOPSIS requires all indicators to point in the same favourable direction. In other words, every indicator must be converted into a benefit-type indicator. Cost-type, intermediate-type, and interval-type indicators must therefore be transformed.

Converting a cost-type indicator into a benefit-type indicator:
$$
x_{new}= \frac{\max - x_i}{\max - \min}
$$
If all elements are positive, the following expression may also be used:

$$
x_{new}=\frac{1}{x_i}
$$

Converting an intermediate-type indicator: Such a value should be neither too large nor too small; a particular value is best, as with pH in water-quality assessment. Suppose that ${x_i}$ is a sequence of intermediate-type indicators and that the optimal value is $x_{best}$. The transformation is:
$$
M = \max{|x_i - x_{best}|}
$$
$$
\bar{x}i = 1 - \frac{|x_i - x{best}|}{M}
$$
Converting an interval-type indicator: A value within a particular interval is best. Human body temperature, for instance, is generally preferable when it lies between 36°C and 37°C. Suppose that ${x_i}$ is a sequence of interval-type indicators and that the optimal interval is $[a,b]$. The transformation is:
$$
M = \max{a - \min{x_i}, \max{x_i} - b},\ \tilde{x}_i =
\begin{cases}
1 - \frac{a - x_i}{M}, & x_i < a \
1, & a \leq x_i \leq b \
1 - \frac{x_i - b}{M}, & x_i > b
\end{cases}
$$
This is essentially the same transformation described for the entropy weight method. For details, see the entropy weight method.

Standardizing the matrix

Standardization removes the influence of different units and dimensions.

Suppose n objects are to be evaluated through m indicators. After all indicators have been converted, the matrix is:

$$
X =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1m} \
x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2m} \
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{nm}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Let the standardized matrix be Z. Each of its elements is:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}^2}}
$$
Thus:

$$
Z =
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{11} & z_{12} & \cdots & z_{1m} \
z_{21} & z_{22} & \cdots & z_{2m} \
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
z_{n1} & z_{n2} & \cdots & z_{nm}
\end{bmatrix}
$$
There is more than one method of standardization, but every method seeks to remove dimensional effects. The chapter on entropy weighting, for example, gives the following expression for data containing negative values:

$$
z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \min{x_{1j}, x_{2j}, \ldots, x_{nj}}}{\max{x_{1j}, x_{2j}, \ldots, x_{nj}} - \min{x_{1j}, x_{2j}, \ldots, x_{nj}}}
$$

Calculating and normalizing the score

The ideal solution is:

$$
Z^+ = (\max{z_{11},z_{21},\cdots,z_{n1}}, \max{z_{12},z_{22},\cdots,z_{n2}}, \cdots, \max{z_{1m},z_{2m},\cdots,z_{nm}})
$$
The worst solution is:
$$
Z^- = (\min{z_{11},z_{21},\cdots,z_{n1}}, \min{z_{12},z_{22},\cdots,z_{n2}}, \cdots, \min{z_{1m},z_{2m},\cdots,z_{nm}})
$$
Define the distance between the $i(i = 1,2,…,n)$th object and the ideal solution as:
$$
D_i^+ = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(Z_j^+ - z_{ij})^2}
$$
Define the distance between the $i(i = 1,2,…,n)$th object and the worst solution as:

$$
D_i^- = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(Z_j^- - z_{ij})^2}
$$
The unnormalized score of the $i(i = 1,2,…,n)$th object is then:

$$
S_i = \frac{D_i^-}{D_i^+ + D_i^-}
$$
Clearly, $0 \leq S_i \leq 1$. As $S_i$ increases, $D_i^+$ decreases; the object is therefore closer to the ideal solution.

II. Extensions

Combining TOPSIS with weights

The procedure above assumes that all indicators have equal weights. In an actual evaluation, however, different indicators generally carry different weights. The distance formulas should therefore be revised, with ω representing a weight:
$$
D_i^+ = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_j(Z_j^+ - z_{ij})^2},\ D_i^- = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_j(Z_j^- - z_{ij})^2}
$$
The entropy weight method can be used directly here. Of course, having experts assign scores—more or less by informed guesswork—is also an option.

Where the formula comes from

The formula

$$
S_i = \frac{D_i^-}{D_i^+ + D_i^-}
$$
is essentially:

$$
\frac{x - \min}{\max - \min} \rightarrow \frac{x - \min}{(\max - x) + (x - \min)} \rightarrow \frac{x与最小值的距离}{x与最大值的距离 + x与最小值的距离}
$$
$D_i^+$ and $D_i^-$ are, in fact, Euclidean distances. The underlying idea has a great deal in common with that of the entropy weight method.
