Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis is an algorithm for dimensionality reduction. It forms mutually uncorrelated principal components from linear combinations of the original variables, retaining the main information while removing noise and accelerating data processing. PCA is often used to simplify data, support cluster analysis, and address multicollinearity in regression. Since the meaning of a principal component is often ambiguous, however, PCA is not commonly chosen as an evaluation model.

I. Introduction

  • Objective: replace a large number of original variables with a smaller number of new ones while preserving as much information as possible.
  • Applications: dimensionality reduction, noise removal, and faster data processing.
  • Suitable situations: the indicators are highly correlated and reasonably easy to explain, while interpretability is not a major requirement.
  • Limitations: nonlinear relationships are handled poorly, and details may be lost if the retained contribution is insufficient.

II. Central Idea

Suppose there are $n$ samples and $p$ indicators, forming an $ n \times p$ sample matrix:

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix}
x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \
x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \
\end{bmatrix}
= (x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_p)
$$
The aim is to find new variables $ z_1, z_2, \dots, z_m $, where $ m \leq p $, such that:

$$
\begin{cases}
z_1 = l_{11}x_1 + l_{12}x_2 + \cdots + l_{1p}x_p \
z_2 = l_{21}x_1 + l_{22}x_2 + \cdots + l_{2p}x_p \
\vdots \
z_m = l_{m1}x_1 + l_{m2}x_2 + \cdots + l_{mp}x_p \
\end{cases}
$$
The coefficients $ l_{ij} $ are determined according to the following principles:

  1. Different principal components are mutually uncorrelated.
  2. The first principal component has the greatest variance among all possible linear combinations.
  3. Subject to being uncorrelated with the first, the second principal component has the greatest possible variance.
  4. The process continues until $ m $ principal components have been selected.

III. Calculation

1. Standardize the data

  • Calculate the mean and standard deviation:
    $$
    \bar{x}j = \frac{1}{n} \sum{i=1}^{n} x_{ij},\quad S_j = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j)^2}{n-1}}
    $$

  • Standardize each observation:
    $$
    X_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j}{S_j}
    $$

  • The standardized matrix is:
    $$
    X = \begin{bmatrix}
    X_{11} & X_{12} & \cdots & X_{1p} \
    X_{21} & X_{22} & \cdots & X_{2p} \
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
    X_{n1} & X_{n2} & \cdots & X_{np} \
    \end{bmatrix}
    $$

2. Calculate the covariance matrix

  • Definition:
    $$
    R = \begin{bmatrix}
    r_{11} & r_{12} & \cdots & r_{1p} \
    r_{21} & r_{22} & \cdots & r_{2p} \
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
    r_{p1} & r_{p2} & \cdots & r_{pp} \
    \end{bmatrix},\quad r_{ij} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n} X_{ki} X_{kj}
    $$

3. Solve for eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  • Eigenvalues:
    $$
    \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_p \geq 0
    $$
    Here, $ R $ is a positive semidefinite matrix.

  • Corresponding eigenvectors:
    $$
    a_1 = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} \ a_{21} \ \vdots \ a_{p1} \end{bmatrix},\quad
    a_2 = \begin{bmatrix} a_{12} \ a_{22} \ \vdots \ a_{p2} \end{bmatrix},\quad \dots,\quad
    a_p = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1p} \ a_{2p} \ \vdots \ a_{pp} \end{bmatrix}
    $$

4. Calculate the contribution and cumulative contribution

  • Contribution:
    $$
    \text{贡献率} = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_k}
    $$

  • Cumulative contribution:
    $$
    \text{累计贡献率} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{i}\lambda_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_k}\quad (i=1,2,\dots,p)
    $$

5. Write the principal components

In general, the first ( m ) components whose cumulative contribution exceeds 80 percent are selected:

$$
F_i = a_{1i}X_1 + a_{2i}X_2 + \cdots + a_{pi}X_p,\quad (i=1,2,\dots,m)
$$

6. Interpret the components

The meaning of a component is judged from the loading of each indicator upon it. An indicator with a larger loading has a greater influence on that component.

IV. Extensions

  1. Cluster analysis: dimensionality reduction simplifies the independent variables and makes graphical presentation easier.
  2. Regression analysis: PCA may alleviate multicollinearity.
  3. Factor analysis: PCA is, in effect, a special case of factor analysis. Since factor analysis is generally easier to interpret, it is often the better choice when interpretation matters.
