Standard Score — Z-Score

Z-Score

1. Introduction

A z-score measures the position of a data point relative to the population mean. It can transform data with different units or distributions onto the same standard—namely, the standard normal distribution—thereby making comparison easier. A positive value indicates that the data point lies above the mean, whereas a negative value indicates that it lies below the mean.

2. Formula

$$
\frac{X-\bar{X} }{s}
$$

That is, the difference from the mean divided by the standard deviation. In MATLAB, the zscore() function can be used directly.

3. Applications

  • Outlier detection
    A z-score can be used to identify a possible outlier. A threshold, commonly 2 or 3, is selected; if the absolute z-score exceeds that threshold, the data point may be abnormal. For example, when |z| > 3, a value is usually regarded as being unusually far from the mean and therefore worthy of further inspection. The rule can be understood through the normal distribution: approximately 68% of observations fall within [-1, 1], about 95% within [-2, 2], and about 99.7% within [-3, 3].
  • Data standardisation
    In machine learning or data mining, z-score standardisation removes the influence of measurement units and therefore supports modelling.
  • Comparison across datasets
    Because a z-score removes the influence of the original mean and standard deviation, values drawn from different datasets can be compared directly.

4. Limitations

The usefulness of a z-score depends to some extent on the distribution of the data. It generally works better when the distribution is closer to normal.

Z-score standardisation removes the original practical meaning of a value and therefore reduces interpretability. The result can be used for comparison, but the original value must be restored if its real-world meaning needs to be explained.
