Your Lie in April

Your Lie in April: A Programme by the Light and Shadow Group

—The April when I met you is coming. The April without you is coming.

Let me once again thank my partner for this programme. She recommended the anime to me, and she also worked with me to complete both the script and the broadcast. In fact, it would be more accurate to say that, as a senior member, she guided a newcomer like me—boo-hoo. Her contribution to the programme was far greater than mine.

Main Programme

Hello, everyone. Welcome to today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow on the Voice of BIT. I am your host, Zhang Hengwei, and I am your host, Wang Yiru. (Introduction.) At a time when the animation market is crowded with adaptations of light novels and fast-food series appear one after another, there is an anime that was released eleven years ago but still ranks second in an online Japanese poll of the most moving animated works. It has also become, for countless viewers, a masterpiece that every newcomer to anime ought to watch. More than ten years after its release, many people still commemorate it in their own ways every April. || If you are interested in stories of pure love and curious about a classic anime with such an enduring reputation, Your Lie in April may be exactly the work you have been expecting. In today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow, let us enter that April of falling cherry blossoms and listen to the symphony of music and love within Your Lie in April.

This story of music and love begins with an unexpected encounter. The protagonist, Kousei Arima, has studied the piano since childhood under his mother’s strict instruction and has repeatedly won first prize in competitions large and small. An unquestionably gifted young pianist, Kousei is, in almost everyone’s eyes, closely associated with the title of a rising star in the world of piano. Yet an unforeseen change destroys those hopes and breaks apart the musical dream he has always pursued. His mother, severe in her teaching and seldom given to smiles, dies suddenly after suffering from cancer. Faced with this disaster and overwhelmed by grief, Kousei becomes unable to hear the sound of his own piano, and his life loses all light. Then another unexpected encounter completely transforms that dim existence. While waiting for a friend, Kousei happens to meet Kaori Miyazono beneath a cherry tree, playing a melodica. He is drawn towards Kaori, a free and uninhibited violinist, and through a series of accidents the two become friends. Beneath Kaori’s bright and cheerful appearance, however, lies a shadow unknown to others. As an accomplished violinist, she began learning the instrument as a child because she wished to perform with the gifted Kousei Arima. To fulfil that childhood promise, Kaori creates their meeting through the lie that she “likes their mutual friend,” concealing the cruel fact that she is terminally ill. Yet this unexpected encounter not only restores music to Kousei’s world, in which only monotonous black and white keys remained, but also allows Kaori’s meteor-bright life to release its fiercest light during its final countdown. In the time left to her, Kaori uses her music and love to teach Kousei that music is not a chain, but a language through which he can speak to the world. She also teaches him how to face loss. Finally, in the spring when Kaori Miyazono departs, he performs the eternal composition that belongs to them both.

Dramatic Excerpt II

Source: Your Lie in April — Excerpt 2 — Bilibili

Kaori: Are you still playing the piano?

Kousei: I don’t play any more.

I knew it. You are sinking into despair alone again.

Kousei: I can’t do it any more. Everyone important to me has left before my eyes. Music took the people who truly mattered away, until I was the only one left.

Kaori: But you still have me, don’t you? Arima, I am going to have an operation on 18 February. I will give everything I have and struggle until the final moment. It is your fault—all of it. The reason I am struggling so awkwardly and chasing the hope of staying alive is you. The time I spent with you made me unwilling to leave. Won’t you try once more? Aren’t we best at fighting with everything we have? Aren’t we performers who struggle as if our lives depend upon it?

Kousei: But I haven’t touched the piano for a week.

Kaori: You hadn’t touched it when you accompanied me either.

Kousei: I occasionally played while working part-time then. There is no way I can perform well now. It would take a miracle to play in this condition…

Kaori: You are looking down again.

(Kaori plays the violin.)

Kaori: You wanted a miracle? There it is.

Main Programme

As a classic of Japanese animation, Your Lie in April is not merely a story of youth. It is also an anime about music, a classic whose flesh and blood are musical. From Beethoven’s Moonlight Sonata in the first episode to Chopin’s Ballade No. 1 in G Minor in the last; from Saint-Saëns’s Introduction and Rondo Capriccioso, with which the two protagonists first perform together, to Kreisler’s Liebesleid, which they never manage to complete together, every piece of classical music corresponds precisely with the plot and becomes a bridge along which emotion can flow. As Kousei breaks free from his mother’s shadow amid the torrent of the Revolutionary Étude, and as Kaori interprets the final blaze of life through the wild freedom of Introduction and Rondo Capriccioso, the notes and the narrative have already become one, producing an almost fated resonance.

The film allows music and narrative to grow together, going far beyond the simple use of “music as background.” With notes as its bricks and tiles, it constructs a city that remains forever in April. As Kaori says, “Music is freedom!” The creators weave that freedom together with the solemnity of classical music, arranging a magnificent concert for the transience of youth. When, ten years later, we still hear news of this concert being performed again and again in the words of so many people, we know that the concert of that April has long since been engraved upon their hearts and entered eternity.

Zhu Guangqian once wrote that, although the contents of tragedy mostly appear frightening on the surface, tragedy is by no means limited to terror. Terror merely causes pain and finally leaves people gloomy and discouraged, whereas tragedy inspires and strengthens them. Your Lie in April is a fine work that carries this principle through. It takes a tragedy in April as its foundation, yet releases an astonishing vitality that urges people to grow and move forward.

The so-called “lie” of the plot is the most beautiful lie woven by a girl who, as her life enters its final countdown, wants to save the person she loves. With the lie that she “likes Watari,” Kaori Miyazono enters the life of Kousei, the former child prodigy, knocks upon the door of his heart, and helps him overcome his inner demon. Through this apparently light and conventional device, the author offers a delicate interpretation of the Japanese aesthetics of mono no aware. In The Tale of Genji, the evening face blooms in the morning and withers by evening; similarly, behind Kaori’s gentle “lie” lies endless sorrow. In the last part of her life, she chooses to build a bridge towards their meeting out of a lie, solely so that, before she disappears, she can use the final vitality within her to save the gifted performer she loves. Like a cherry blossom whose fall is already determined at the instant of its full flowering, this countdown narrative casts a reflected sorrow over every warm scene, producing a tragic aesthetic space with a profoundly Eastern quality.

Just as every cherry blossom that opens in April must return to dust, and just as every performance must eventually end, Kaori—who brings hope and vitality to Kousei—still falls before that April of cherry blossoms arrives. The ending reveals the existential nature of life as “being towards death”: every apparently eternal beauty is, in essence, merely a suspension granted by death. Such recognition of life’s meaninglessness ought to lead towards nihilism. In this work, however, the author completes the turn from nothingness to existence through the characters’ devotion to and love for music. In the last days of her life, Kaori continues her final struggle so that she might perform on the same stage as the person she loves. Even after both his mother and his beloved have left him, Arima continues to play Bach and Chopin for the world, still carrying hope for life. These moments of earnest living constitute defiance against an absurd world. Like Camus’s Sisyphus, who knows the boulder will roll down again but continues climbing, Kousei defies his fate. Likewise, in that final April, Kaori smiles and says, “Even if grief is impossible to restrain, even if we lie wounded at the bottom of the valley, we must not stop playing. Only in this way are we truly alive.” This is her defiance against her terminal illness. Only now do we suddenly understand: life conceals the essence of disappearance beneath the appearance of existence, while human beings, fully aware of the ending, still choose to live with passion. As Romain Rolland wrote, “There is only one heroism in the world: to see the world as it is and to love it.” Yes—the most magnificent heroism of life does not lie in avoiding the inevitability of disappearance, but in turning the final scene into a splendid movement in full bloom. Even if I am to disappear tomorrow, today I will still straighten my tie and go out to greet the final evening glow and the rising sun.

Today, ten years later, Japan’s cherry-blossom front still moves northwards at seventeen kilometres per day. Petals still fall at five centimetres per second, and a pink glow once again rises upon Tokyo Tower. When real visitors lift their heads towards that fleeting splendour on the branches and reach out to catch petals falling at five centimetres per second, perhaps they do not know that Chopin’s Ballade No. 1 in G Minor from ten years ago remains condensed within those blossoms. Perhaps they do not know that the cherry blossoms once contained the harmonics of the young girl’s violin. Yet perhaps, as people already understand, the greatest beauty of cherry blossoms lies in the rain of petals that fills the sky as they fall. This happens to echo the final lie Kaori leaves to the world: although the words written upon it are “farewell forever,” what we hear is “I will wait for you in the future.”

The April when I met you is coming. The April without you is coming.
