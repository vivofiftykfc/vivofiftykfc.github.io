Fall Detection Based on Human Keypoint Recognition

Fall Detection Based on Human Keypoint Recognition

When we first worked on fall detection, we used conventional object-detection algorithms such as YOLO. Discussion soon exposed a problem: a traditional detector finds it difficult to distinguish falling from running, jumping, sitting, or squatting. In other words, the difficulty lies in classification between closely related actions. Then came the rather abrupt thought that fall detection might instead be based on human keypoints, which would make it easier to separate a fall from other movements. The approach is already fairly mature, as the references below indicate. For the moment, this article records an initial investigation of the algorithms. A detailed account of our particular model can wait until the project is completed.

Existing Algorithms

Conventional spatiotemporal analysis

This approach tracks skeletal keypoints—the head, shoulders, knees, ankles, and so forth—in real time and determines whether a fall has occurred by analysing changes in their positions. It is intuitive and readily interpretable, which makes it suitable for real-time detection. Its weakness is that missing data may lead to errors, although there is room to improve its ability to withstand such disturbances.

  • Keypoint extraction:

    A human-keypoint model such as OpenPose or BlazePose obtains the coordinates of body keypoints from a video.

  • Dynamic-feature calculation:

    Height of the centre of mass: a sudden drop in the height of the head or torso may signal a fall.

    Joint angles: the angle between the shoulder–knee line and the ground may decrease sharply, for instance from 90° while standing to nearly 0° after a fall.

    Motion speed: the vertical acceleration of the head or torso may rise suddenly, as in the brief sensation of “weightlessness” during a fall.

  • Classification decision:

    Manually chosen thresholds: define a feature threshold—for example, classify the action as a fall when the centre of mass drops below the knees.

    Machine-learning models: use an SVM or random forest to classify combinations of features. Frankly, we might as well go straight to machine learning.

Deep-learning models of temporal sequences

These methods demand more computing power and more data.

  • LSTM: analyse temporal changes in a sequence of keypoints and capture the dynamic process before, during, and after a fall.
    • Patent CN112163564A, for example, divides the body into the head, torso, and legs, and uses an LSTM to retain motion trajectories and anticipate the risk of a fall.
  • 3D CNN: accept video clips as direct input and extract spatial features of posture together with temporal features of motion. It just about counts, though getting it built would be painful.
  • Graph convolutional networks (GCNs): regard the skeleton as a graph and analyse relations among joints, making them suitable for complex actions. I am still not especially familiar with this architecture; that can be learned when the time comes.

Target-skeleton extraction based on CLAHE and morphology

This is primarily a method of skeleton extraction. It does not require a deep-learning model and therefore consumes fewer computing resources, but its performance may be less stable in complicated backgrounds.

  • Image enhancement: use contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) to improve image contrast and emphasise the human outline.
  • Skeleton extraction:
    1. Separate the moving target through background subtraction and fill holes through morphological closing.
    2. Extract the body contour with an edge detector such as the Sobel operator and remove noise with median filtering.
    3. Apply a thinning algorithm to generate the body skeleton and identify approximate keypoints such as the head and the ends of the limbs.

Extensions

Occlusion:

Use spatiotemporal filtering, such as a sliding-window filter, to fill in keypoints lost through occlusion.

Real-time optimisation:

  • Apply model distillation to transfer knowledge from a large model to a smaller one.

  • Deploy the model on an edge device through tools such as OpenVINO™, thereby reducing reliance on the cloud.

Improving robustness—and yes, I still dislike this translation of robust every time I see it:

  • Fuse data from several sensors. One might, for example, put an accelerometer in a wristband, although that immediately drives up the cost.
