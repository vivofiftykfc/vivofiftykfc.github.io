Understanding Monitor Specifications and the Principles behind Them

Monitor Specifications and Their Underlying Principles

I recently bought a secondary monitor and decided to organise the relevant knowledge into an article that might make future purchases easier.

How a Colour Display Works

Prerequisite Knowledge

  • Liquid crystal is a synthetic organic material whose state lies between a solid and a liquid. Without an electric field, its molecules may be arranged irregularly and block light; under voltage, they become ordered and transmit light. The reverse arrangement also exists, in which voltage blocks rather than transmits light.
  • Polarisation is the selection of the vibration direction of a transverse wave. By controlling light with polarising filters and observing changes in intensity, one can verify both the transverse nature of light and the selectivity of its vibration direction. A high-school physics textbook gives the details.

Central Idea

Colours are produced by controlling and mixing RGB values.

Technical Approaches

LCD

Conventional LCD

This remains a mainstream, if relatively old, approach. At the bottom is a backlight emitting white light. Above it lies a liquid-crystal layer between two mutually perpendicular polarisers. The liquid crystal rotates the direction of polarisation and thereby controls how much light can pass, which controls brightness. A colour filter outside the liquid-crystal layer produces colour. A conventional backlight usually consists of an LED strip between a reflector and a diffusion plate. When the monitor displays black while switched on, the backlight is still active; moreover, even an unpowered liquid-crystal layer rotates part of the light. The result differs from the pure black of a powered-off screen and reduces contrast.

According to the default state of the liquid-crystal layer, panels can be divided into three types:

  • A VA panel is vertically aligned by default and twists under voltage to transmit light. It offers high contrast, average viewing angles, and a relatively slow response. Inferior panels may smear. It suits films and AAA games.
  • An IPS panel is horizontally aligned by default and rotates in the horizontal plane under voltage. It provides average contrast, broad viewing angles, and a moderate response. It is the balanced choice for most situations.
  • A TN panel twists horizontally by default and turns vertical under voltage to block light. Its contrast and viewing angles are poor, but its response is fast, making it suitable for Counter-Strike veterans and Valorant juniors.
QLED

QLED modifies an LCD by using a blue backlight and coating the substrate with quantum dots. A quantum dot is an extremely small compound that absorbs blue light and emits light at another wavelength—rather like an electron being excited and then returning to its ground state. This approach addresses the limited saturation of an ordinary LCD, but remains an LCD.

Mini-LED

Mini-LED divides the backlight into zones and switches off those behind black regions; everything else resembles an LCD. This supports advanced HDR, although poor tuning can produce smearing or halos.

LED

OLED

OLED stands for Organic Light-Emitting Diode. Conventional diodes work well, but placing three diodes in every pixel and integrating them into a large panel is difficult, which led to OLED technology. Electricity directly excites fluorescent materials to emit coloured light, and three emitters form one pixel. This can also be understood as pixel-level local dimming: it produces no halo, high saturation, a thin panel, fast response, a broad colour gamut, low power consumption without a backlight, and even flexible displays. Its disadvantages arise partly because the three fluorescent materials age at different rates, making burn-in, retained colour, and colour shifts more likely. Other limitations include low peak brightness, PWM dimming, and reduced effective resolution.

W-OLED

W-OLED replaces the three coloured fluorescent materials with white emitters and places an RGBW filter above them. The white subpixel compensates for brightness, but its white light also interferes with the red, green, and blue subpixels, taking us back towards the problem faced by LCDs.

QD-OLED

QD-OLED replaces the QLED backlight with blue fluorescent material and remains within the LED family. It addresses the problem of W-OLED, although external light may excite the quantum-dot material and contaminate the signal.

Micro-OLED

This uses microscopic light-emitting diodes and solves the problem of integrating three conventional diodes into every pixel of a large display. Massive-transfer technology is still immature, however, so a panel may cost hundreds of thousands.

Specifications

Screen Size

Screen size is the diagonal length, usually measured in inches. Ordinary mainstream monitors commonly measure 24, 27, or 32 inches. The portable monitors I have seen—not many—are often around 14 inches. A 24- or 27-inch display is the usual choice and resembles what one finds in an internet café. Anyone wanting more space can choose a larger or ultrawide panel; wealthy buyers may proceed as they please.

Resolution

The most familiar resolutions are probably 1080p (1920*1080), 2K (2560*1440, or 16:9), and 4K (3840*2160). One also encounters names such as 2.5K, commonly used for a 16:10 resolution, although some people call 2560*1440 2.5K. These are only common examples, and labels such as 2K and 2.5K are often used rather casually. The following table, sourced from HeyBox, gives others.

The terminology is so inconsistent that it should not be examined too literally. Gamers, monitor manufacturers, film studios, and television companies all follow different conventions. A few examples:

  • On common video platforms, standard definition means 480p, high definition 720p, ultra-high definition 1080p, Blu-ray 1080p at 60 frames per second, and 4K 2160p; the bit rates remain unknown.
  • According to an editor at a Shanghai television station, 1080p at 10 Mbit/s is HD, 2560*1440 is 2K, and 3840*2160 or 4096*2160 is 4K. Blu-ray refers specifically to an optical disc, a physical storage medium like magnetic tape.
  • According to a worker at a Shandong television station, 480p is standard, 720p is HD, 1080p is ultra-HD, and 4K is simply 4K. Bit rate is calculated from MXF.

For video, resolution alone is insufficient; bit rate is equally important. A 4K video at 5 Mbit/s may look less clear than a 1080p video at 10 Mbit/s. Likewise, a monitor should not be judged by resolution alone, since PPI also matters. Domestic streaming video often looks less clear than locally downloaded material because its bit rate is too low. Some serious film enthusiasts even use PotPlayer to adjust colour balance and decoding methods. I came across that practice while browsing forums.

Another issue is scaling. Displaying a 1080p photograph on a 2K screen can make it appear blurrier because the source lacks enough exact pixels and the 2K panel must approximate them, though the difference may not be very noticeable.

Some users point out that the advertised resolution of an OLED should be discounted by about twenty per cent because its pixels operate differently.

Basic Units

  • px, or pixel: the smallest display unit and the basic component of an image.
  • pt, or point: a unit for font size. On digital devices, conversion between pt and px usually depends on resolution or PPI, such as 72 or 96 PPI.
  • PPI, pixels per inch: a measure of pixel density. It is calculated as:

$$
PPI = \frac{\sqrt{(宽度像素数)^2 + (高度像素数)^2}}{屏幕对角线长度（英寸）}

  • DPI, dots per inch, describes the precision of printer output. DPI and PPI are sometimes confused, but strictly speaking DPI belongs mainly to printing, while PPI describes display devices.

Choosing a Panel

See the preceding discussion of light-emission principles. In general, choose IPS or OLED directly. VA is worth considering for AAA games and films, while TN serves high-end competitive FPS play.

Refresh Rate

This is effectively the frame rate: the higher it is, the smoother motion appears. There is little else to say. For someone like me who mainly types and plays visual novels, 60 Hz is enough. Gamers, especially FPS players, benefit from higher rates.

Brightness

Units

Monitor brightness is generally measured in nits, which describe luminous intensity per unit area. Elsewhere, one may also encounter:

  • Candela: one of the seven SI base units and a measure of luminous intensity in a specified direction. It can be understood roughly as the luminous intensity of a point source divided by the solid angle of a sphere centred on that source. One candela is the intensity produced when a source emits radiation of 1/683 watt per steradian in a given direction at a frequency of 540×10¹² Hz, corresponding to green light.
  • Candlepower: an older unit, slightly smaller than a candela but often treated as approximately equal; it is now largely obsolete.
  • Lumen: the total quantity of visible light emitted in all directions. 1 lumen = 1 candela × 1 steradian (lm = cd·sr).
  • Nit: luminous intensity emitted or reflected per unit area. 1 nit = 1 candela per square metre (nt = cd/m²).
  • Lux: illuminance, or incident luminous flux per unit area. 1 lux = 1 lumen per square metre (lx = lm/m²).

In summary, luminous flux in lumens describes the ability of a source to emit light in all directions; luminous intensity in candelas describes its ability in a particular direction; luminance in nits describes emitted or reflected intensity per unit area; and illuminance in lux describes incident light per unit area.

Examples

A cinema screen is roughly 50 nits, while a phone used outdoors generally reaches at least 300 nits. This is why one should not look at a phone in a cinema. In total lumens, of course, the cinema screen far exceeds the phone.

Sony HDR televisions can reach 10,000 nits. Most HDR devices peak near 2,000 nits; anything higher becomes an instrument for blinding people.

Smartphones and tablets range from 200 to more than 1,000 nits. A 400–500-nit screen remains visible during the day, whereas 200 nits is difficult to see in sunlight.

Laptops and monitors range from 200 to more than 600 nits. Excessive brightness is not recommended: a 200-nit maximum is low-end, while 400 is sufficient. If the display remains hard to see, close the curtains instead of raising the brightness. The secondary display I just bought reaches 400 nits.

Contrast Ratio

Contrast is the ratio between the brightest white and darkest black in an image. A greater range means greater contrast. High contrast reveals more detail in both highlights and shadows and gives the picture a stronger sense of depth.

Interfaces

  • VGA: an ancient analogue interface. The last one I saw belonged to an antique family computer. It carries video only, is vulnerable to interference and distortion, often produces blurry text, and supports only relatively low resolutions such as 1080p@60Hz. It is disappearing, though it was glorious several decades ago.
  • DVI: introduced to remedy VGA’s limitations, DVI can carry both analogue and digital video and reach 1080p@144Hz, but does not support audio. It has also become rare and needs no detailed treatment.
  • HDMI: the mainstream digital interface, carrying synchronised audio and video. HDMI 2.0 provides 18 Gbit/s for 1440p@144Hz, 1080p@240Hz, or 4K@60Hz. HDMI 2.1 provides 48 Gbit/s, supports 4K@120Hz or 8K@60Hz, and adds dynamic HDR, making it suitable for game consoles and high-end graphics cards.
  • DisplayPort (DP): a professional PC interface. DP 1.4 supports 4K@120Hz HDR or 8K@60Hz HDR and 3:1 DSC compression. DP 2.0 raises bandwidth to 80 Gbit/s and natively supports 8K@60Hz.
  • USB-C: a multifunction interface supporting video through DP Alt Mode, data, and charging through USB Power Delivery. Some monitors connect to laptops with a single USB-C cable, though bandwidth limitations must be considered; USB 3.2 Gen 2, for example, supports only 4K@60Hz.

Competitive gamers should prefer DP 1.4 or HDMI 2.1 and above. Users of thin laptops may consider a single USB-C connection. Anyone switching among several devices should check whether the monitor includes KVM functionality.

Colour Gamut

Overview

Colour gamut is the range of colours a monitor can display; broader coverage generally means richer performance. Common standards include NTSC, now rather old, sRGB, P3, and Adobe RGB. For ordinary use, sRGB is usually enough: at least 95% for daily work and 99% or 100% for design or stronger preferences. Continue reading if more detail is required.

Further Detail

  • sRGB: developed by Microsoft and HP, it is the default gamut for Windows interfaces and much software, as well as the standard colour space for online graphics. Its coverage within CIE 1931 is limited, especially in green, but most current monitors easily exceed 96% sRGB.
  • Adobe RGB: created by Adobe when sRGB proved insufficient. It is familiar to photographers and editors using products such as Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere. Its broader gamut produces richer colour and is used mainly in expensive professional art and design monitors.
  • DCI-P3: proposed mainly by the American film industry, this relatively new digital-imaging standard emphasises realistic human visual experience and meets cinematic colour requirements. It covers more red and green than sRGB, though I have encountered it less often.
  • Rec.2020: the International Telecommunication Union treats it as a reference standard for high-quality projectors, televisions, and broadcasting equipment. It specifies resolution, frame rate, bit depth, colour gamut, and other parameters.
  • P3: broadly includes DCI-P3 and Display P3. The former is a film-industry standard; the latter is Apple’s revision of DCI-P3 with reference to sRGB. Their white points and gamma differ, but they cover the same colours and both expand substantially beyond sRGB except in blue.
  • NTSC: established by the US National Television System Committee in 1953 for the then-new CRT colour television. It is far too old for modern displays—Apple DOS 3.1 appeared in 1978 and MS-DOS in 1980—and almost no creator working for PCs or mobile devices uses NTSC as a workspace. Its main surviving purpose is comparison with other colour spaces.
  • Rec.709: the film and high-definition television counterpart of sRGB. It began as a colour-gamut standard for early colour television and remains the most widely used standard, including in China’s film and television industry.

Adobe RGB and DCI-P3 are much broader than sRGB and are called wide gamuts. A screen covering about 92% of DCI-P3 can generally be called wide-gamut.

Several Misconceptions

  • Monitors supporting multiple gamuts

Some professional monitors support different gamuts. A wide-gamut display usually exceeds sRGB, but showing sRGB material without management may produce colour shifts and distortion despite impressive saturation. The gamut must therefore be restricted to sRGB for ordinary use. Monitors with gamut-clamping functions explicitly advertise several modes: sRGB for everyday work and Adobe RGB for photographic editing or print proofing, where reliable colour matters.

  • Gamut volume above 100%

A manufacturer may advertise a figure such as 130%, meaning that the covered area extends beyond sRGB and reaches 130% of its nominal area. One trap is that shifting the gamut can improve the numerical value while distorting the actual picture.

  • 72% NTSC

Many irresponsible manufacturers and media outlets have promoted the equation “72% NTSC = 100% sRGB” to present an ordinary panel as suitable for professional work and equal to an older MacBook Pro with 100% sRGB, arguing that the MacBook also measured 72% NTSC. This exploits an information gap in consumers’ knowledge; the claim deserves scepticism.

Colour Depth

Colour depth is the number of bits used to store the colour of one pixel in a bitmap or video frame buffer, also expressed as bits per pixel (bpp). Greater depth provides more available colours and smoother transitions.

For each colour channel, 8 bit provides 256 grey levels, or 16.7 million colours across RGB, while 10 bit provides 1,024 levels, or 1.07 billion colours. Greater depth reduces banding and produces more natural gradients in skies and shadows.

Most mainstream monitors use native 8-bit panels. Some IPS panels simulate 10-bit output through frame-rate control and are labelled 8 bit + FRC. Native 10-bit panels cost more and appear mainly in professional design monitors and high-end OLED displays. Eight bits is sufficient for most people.

Note: some manufacturers advertise “1.07 billion colours” without making clear that dithering produces them.

Colour Accuracy

Colour accuracy measures whether a monitor displays the colour it is asked to display. It is usually expressed as ΔE, the deviation between displayed and standard colours; lower is better.

The same parameter is also written Delta E or △E.

  • △E ≤ 1: the difference is almost imperceptible and suitable for professional photography and print calibration.
  • △E ≤ 2: sufficient for design and film post-production.
  • △E > 3: colour shifts are visible and calibration is needed.

Professional monitors are calibrated before leaving the factory and include a ΔE report. Some support hardware calibration, such as writing a LUT. Ambient-light changes and panel ageing may cause accuracy to drift, so periodic recalibration with a device such as a Datacolor SpyderX or X-Rite i1Display Pro is recommended. During calibration:

  • Warm up the monitor for at least 30 minutes.
  • Disable dynamic contrast and HDR.
  • Select a gamut that matches the workflow.

HDR

HDR, High Dynamic Range, has become popular in recent years. Put simply, switching it on can make the image more impressive; the certification indicates its level.

By expanding brightness range and contrast, HDR reproduces highlight and shadow detail and colour levels closer to the physical world. Compared with standard dynamic range, it can preserve both textures in bright clouds and details in shadowed rocks, strengthening depth and immersion.

Standards

No single standard governs the whole industry.

  • HDR10: the basic standard, using static metadata with one brightness setting for an entire film. It supports 10-bit colour and usually requires peak brightness of at least 1,000 nits. It is widely used for games and video.
  • HDR10+: an upgraded form using dynamic metadata to optimise brightness frame by frame, with relatively broad compatibility.
  • Dolby Vision: a high-end Dolby Laboratories standard supporting 12-bit colour and dynamic metadata. It demands more capable hardware and appears mainly on premium televisions and streaming services such as Netflix.
  • DisplayHDR: a VESA certification system based on HDR10, with levels such as DisplayHDR 400, 600, and 1000. It specifies peak brightness, gamut coverage, and other parameters. DisplayHDR 1000, for example, requires 1,000-nit peak brightness and 99% DCI-P3 coverage.

Points to Note

  • Some monitors accept an HDR input signal but lack enough brightness or local dimming, so their actual result differs little from SDR.
  • HDR works better in a dark room; intense ambient light reduces the benefit of high brightness.
  • The source material, playback device, and display must all support HDR.
  • HDR is less necessary for games because games possess an inherent advantage at the signal source.
  • A television or monitor advertised as HDR-compatible may mean one of two things. First, both its display controller and panel satisfy an HDR standard. Second, the controller can decode HDR while the panel cannot necessarily pass certification. SDR is commonly based around 100 nits and the lowest HDR certification around 400 nits. A panel between those values can use controller-level HDR support to provide a wider range than SDR and an experience approaching HDR without meeting the full certification.

Weight

Weight matters for a portable monitor, unless one enjoys carrying a brick around. The acceptable value depends on the individual.
