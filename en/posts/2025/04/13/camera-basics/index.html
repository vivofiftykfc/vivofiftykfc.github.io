An Introduction to the Fundamentals of Cameras

A Complete Explanation of Camera Fundamentals: From Beginner to Expert

1. Preface

Photography is not only an art for recording life, but also a product of the encounter between technology and creativity. From the earliest black-and-white film cameras to today’s powerful digital equipment, continuous technological innovation has given us unlimited possibilities. Understanding the basic structure and operation of a camera is an important first step towards professional practice for every photography enthusiast. This article examines the fundamentals of cameras from several levels, helping readers understand both the principles behind equipment specifications and their flexible use in the creative process. (Thanks to ChatGPT and DeepSeek for their summaries, as well as to the summaries produced by earlier writers.)

2. Classification and Basic Knowledge

2.1 Types of Camera

  • Digital Single-Lens Reflex Camera (DSLR):

    A digital single-lens reflex camera. The term single lens is used in contrast with twin-lens reflex cameras: the same lens on a DSLR is used both to compose and to capture the image. A mirror system makes viewing and image formation possible. More specifically, while the photographer is composing the image, light entering through the lens strikes the mirror, is reflected towards the pentaprism, and is then refracted by the pentaprism so that the photographer can observe it through the optical viewfinder. When the shutter is pressed to expose the image, the mirror rises and the light travels directly to the image sensor. Once the exposure is complete, the mirror falls again. DSLRs respond quickly and offer stronger manual control, making them suitable for professional photographers and serious enthusiasts. They are now gradually being replaced by mirrorless cameras.

  • Mirrorless Camera:

    A mirrorless camera removes the reflex-mirror design and uses an electronic viewfinder directly. The advantages are the absence of vibration caused by the mirror opening and closing, as well as a smaller body and lower weight. At the same time, it preserves most of a DSLR’s performance—whatever a DSLR has, it generally has too—and has become increasingly popular in recent years.

  • Compact Digital Camera (DC):

    In other words, a point-and-shoot camera. Its lens cannot be changed, but it is simple to operate and convenient to carry, making it suitable for ordinary people’s daily records and travel photography.
    At this point, CCD cameras within this category deserve a brief mention. They basically replace the CMOS sensor found in an ordinary camera with a CCD. I shall not pass judgement: buy one if you want. From my personal perspective, however, CCD cameras can sit at the same table as Pop Mart products and other inexplicably fashionable objects. For details, see the explanation by the Zhihu expert listed in the references.

  • Smartphone Camera:

    Omitted.

2.2 Types of Image Sensor

The image sensor is the heart of a camera, and its performance directly influences image quality. There are two principal types:

  • CCD (Charge-Coupled Device):

    CCD is short for charge-coupled device. It can convert light into electric charge, store and transfer that charge, and retrieve the stored charge so that a voltage changes. The photoelectric effect produces charge upon the device’s surface, thereby creating a digital image.
    CCDs produce delicate images and reproduce colour accurately, but their manufacturing cost and energy consumption are relatively high. In practice, CMOS has already surpassed them, and they have gradually disappeared from public view. Recently, however, a trend has begun to speculate upon them again.

  • CMOS (Complementary Metal-Oxide Semiconductor):

    CMOS sensors are relatively inexpensive and consume less power. Most mainstream cameras now employ the technology, while technical progress continues to improve its image quality.

The larger the image sensor, or format—for example, full frame or APS-C—the more light it can generally capture. It therefore performs better in low-light environments, while also offering a relatively wider dynamic range and better control of noise.

2.3 Sensor Formats

  • Full Frame:

    A full-frame sensor is the same size as traditional 35 mm film, approximately 36 × 24 mm. It offers outstanding image quality, limited noise in weak light, a wide dynamic range, and flexible control of depth of field. It is the first choice for professional photography and high-end video production. Its disadvantages are the larger bodies and lenses, together with higher prices.

  • APS-C (Crop Sensor):

    An APS-C sensor is approximately half the size of full frame, around 23.6 × 15.7 mm. Because it is cheaper and more portable, it is widely used in entry-level DSLR and mirrorless cameras. It should be noted that the focal length of the lens must be multiplied by a crop factor, such as 1.5 or 1.6. The format is suitable for daily photography, travel, and enthusiast projects. Its image quality is weaker than that of full frame, but its cost-performance ratio is excellent.

  • Medium Format:

    Medium-format sensors are considerably larger than full frame—for example, 44 × 33 mm or more—and provide exceptional resolution, colour depth, and dynamic range. They are designed for commercial advertising, high-end portraiture, and artistic photography. Traditional medium-format cameras are bulky and expensive, although recent models have gradually become more portable, as demonstrated by Fujifilm’s GFX series.

  • Micro Four Thirds (M4/3):

    Measuring approximately 17.3 × 13 mm, this format emphasises a lightweight design. Both bodies and lenses are compact, making the system suitable for video, vlogs, and mobile outdoor applications such as drones and action cameras. Its image quality is weaker than full frame and APS-C, but its video performance is generally well optimised.

  • One Inch and Below (for example, 1 inch or 1/1.8 inch):

    These sensors are common in compact cameras, action cameras, and smartphones. Their extremely compact size limits their ability to collect light, so they depend upon algorithms such as multi-frame composition and AI noise reduction to improve image quality. They are suitable for casual daily records and sharing on social media.

2.4 Lenses and Imaging Systems

The following parameters deserve the greatest attention:

  • Focal Length:
    Focal length determines both the field of view and the degree of visual compression. Wide-angle lenses are suitable for landscapes and street photography, while telephoto lenses are often used for portraits and wildlife.

  • Aperture:
    The size of the aperture determines the quantity of incoming light and the resulting depth of field, and is commonly expressed as an f-number. A smaller f-number represents a wider aperture, which performs better in weak light and can also produce a softly blurred background.

  • Image Stabilisation:
    Some lenses or cameras contain image-stabilisation systems that reduce the effect of hand movement at slow shutter speeds. This is useful for indoor and night photography.

2.5 Understanding the Controls

Before using a camera, one should at least know what its buttons do. The information below comes from a post on Xiaoheihe. I have not reproduced its detailed illustrations; consult the original article instead:

  • Practical Knowledge: An Introduction to Cameras - Xiaoheihe

  • Basic Control Area

    • Power switch: Usually marked ON/OFF; on some cameras it is integrated into the mode dial.
    • Shutter button: Half-press to focus and press fully to take the photograph. A beep indicates that focusing is complete.
    • Mode dial: Selects a shooting mode, such as AUTO (fully automatic), P (programmed auto), S/Tv (shutter priority), A/Av (aperture priority), or M (fully manual).
    • Hot shoe: The metal slot on top of the camera, used to attach an external flash or microphone.

  • Function Shortcuts

    • ISO button: Quickly adjusts sensitivity. A higher value produces a brighter image but also more noise.
    • Exposure-compensation button (±): Raises or lowers image brightness in automatic modes. Follow the principle of adding for white and subtracting for black.
    • AF/MF selector: Switches between autofocus (AF) and manual focus (MF).
    • Q/Menu button: Opens the settings menu or a quick-control interface.
    • Directional buttons/control wheel: Adjust parameters or change the focus point.

  • Other Important Buttons

    • INFO/DISP: Changes the information displayed on screen, such as the histogram or electronic level.
    • Delete button (bin icon): Deletes photographs or videos.
    • Playback button (▶️ icon): Displays captured material.

The following key parameters can be seen through the viewfinder or upon the LCD screen, although their presentation varies slightly among models:

  • AFS: single autofocus

3. The Three Elements of Exposure

3.1 Aperture

Aperture controls not only the quantity of incoming light, but also depth of field:

  • Wide Aperture (small f-number):
    Allows more light to enter and is therefore suitable for weak-light conditions. It also produces a shallow depth of field, keeping the subject clear while blurring the background.

  • Narrow Aperture (large f-number):
    Reduces the amount of incoming light but increases depth of field. It is appropriate for landscapes and other scenes in which detail should remain balanced throughout the image.

3.2 Shutter Speed

  • Slow Shutter Speed:

    The exposure lasts longer, making it suitable for night scenes, flowing water, and light trails. A tripod is required to prevent blur.

  • Fast Shutter Speed:

    Freezes movement and is appropriate for sport, wildlife, and other rapidly moving subjects. It may, however, cause underexposure and leave you with a great dark lump of an image.

In creative photography, deliberately choosing a slow shutter can produce motion blur and add a dynamic rhythm to the image. Photographing star trails requires a different method: set an interval between individual exposures and combine the resulting images in post-production.

3.3 ISO Sensitivity

  • Low ISO:

    Produces less noise and better image quality, and is suitable for well-lit scenes.

  • High ISO:

    Increases sensitivity and is useful in weak light, but introduces more noise.

4. Metering and Exposure Compensation

4.1 Metering Modes

  • Spot Metering:

    Measures only a small area of the image and is suitable when the subject needs to be emphasised.

  • Centre-Weighted Metering:

    Gives priority to the centre of the frame while taking the surrounding light into account. It is suitable for most ordinary photographs.

  • Matrix/Evaluative Metering:

    Analyses the light across the complete frame and uses an algorithm to calculate the best exposure value. It is appropriate for general use.

  • Partial Metering:

    Meters the central portion of the frame. Use it when the subject occupies a relatively small local area and contrasts strongly with the background.

4.2 Exposure Compensation

In automatic modes, the camera may sometimes fail to measure complicated light accurately. Exposure compensation can then be used:

  • Positive Compensation:
    Increases exposure when insufficient detail is visible in dark areas.

  • Negative Compensation:
    Reduces exposure to prevent highlights from being overexposed and preserve more detail.

Understanding these settings makes it easier to respond flexibly to complicated lighting conditions and obtain more accurate exposures.

5. Shooting Modes

  • M Mode (Fully Manual):

    Gives the user complete manual control over aperture, shutter speed, and ISO, requiring the three elements of exposure to be balanced independently. It is suitable for experienced photographers dealing with complicated lighting, such as night scenes or studio work, and for precise forms of creation such as long exposures and astrophotography. It is demanding to operate, and the exposure scale must be observed in real time. Then again, you can take pictures perfectly well without it. There is no need to force yourself to use manual mode; enjoying the process is enough.

  • P Mode (Programmed Auto):

    The camera sets the aperture and shutter speed automatically, while offering slightly more manual adjustment than A mode.

  • Av/A Mode (Aperture Priority):

    The photographer sets the aperture manually and the camera chooses a matching shutter speed. Control of the aperture adjusts depth of field: a wide aperture can blur the background, while a narrow aperture extends the region in focus. It is suitable for portraits, landscapes, still life, and other subjects in which visual layers should be emphasised.

  • Tv/S Mode (Shutter Priority):

    The photographer sets the shutter speed manually and the camera selects the aperture. Control of the shutter speed can freeze movement with a fast shutter or create motion blur with a slow one, making the mode suitable for sport, traffic, and flowing water.

  • Auto Mode (Fully Automatic):

    The camera takes complete control of every parameter, including the flash, and the user needs only to compose the image and press the shutter. It is useful for urgent snapshots and complete beginners, but offers very little creative freedom and can easily be affected by an incorrect judgement from the algorithm.

  • Bulb Mode:

    Allows the shutter’s open time to be controlled manually: press the shutter to begin the exposure and release it to end. It is used for extremely long exposures, including star trails, light painting, and fireworks. A tripod and cable release are required to prevent camera shake.

  • Scene Modes (such as portrait, landscape, and night scene):

    The camera optimises a combination of parameters for a preset scene. Portrait mode, for example, automatically opens the aperture, while night mode raises the ISO. These modes are convenient for quickly photographing a particular subject, but are less flexible than semi-automatic modes such as P, Av, and Tv.

The essential difference among these modes is the distribution of “control over parameters.” From Auto to M, the user gradually takes control of more parameters. The cost is greater operational complexity, but the space for creative choice expands at the same time.

6. White Balance and Colour Management

The basic idea of white balance is that “under any light source, a white object should be restored to white.” In other words, it attempts to reproduce white as white under light of different colour temperatures.

Automatic white balance is sufficient in most situations. When the lighting is complicated, or when a particular tone is required, different Kelvin values can be selected to influence the image, producing a warmer or cooler appearance.

  • A lower K value → bluer, or cooler → suitable for snow scenes and ice sculptures;
  • A higher K value → yellower, or warmer → suitable for cafés and candlelit dinners.

6.1 Foundations of White Balance

White balance is primarily used to correct differences in colour temperature among light sources:

  • Automatic White Balance (AWB):

    The camera judges the colour temperature of the environment automatically, but complicated sources of light may produce a colour cast.

  • Manual Settings:

    The photographer adjusts the setting according to the source on location, such as daylight, tungsten lamps, or fluorescent light, ensuring that the colours in the image are accurate.

In practice, none of this is necessary. Just shoot RAW and throw the file into post-production for correction.

7. Focusing Systems and Focus Control

1. Manual Focus (MF): turn the ring yourself.

  • When to use it:

    • Macro photography, such as small insects or details of flower petals;
    • Stars at night or extremely dark scenes;
    • Situations in which autofocus repeatedly misses, such as animals behind cage bars or subjects behind glass.

  • Disadvantage: It is slow. You must turn the focusing ring yourself, and beginners can easily shake the camera and produce a blurred image.

2. Single Autofocus (AF-S): half-press the shutter to focus once; suitable for stationary subjects.

  • When to use it:

    • Landscapes, identification photographs, and arranged still life;
    • Focusing first and then moving the frame to compose—for example, focus upon a face before placing the person on the left side of the image;
    • It is less suitable for children or pets running around, or for sporting events.

3. Continuous Autofocus (AF-C): select the appropriate mode and hold the shutter for a burst, capturing moving subjects.

  • When to use it:

    • While the shutter is half-pressed, the focus follows a moving subject, such as a runner, bird, or car;
    • It works well with burst shooting.

  • Note: A complicated background may cause the system to lose the subject, so choose a clean background where possible.

4. Automatic Autofocus (AF-A): switches between AF-S and AF-C by itself. For example, it may first focus upon a stationary person and then follow automatically if the person suddenly starts running.

8. File Formats and Colour Spaces

  • RAW Format: An uncompressed file of original data, similar to a film negative, which records all the information captured by the sensor, including unprocessed colour, dynamic range, and detail.

    • Advantages:

      • Preserves the greatest possible room for post-production, allowing non-destructive correction of exposure, white balance, and colour deviation;
      • A high dynamic range reduces the risk of blown highlights and completely black shadows;
      • Suitable for professional photography, commercial work, and situations requiring careful retouching.

    • Disadvantages:

      • Large files, with high storage and transmission costs;
      • Requires specialised software such as Lightroom or Capture One and cannot be shared directly;
      • Straight-out-of-camera results appear relatively flat and depend upon post-processing skill.

  • JPEG Format: An image file compressed and processed inside the camera, directly producing a finished picture that can be shared.

    • Advantages:

      • Small files with strong compatibility; phones and computers require no special decoding;
      • In-camera algorithms optimise the output colours, including skin tones in portraits and saturation in landscapes;
      • Suitable for rapid delivery, social-media sharing, and non-professional requirements.

    • Disadvantages:

      • Compression loses detail, while reductions in dynamic range and colour information are irreversible;
      • Leaves very little room for post-production and easily produces tonal banding or noise.

Anyway, when taking photographs, just use RAW. JPEG is what you export after editing. As for the colour space, simply choose sRGB. Colour spaces are somewhat similar to the colour gamuts discussed in the article on monitors, which you can read if interested.
