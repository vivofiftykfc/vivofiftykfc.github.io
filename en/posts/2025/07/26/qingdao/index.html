2025-07-26 Qingdao

I have just returned from Qingdao. I might as well write another monthly note.

This was my second visit to Qingdao and my first trip with university classmates. To be honest, however, these two recent journeys have left me more confused than before. I no longer know what travelling is for. At first, perhaps, it meant seeing more places. Then it became an opportunity to talk with friends, and later a way of experiencing local life and meeting different people. After Dalian and Qingdao, though, I am no longer sure what the purpose is.

The itinerary was simple. On the first day, we walked around Ocean University of China and then watched the sea beside its gate. The second day was the competition; afterward, we sat on the beach for a while and ate seafood. On the third morning, we attended the award ceremony at a hotel. To my surprise, our rubbish project actually won second prize at the provincial level. Then we went to the aquarium and later to the bathing beach, where I nearly drowned. The fourth day is today: we slept until eleven, played some billiards, and then went our separate ways. I had several hours before my train, so I visited the Wine Museum and began writing this on the journey.

At Ocean University on the first day, the difference between the coast and the interior was immediately visible. Even the elderly security guards were tanned a remarkably—what is the word—sexy shade? It was the deep bronze that seems possible only after years of sea wind, a colour I had almost never seen in Xinyang or Beijing.

The university’s West Coast campus has indeed been sent into exile. There is no nearby metro station, the area is rather deserted, and commerce is less developed than in Liangxiang. The natural surroundings, however, are vastly better. Step outside and there is an open beach where one can watch the water and stand in the wind. The day of our visit was cloudy rather than painfully sunny. The sea was clean and blue, with no Ulva bloom in sight. I stood on the sand facing the water, the wind coming in from the sea and a small fishing boat being repaired nearby. It was difficult not to feel content.

“From my house, facing the sea, spring blossoms.” That is roughly the mood.

The blue water extended beyond sight, set beneath a blue sky and white clouds. Consider the joys and sorrows of a person scarcely more than a metre tall and weighing perhaps a hundred jin: what can they amount to before such an expanse? Whatever happens, the sea continues to rise and fall without expression. Day after day, waves strike the shore and wash the golden sand. As far as the water is concerned, nothing has happened at all.

I began to understand how an environment shapes those who inhabit it. People who live beside the sea may indeed acquire a broader heart.

On the second morning, we prepared for the defence. Our team was fourth, which meant we could eat and leave once the morning session ended. Lunch was provided in the canteen. To be honest, the competition taught me quite a lot.

Three teachers questioned us. The first asked about our latency index and the frame rate of the thermal camera. Since our project was a fire-monitoring drone, these were basic measurements. We had not taken them.

The second teacher worked in optoelectronics and on military drones. He began from requirements analysis and challenged the project point by point. Our thermal image, he argued, was of little use; the initial temperature data alone could support automatic judgment, while the image could be displayed on the terminal if necessary. If there was only one ultrasonic obstacle sensor, why not use a laser module costing a few yuan instead? The object-detection component also served little real purpose.

In summary, we had misunderstood the requirements and therefore misunderstood what we were supposed to build. A system intended to deliver genuine value should have emphasised speed and stability, rather than becoming the demonstration-oriented object we had produced.

The performance standards should also have been specified at the beginning. For ultrasonic obstacle avoidance, for example, the required detection and refresh rates ought to be derived from the drone’s maximum turning and flight speeds.

The teacher’s criticism was concrete and useful.

The third teacher asked which dataset we had used, then turned my own words against a claim in the report that the system could recognise building structures. The lesson was that one cannot be lazy with technical indicators.

That was roughly the feedback. The five-minute defence ended before we had finished. That evening, after seeing a senior student’s post about a top conference, I spoke with SC:

Exactly. We never identified the problem we were trying to solve.
Our requirements analysis really was poor.
We also lacked project-management experience.
But it does point toward another question:
how should I decide where my own energy belongs?
Requirements analysis for a project corresponds to the allocation of effort in personal development.
Even now, I have never objectively mapped the career paths available within our major.
I need to work out what preparation each path requires.
Most of the time, I just bury my head and compete without knowing where I am going.
Perhaps that is the next thing I need to do.

Two years spent in busy mediocrity. Is there still time to compete?

After lunch in the university canteen, I lay down at the hotel for a while and then returned to the beach to let the wind blow and play with the sand. It was ordinary enough, and there is little to write. Nothing happened that evening.

The award ceremony on the third day was held in the kind of luxurious hotel where I would never choose to eat on my own.

To my surprise, our pile of rubbish won second prize. We had no answer to question after question. It was more like an advance notice of admission: we did not enter the national competition, but the result was not entirely bad.

Still, at this stage I would probably be better served by placing my feet on the ground and building a few small things properly.

In the afternoon, we visited the aquarium. The exhibits were average and the ticket expensive. I took photographs, looked at jellyfish, fish, seals, sea lions, and specimens. That was about it.

What remains disturbing is what happened next at Qingdao No. 1 Bathing Beach.

I cannot swim, yet somehow charged into the sea with another friend. I assumed that water below my height could do little harm and ignored the force of the current. A wave knocked me over immediately. Fortunately, my friend saw me disappear and pulled me up. I swallowed only a couple of mouthfuls of seawater, which left me badly nauseated.

But what if he had not seen me? What if the water had carried me away? The thought is frightening. Seawater really is both salty and bitter.

I should stop imagining myself as uniquely important, intelligent, or powerful. There are things one must fear and dangers against which one must guard. A prudent person does not stand beneath a collapsing wall.

For a moment, I even seemed to experience the edge of dying—not that I was truly about to die. The mind simply froze: wait, am I actually going to die? Can anyone save me? What am I supposed to do? Wait—

It was a calm sensation of death, as though the person about to die were not me and yet undeniably were. Or perhaps it was numbness. Perhaps relief.

After I was pulled out, a man nearby told me, in a severe voice, not to enter the water if I could not swim. I laughed it off, grinning and saying that I would stop, and the moment passed.

It reminded me of the traveller who died inexplicably in the Lop Nur desert:

To be honest, I agree with the man’s reaction. Anger evolved as a tool of communication—or, for that matter, all emotions are tools of communication. A stern rebuke was one of the best uses to which that tool could have been put. I am grateful to him. I am the one who needs to reflect.

I cannot continue acting like that. A prudent person does not stand beneath a dangerous wall. The external world must not be treated lightly, although neither should it be feared beyond reason. Some things are to be done; others are not.

The fourth day is today. I slept until eleven, played billiards with Dingding for an hour, and took a taxi into the city. We have spent a ridiculous amount on taxis over the past few days. I wrote the paragraphs above while travelling.

I first visited the Wine Museum. Afterward, I had intended simply to walk toward the railway station. It was only a few kilometres and I still had two hours; I was alone and free to decide. Then I somehow reached a metro station, looked again at the map, and realised how close I was to the old streets around Zhongshan Road. So I went to Qianhaichao for sea-cucumber fried rice.

The Beer Museum was honestly overpriced. Fortunately, I bought my ticket online. Beside me, a young Korean couple with a child purchased theirs on site and paid considerably more.

The encounter made me conscious of deficiencies in both communication and foreign-language ability. The museum employee’s English was poor, so communication was difficult. I used my own half-finished foreign language to explain the situation. The man spoke some Chinese, though not fluently. I then offered to translate the museum text for them and was refused.

The sea-cucumber rice at Qianhaichao really was delicious, but painfully expensive: more than seventy yuan for a bowl that was not enough to make me full. I added a plate of fried clams and a serving of dumplings, spending one hundred and fifty yuan in total. Still, the rice really was good.

Leaving the Wine Museum and walking toward the metro, I suddenly realised that I was about to depart and might never see the street before me again. A strange sadness arrived.

It should have been a beautiful departure. Why could I not leave without hesitation? Was I still unable to let go? Perhaps my friend was right: you do not love beautiful scenery; you love the self who is looking at it.

I think I may nevertheless have found a meaning for travel. Within China, however, the experience does not feel unfamiliar enough. I still want to enter a foreign country, experience an environment that is wholly strange, meet people I have never known, communicate with them, and understand their thoughts and positions.

Before that, I must learn a foreign language properly and change the part of my character that does not know how to live among other people.

I am nearly back at university. It is 23:23, so I will stop here.

Again

I have experienced much more during this period than I would have by remaining in the dormitory, and I have also thought a great deal. Why do I keep this blog? Why place it somewhere most people will never see—somewhere equivalent to remaining undiscovered? And if I do not want it discovered, why put it on the public internet at all?

I do not know.
