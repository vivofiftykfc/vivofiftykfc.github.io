Principles of Communication, Chapter 1: Introduction

This textbook is, in fact, an introduction to an introduction. It provides the background of communication: what communication is, how its quality may be judged, and how information can be transmitted both efficiently and reliably.

This chapter supplies that initial background.

1. Basic Concepts of Communication

Objects of study: signals, messages, and information. Their distinctions can be left aside for the moment.

The basic system model is shown below:

An analog communication system uses a modulator and a demodulator. The frequency of an electrical signal suitable for transmission through free space is generally different from that of the source signal, so modulation is needed to transform it. The resulting signal is called a modulated signal, or a bandpass signal, because its spectrum is usually bandpass in character.

A digital communication system uses source coding, channel coding, and the corresponding demodulation and decoding procedures. It does not modulate information in quite the same way as an analog system. What it requires is digital modulation and demodulation, which amounts to shifting the spectrum, together with efficient AD conversion and interference suppression.

PS:

One disadvantage of digital communication is its relatively large demand for bandwidth. Its time-domain waveform is rather square and therefore contains high-frequency components. Even an approximation in the frequency domain cannot be too narrow without causing serious distortion.

Moreover, common digital modulation schemes, including ASK, PSK, and QAM, also carry digital information within a frequency band.

The higher the modulation order—for example, 16QAM or 64QAM—the more bits can be transmitted within the same bandwidth. This is what is commonly meant by an improvement in spectral efficiency.

Other classifications, such as half-duplex and full-duplex, need not be memorized at this stage. For now, it is enough to know what the field studies: the units of its objects and the model formed from those units.

2. Measuring Information

Information theory has already covered this part once. What operation did it use? Take the reciprocal and then the logarithm, and one obtains the self-information:

$$
I=-log_aP(x)
$$

This definition neatly satisfies two requirements:

  • The less probable an event is, the more information it carries.

  • The information carried by a message composed of several independent events equals the sum of the information carried by those individual events.

The information entropy $H$ of a discrete source is then defined as:

$$
H(x) = \sum P(x_i)I(x_i)
$$

It is simply a weighted sum describing the average amount of information.

3. Principal Performance Measures of a Communication System

The first measure is effectiveness, which describes whether a system can transmit signals efficiently. More specifically, it is judged through transmission rate.

In a digital communication system, bandwidth efficiency is defined as the transmission rate per unit bandwidth:

$$
\eta = \frac{R_B}{B}(Baud/Hz)
$$

or

$$
\eta_t = \frac{R_b}{B} \quad (\text{b/(s·Hz)})
$$

$R_B$ is the symbol transmission rate, or simply the symbol rate. It is the number of symbols transmitted per unit time, normally per second. Its unit is the baud (Baud), which is why it is also called the baud rate.

If the duration of each symbol is $T_B(s)$, then:

$$R_B = \frac{1}{T_B} \quad (\text{Baud}) $$

$R_b$ is the information transmission rate, also known as the bit rate. It is the average amount of information transmitted per unit time and is measured in bits per second (b/s).

Since each symbol in an $M$-ary system carries $\log_2 M$ bits of information, the symbol rate and information rate have the following definite relationship:

$$R_b = R_B \log_2 M \quad (\text{b/s})$$

or

$$R_B = \frac{R_b}{\log_2 M} \quad (\text{Baud})$$

For example, if the symbol rate is 1200 Baud and an octal system is used, the information rate is 3600 b/s.
If the duration of each binary symbol is T_b, then T_B and T_b are related as follows:

$$T_B = T_b \log_2 M$$

A good communication system must not merely transmit information; it must also ensure that the transmission is correct and reliable.

In an analog communication system, reliability is normally measured by the ratio of output signal power to noise power at the receiver, namely the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N). The value of S/N is related to the modulation scheme, a subject to be discussed later.

In a digital communication system, reliability can be represented by the error rate. It reflects the probability of an error during transmission and is often defined as the proportion of incorrectly transmitted symbols among all transmitted symbols. More precisely, the symbol error rate is the probability that a symbol is received incorrectly:

$$P_e = \frac{\text{错误码元数}}{\text{传输总码元数}}$$

or

$$P_b = \frac{\text{错误比特数}}{\text{传输总比特数}}$$
