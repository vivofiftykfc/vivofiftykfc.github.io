1 Spectral Analysis of Random Processes

As usual, before analysing random signals, let us begin with deterministic signals and establish an initial intuition.

1.1 Review of the Spectrum of Deterministic Signals

(1) Energy Signal

This is a relatively simple idea. Consider, for example, a sudden “duang” sound: once it has been played, it is over, and its finite energy can be calculated directly.

A signal may be highly irregular in time, yet what often matters is the frequencies at which its energy is concentrated.
Thus, in moving from the time domain to the frequency domain, the first step is to define energy:

$$
E = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |x(t)|^2 , dt
$$

If $E<\infty$, the signal is called an energy signal, as in the case of a finite-duration, non-periodic signal.

Its Fourier transform is:
$$
X(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t} dt
$$

The squared magnitude gives the distribution of energy over frequency and is called the energy spectral density:

$$
|X(\omega)|^2
$$

Parseval’s theorem:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |X(\omega)|^2 d\omega
$$

The energy spectral density reveals how a signal’s energy is distributed in the frequency domain.

Intuitively, the theorem converts the integral of a squared signal in the time domain into a corresponding integral in the frequency domain. Energy can therefore be calculated from two different perspectives.

(2) Power Signal

$X(t)$ generally does not come to an end, so its energy is infinite.

We therefore adopt a different perspective and examine only the average energy per unit time—that is, power:

$$
P = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt
$$

A periodic signal with finite power is called a power signal. Our concern then shifts to the distribution of power over frequency, namely, power spectral density (PSD).

Power spectral density is defined as:

$$
S_x(\omega) = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{E[|X_T(\omega)|^2]}{2T}
$$

Here, $X_T(\omega)$ is the Fourier transform within the time window $[-T, T]$.

PSD is therefore the distribution of average power along the frequency dimension.

1.2 Average Power Spectral Density of a Random Signal

The power spectral density of a random signal $X(t)$ is defined below.

It represents the average power of the signal in the neighbourhood of a particular frequency.

$$
S_X(\omega) = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{E[|X_T(\omega)|^2]}{2T}
$$

Its supposed characteristics include the following:

  1. A sample signal is generally a power signal;

  2. Different sample signals possess different spectral densities;

  3. $S_X(\omega)$ is a random function, and its mathematical expectation is generally taken;

  4. The random-signal power $P_X = E[|X(t)|^2]$ is a random variable, and usually only its mean is of interest.

1.3 Power Spectral Density and the Complex-Frequency Plane

The power spectral density $S_X(\omega)$ is defined on the complex-frequency plane $s=j\omega$:
$$
S_X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} R_X(\tau)e^{-s\tau} d\tau
$$
If $S_X(\omega)$ is a rational spectrum, it can be expressed as:
$$
S_X(s) = K \frac{\prod (s - \alpha_i)(s + \alpha_i)}{\prod (s - \beta_i)(s + \beta_i)}
$$
A real signal satisfies:
$$
S_X(s) = S_X^*(-s)
$$

2 Properties of the Power Spectral Density of a Random Process

  1. Real-valued evenness:
    $$
    S_X(-\omega) = S_X(\omega)
    $$

  2. Non-negativity:
    $$
    S_X(\omega) \ge 0
    $$

  3. Parseval relation:
    $$
    R_X(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} S_X(\omega)d\omega
    $$

  4. Finiteness of the integral:
    For a stationary process, $\int S_X(\omega)d\omega$ converges.

  5. Rational spectral factorisation:
    If the spectrum is a rational function, its poles lie in the left half-plane, while its zeros lie on the imaginary axis or in the left half-plane.

3 Relationship between Power Spectral Density and the Autocorrelation Function

Wiener–Khinchin Theorem

If the random process $X(t)$ is wide-sense stationary (WSS), then:
$$
S_X(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} R_X(\tau)e^{-j\omega\tau} d\tau
$$
$$
R_X(\tau) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} S_X(\omega)e^{j\omega\tau} d\omega
$$

The average power is:
$$
E[X^2(t)] = R_X(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} S_X(\omega)d\omega
$$

The smoother a signal is, the more slowly $R_X(\tau)$ decays, and the more its spectrum is dominated by low-frequency components.
The faster a signal changes, the more rapidly $R_X(\tau)$ decays, indicating stronger high-frequency components.

4 Power Spectral Density of a Discrete-Time Random Process

Suppose that $X(n)$ is a wide-sense stationary random sequence obtained by uniformly sampling $X(t)$ at a sampling interval $T$.

If $X(n)$ is a stationary discrete random sequence:

$$
R_X(m) = E[X(n)X(n+m)] = R_X(mT)
$$

Its power spectral density is:

$$
S_X(\Omega) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty} R_X(m)e^{-j\Omega m}
$$

Its inverse transform is:

$$
R_X(m) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S_X(\Omega)e^{j\Omega m} d\Omega
$$

The average power is:

$$
E[|X(n)|^2] = R_X(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S_X(\Omega)d\Omega
$$

When the sampling interval is $T$:

$$
S_X(\omega) =\frac{1}{T} \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} S_c(\omega - k\omega_s)
$$

This is the sampling theorem for power spectral density.

Here, $\omega_s = \frac{2\pi}{T}$.

5 Cross-Spectral Density of Jointly Stationary Random Processes

To be honest, I can no longer follow what she is deriving on the slides. All the notes below were therefore completed by AI.

If $X(t)$ and $Y(t)$ are jointly stationary processes, their cross-power spectral density is defined as:
$$
S_{XY}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} R_{XY}(\tau)e^{-j\omega\tau} d\tau
$$

It has the following properties:

  1. $S_{XY}^*(\omega) = S_{YX}(\omega)$;
  2. For real processes, $S_{XY}(-\omega) = S_{YX}^*(\omega)$;
  3. If $X,Y$ are uncorrelated, then $S_{XY}(\omega)=0$.

6 White Noise

(1) Definition

  • If the power spectral density is constant:
    $$
    S_N(\omega) = \frac{N_0}{2}
    $$
    the noise is called white noise.

  • Its autocorrelation function is:
    $$
    R_N(\tau) = \frac{N_0}{2}\delta(\tau)
    $$

(2) Ideal White Noise

Its characteristics include:

  • A mean of zero;
  • A constant $S_N(\omega)$;
  • Infinite average power;
  • Non-existence in practice, meaning that it can only be approximated.

(3) Band-Limited White Noise and Coloured Noise

  • If $S_N(\omega)$ is constant within the finite bandwidth $[-W, W]$:
    $$
    S_N(\omega) = N_0/2, \quad |\omega| \le W
    $$
    it is called band-limited white noise;

  • If the spectral density varies with frequency, the noise is called coloured noise.

(4) Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

It is defined as:
$$
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_s}{P_n}, \quad \text{(dB)} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_s}{P_n}
$$

Example:
Suppose $s(t)=4\cos(\omega_0 t)$, while $n(t)$ is low-pass white noise with a bandwidth of $2$ kHz and a power spectral density of $S_N(\omega)=2$:

$$
P_s = \frac{A^2}{2} = 8, \quad P_n = 2W/\pi = 8000
$$
$$
\mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{8}{8000} \approx -30 \text{ dB}
$$
