2025-10-12 One Plus One

One

It seems that, in the course of a life, one must leave the place where one stands before the existence of distance can be understood. And so, at a midnight already touched by cold, I arrived at the airport. Familiar classmates travelled beside me; what truly kept me company was a question that appeared quietly at the beginning of the journey: why was I setting out?

Under the lights of the departure hall, I watched aircraft rise and land beyond the glass and thought about my own familiar cycle of sleeping and waking. The immense terminal appeared empty and indifferent. Someone stood by the railing with a book, taking shelter from one part of reality after another; someone else folded loneliness into a quiet posture across several seats. Leaning against an automatic massage chair while a meaningless mechanism pushed at my rigid back, I suddenly realised that what prevents sleep is never the hardness of a chair. It is the drifting sensation of belonging nowhere at that moment—no longer at the point of departure, not yet at the destination, suspended like a pendulum in mid-air and uncertain toward which end it should fall.

—Why was I setting out?

Guangzhou possesses a kind of low-voiced prosperity. Walking through the city, I passed the carved beams and painted rafters of the Chen Clan Ancestral Hall, where fine patterns of light turned across the walls like cloth wrinkled by time. Red flags lined the roads of Shamian Island, as if retelling the fierce years of past uprisings. I walked through damp old lanes, ate fragrant beef, drank a bowl of cool sweet soup, watched moss spread across mottled stone, and climbed Canton Tower to look down upon the lights of countless homes unfolding like a river of stars.

At that moment, however, I felt a desolate truth with unusual clarity. No matter how far one looks upward, how high one climbs, or how persistently one searches, a human being remains no more than a grain in the sea. Perhaps an entire life is enough only to light one’s own small candle.

The last time I looked upon a scene like this, I was travelling south from Beijing and sitting beside the aircraft window. The ground seemed to lie beneath my feet; the whole city could be taken in at a glance. I had the pride of youth, flowers and a spirited horse. Why, then, did the same view produce such a different state of mind now?

Perhaps it is time to seek the spirit of those honoured people of the past: neither delighted by external things nor saddened by personal fortune. Even if a lifetime purchases only one candle and one inch of light—unable to measure boundless space or disperse a night black as lacquer—that may be enough. That inch of light is sufficient to prove that we once stood and once breathed.

Sunset followed sunrise. At the old site of the Whampoa Military Academy, time acquired a solemn voice. The wind in the corners resembled an echo of the past; it raised no great wave and merely sighed beside the ear. Had I come several years earlier, I would probably have been full of confidence, discussing the fate of nations and writing with extravagant force. Now I lowered my head and walked in silence. I listened instead: to history turning itself into story within silence, and to the self distinguishing sincerity from performance within the returning echo. Memory and reality crossed beneath my feet. Many expectations and desires that had been enlarged and trusted proved to be little more than long acts of self-deception. What mattered was often only a small action in the present, a rational thought, or a quiet answer.

—Why was I setting out?

I reached Shenzhen at night. The city surged in the darkness like a tide. Skyscrapers stood without speaking, silent giants above the streets, while a helicopter cut a cold path overhead and crowds gathered only to disperse again. Beside the lake in Talent Park, I received news from home. The vibration of the phone felt unusually abrupt in my palm.

At that moment, a strange estrangement appeared. I was a grain of dark sand in an ocean of light, unable to enter the world’s current no matter how violently it moved. The louder the surroundings became, the more clearly one heard the echo inside oneself.

—For what did the person I was three years ago set out?

I entered Hong Kong through Lo Wu. Hong Kong seemed an absurd city, where prosperity existed beside decay and inclusion beside discrimination. Kowloon Walled City had become history, yet something of it still hid beneath the street’s neon lights, in the narrow lanes and in the rough, cold sound of Cantonese.

Inside an art exhibition, dismantled and reconstructed installations stood beside declarations against tradition. Under the neon lights, several questions flashed repeatedly: “What do you believe?” “Where do you stand?” I had no answer. I had imagined that travel meant looking at scenery; instead, the scenery had turned around to examine me.

I climbed the mountain road toward Victoria Peak. Every step pressed the questions again:

—What am I seeking? Why do I continue walking?

I remained silent. I did not know.

That night I watched the fireworks. Wind from Victoria Harbour moved across my face, while the city’s outline was softened by the water into a blurred line of verse. The fireworks and the light offered more than a moment’s splendour. Somewhere between immensity and smallness, they restored a sense of balance: the world remained vast, yet I could still breathe within it.

I still did not know why I had set out.

Beyond the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge, the cities changed rapidly. Macao resembled a hollow city concealed beneath a fading shell of gold. The casinos remained brilliantly lit. People revolved through them, placed bets, shouted in triumph, then lost and left. Magnificence had hardened into an almost absurd stillness: the more splendid the surroundings, the smaller the people appeared. We held up our chips believing ourselves to be gambling with fate, without recognising that what we were spending was our own capacity for hope.

I suddenly understood that this journey had not been a search for answers. It had been an opportunity to observe the many forms of the world, to use other lives as mirrors, and to strip away one false and immature self after another until only a candle willing to burn remained.

I was setting out toward the beginning.

The return journey unfolded at the airport. The aircraft crossed the clouds and left behind the sea and lights of Lingnan. Beyond the window lay plains and mountains without apparent end, and a peculiar calm arrived. I had found no answer on the journey. Yet through repeated questions and fatigue, I had confirmed that I was still searching.

Perhaps that was already enough.

We do not enter the world in order to win a particular ending. We enter it so that, in the limitless dark, we may remain willing to raise a small candle of our own. It may be weak and the wind may extinguish it at any moment. Yet as long as I choose to shelter it, it continues to burn.

I paused among the lights of the south. Now I carry one of them back within me. It does not illuminate the distance, but it illuminates the person I am at this moment.

—I am still on the road. I still have to set out.

Two

Before acting, one should certainly weigh advantages and harms. More important, however, is the recognition that every action moves some interest from one place to another. Whether the movement appears lighter than a feather or heavier than a mountain, it will touch another person’s boundary and disturb some invisible string.

There is therefore no need to be terrified of offending people. As long as the world continues to operate and people remain in contact, even a silent glance or an apparently casual answer contains movement—of resources, emotion, or position. Wherever something moves, friction follows. The promise that one can avoid offending everyone is little more than self-deception.

There are only three circumstances in which nobody is offended:

First, the other person voluntarily cuts away part of themselves and remains grateful for the opportunity.

Second, the sacrifice ends with you, while everyone else only receives.

Third, you are so insignificant that nobody cares even when you shout.

If that is so, one need ask only three questions: is my action logically consistent? Can I answer for it to my own conscience? Does it meet the standards by which I have chosen to conduct myself? If the left side of the brain has no reason to attack the right and one’s values do not begin fighting inside the chest, there is no need to tremble. A clear position should be followed by a steady step.

By contrast, if one hesitates over everything, retreats before everyone, and refuses to state a position, what others eventually see is not gentleness but transparency. To be understood is fortunate. To be ignored is a slow and silent disappearance.
