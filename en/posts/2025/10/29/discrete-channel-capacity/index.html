Information Theory, Chapter 3: Discrete Channels and Channel Capacity

1. Mathematical Models and Categories of Channels

1.1 The Function of a Channel and the Study of Capacity

  • Function of a channel: to transmit and store information in the form of signals.

  • What channel-capacity research asks: under what conditions can the amount of information passing through a channel be maximized?

1.2 Categories of Channels

Channels may be classified according to several main criteria.

(To put it bluntly, there is no need to examine this list too closely.)

  1. According to the characteristics of the input and output signals: discrete channels, continuous channels, semi-discrete or semi-continuous channels, and waveform channels.

  2. According to the number of input and output random variables: single-symbol channels and multi-symbol channels.

  3. According to the number of inputs and outputs: single-user channels and multi-user channels.

  4. According to whether interference is present: channels with interference, which are actual channels, and channels without interference, which are ideal or simple channels.

  5. According to whether the channel has memory: memoryless channels, whose output depends only on the current input, and channels with memory.

1.3 Mathematical Model of a Discrete Channel

Both the input and output random variables of a discrete channel take discrete values.

A Single-Symbol Discrete-Channel Model

  • Input alphabet: $X = {a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_r}$

  • Output alphabet: $Y = {b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_s}$

  • Channel characteristics: described by the transmission probability, also called the transition probability, $P(y_j|x_i)$.

  • Model description: the probability space $[X, p(y|x), Y]$ can be used to describe the model.

The Channel Matrix

To describe the mathematical model of a channel more conveniently, we can write it in matrix form.

The transmission probabilities $p_{ij} = P(b_j|a_i)$ form the following $r \times s$ matrix:

$$
\mathbf{P} =
\begin{bmatrix}
P(b_1|a_1) & P(b_2|a_1) & \cdots & P(b_s|a_1) \
P(b_1|a_2) & P(b_2|a_2) & \cdots & P(b_s|a_2) \
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \
P(b_1|a_r) & P(b_2|a_r) & \cdots & P(b_s|a_r)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The elements in each row of the matrix sum to 1.

Probability Relations

  1. Joint probability: $P(x_i y_j) = P(y_i)P(x_i|y_i) = P(x_i)P(y_j|x_i)$.

Here, $P(y_i|x_i)$ may be called the forward probability, or the prior probability, while $P(x_i|y_i)$ may be called the backward probability, or the posterior probability.

  1. Output probability: $P(y_j) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(x_i)P(y_j|x_i)$.

  2. Posterior probability: $P(x_i|y_j) = \frac{P(x_i, y_j)}{P(y_j)}$.

All the posterior probabilities sum to 1. This means that whatever signal is received at the output must have resulted from one of the symbols entered at the input.

2. Average Mutual Information

2.1 Definition

Average mutual information $I(X;Y)$ represents, after the output $Y$ has been received, the average amount of information obtained from each symbol about the input $X$.

$$
I(X;Y) = H(X)-H(X/Y) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{s} P(x_i, y_j) \log_2 \frac{P(x_i, y_j)}{P(x_i)P(y_j)} \quad \text{(bit/符号)}
$$

Wait—is the latter half of this formula actually correct? Let us set that question aside for the moment; at this stage, understanding what it means is enough.

In practice, the definition tells us how much useful information has been transmitted, so it serves as a measure of information transmission. We begin with the original uncertainty about the source, subtract the uncertainty that remains afterwards, and thereby find how much uncertainty has been removed.

2.2 Physical Meaning Expressed Through Entropy

We first introduce the concept of channel equivocation.

To begin with, $H(X)$ measures the prior uncertainty of the variable $X$ and is therefore also called prior entropy.

We can then extend the idea: after $b_j$ has been received, the posterior entropy of $X$ is the information measure of the input symbol after receiving $b_j$:

$$ H(X / b_j) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(a_i / b_j) \log \frac{1}{p(a_i / b_j)}$$

On this basis, we obtain the channel equivocation, or conditional entropy:

$$ H(X / Y) = E\left[ H(X / b_j) \right] = \sum_{X,Y} P(xy) \log \frac{1}{P(x / y)} $$

It represents the uncertainty about the input symbol that remains after one symbol has been received at the output, and this uncertainty is caused by interference in the channel. If there is no interference, $H(X/Y)=0$; in general, $H(X/Y) < H(X)$, which indicates that transmission through the channel removes part of the uncertainty of the source and thus supplies information.

  1. From the input side—the acquisition of information:

    $$
    I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y)
    $$

    • $H(X)$: prior uncertainty, also called prior entropy.

    • $H(X|Y)$: channel equivocation, or loss entropy, representing, after $Y$ has been received, the uncertainty about $X$ that remains.

    • Meaning: $I(X;Y)$ is the amount by which, after $Y$ is received, uncertainty about $X$ decreases.

  2. From the output side—the loss of information:
    $$
    I(Y;X) = H(Y) - H(Y|X)
    $$

    • $H(Y)$: uncertainty of the output.

    • $H(Y|X)$: noise entropy, representing, after $X$ has been sent, the uncertainty about $Y$ that remains because of channel noise. Whether it is called noise entropy or loss entropy depends on the perspective from which it is observed.

    • Conclusion: average mutual information is symmetric; that is, $I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)$.

3. Properties of Average Mutual Information

  1. Symmetry: $I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)$.

Different perspectives lead to the same result; only the point of departure changes.

  1. Non-negativity: $I(X;Y) \ge 0$.

  2. Extremal property: $I(X;Y) \le H(X)$ and $I(X;Y) \le H(Y)$.

    • Ideal channel, with a noiseless one-to-one correspondence: $H(X|Y)=0, H(Y|X)=0$, and therefore $I(X;Y) = H(X) = H(Y)$.

    • Worst channel, with independent input and output: $H(X|Y)=H(X), H(Y|X)=H(Y)$, and therefore $I(X;Y) = 0$.

  3. Convexity and concavity:

    • $I(X;Y)$ is a concave function of the source probability distribution $p(x_i)$.

    • $I(X;Y)$ is a convex function of the channel transition probability $p(y_j|x_i)$.

4. Channel Capacity and Its General Method of Calculation

4.1 Definition of Channel Capacity

This section begins to examine how much a channel can carry.

  • The information transmission rate $R$ of a channel: $R = I(X;Y)$ (bits per symbol).

  • Channel capacity $C$: the maximum information transmission rate of the channel, namely the greatest value of average mutual information over all possible input probability distributions $\mathbf{p}(x)$.

$$
C = \max_{p(x_i)} I(X;Y) \quad \text{(bit/信道符号)}
$$

  • Channel capacity per unit time $C_t$: $C_t = \frac{1}{t} C$ (bits per second).

  • Conclusion: the channel capacity $C$ is a parameter that describes an inherent property of the channel.

4.2 Capacity of a Discrete Noiseless Channel

A discrete noiseless channel is an ideal channel. In its transition-probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$, every row contains one and only one non-zero element, whose value is 1.

  1. Noiseless and lossless channel, with a one-to-one correspondence: $r=s$, and every $a_i$ corresponds to one $b_j$.

    • $H(X|Y)=0$ and $H(Y|X)=0$.

    • Capacity: $C = \max H(X) = \log_2 r$ (when the $P(x_i)$ are equiprobable).

  2. Noisy but lossless channel, with one non-zero element in each column: $r < s$, while each column contains only one non-zero element.

    • $H(X|Y)=0$ (receiving $Y$ determines $X$).

    • Capacity: $C = \max H(X) = \log_2 r$.

  3. Noiseless but lossy channel, with one non-zero element in each row: $r > s$, while each row contains only one non-zero element.

    • $H(Y|X)=0$ (sending $X$ determines $Y$).

    • Capacity: $C = \max H(Y) = \log_2 s$ (when the $P(y_j)$ are equiprobable).

The conclusion is that the channel capacity C depends only on the number r of input symbols or the number s of output symbols, rather than on the characteristics of the source.

5. Capacities of Extended, Parallel, and Cascaded Channels

5.1 Capacity of an Extended Discrete Memoryless Channel

  • Using a single-symbol discrete memoryless channel independently $N$ times—that channel being $W$—forms the extended channel $W^N$.

  • Channel capacity: $C^{(N)} = N C$.

5.2 Capacity of Independent Parallel Channels

  • The channel consists of $k$ independent subchannels connected in parallel.

  • Channel capacity: $C_{\text{并联}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} C_i$.

This is more or less self-evident. The capacity of the parallel channel does not exceed the sum of all its parts.

5.3 Mutual Information in Cascaded Channels and the Data Processing Theorem

  • Two channels are connected in cascade, and information travels along $X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z$, forming a Markov chain. In other words, for the Markov chain $X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z$, channel $\text{I}$ has an output $Y$ that depends only on its input $X$, while channel $\text{II}$ has an output $Z$ that depends only on its input $Y$; hence $P(z|y,x)=P(z|y)$.

  • Data Processing Theorem:

    $$
    I(X;Z) \le I(X;Y) \quad \text{且} \quad I(X;Z) \le I(Y;Z)
    $$

  • Conclusion: after a message has passed through several stages of processing, the average mutual information between the input and output messages tends to decrease. Data processing cannot increase information.

Basically, this too is a rather obvious statement.

6. Matching a Source to a Channel

This section mainly considers how suitable source coding can be used to match the average information rate $R_s$ of a source to the channel capacity $C$, thereby making communication both efficient and reliable.

$$
R_s \le C
$$

This part introduces the concept of channel redundancy. Since the capacity of a channel does not necessarily match the source, some part of that capacity will often remain unused. Channel redundancy is simply obtained by subtracting the average mutual information of the source from the channel capacity. We want to transform the messages produced by the source into messages from a new source that are better suited to transmission through the channel; this process is called lossless source coding. In this way, the amount of information carried by the source can be maximized and the channel redundancy can approach zero. The matching problem will be discussed further in Chapter 5, so there is no need to go into detail here.
