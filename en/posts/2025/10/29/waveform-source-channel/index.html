Information Theory, Chapter 4: Waveform Sources and Waveform Channels

Section 1: Statistical Properties and Discretization of Waveform Sources

A random waveform source produces a random process ${x(t)}$ that is continuous both in time and in value. Each sample function $x_i(t)$ is one realization of that random process. Such processes may be divided into stationary and non-stationary ones—a distinction that has now appeared for the third time in different courses.

Ergodicity

I will not explain precisely what it is here; let us simply use the property of ergodicity. We have encountered it several times already.

For an ergodic random process, the time average equals the statistical average, with probability 1:

$$
\lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^T x(t),dt = E[X(t)].
$$

Sampling and Quantization

When a band-limited random process is sampled, each sample value is a random variable.

Within a duration $T$ and a bandwidth $F$, we can obtain $2FT$ sample values.

After quantization, or discretization, these values form a discrete random sequence.

Sampling + quantization transforms a random waveform source into a continuous source and then into a discrete source.

Generally speaking, a signal in a communication system is treated directly as a stationary random process.

Section 2: Information Measures for Continuous and Waveform Sources

(1) Definition of a Continuous Source

The output of the source is a continuous random variable, which can be described by a probability density function $p(x)$:

$$
\int_R p(x),dx = 1.
$$

Its one-dimensional, joint, and conditional probability density functions are denoted by $p(x)$, $p(x,y)$, and $p(y|x)$, respectively.

(2) Entropy of a Continuous Source: Differential Entropy

The definition is extended from discrete entropy:

$$
h(X) = -\int_R p(x)\log_2 p(x),dx
$$

Several points deserve attention:

Although its form resembles that of discrete entropy, the concept is different.

$h(X)$ may be negative—for example, when the interval of a uniform distribution is smaller than 1—so it does not possess the non-negativity of discrete entropy.

It represents “relative uncertainty” and is also called relative entropy here.

Still, it is better to call it differential entropy. Since it does not possess the absolute character of discrete-source entropy, it cannot represent either the average uncertainty of the source or the amount of information produced by a continuous source. It is only a relative value and need not be non-negative.

(3) Differential Entropy of Common Continuous Sources

  • Uniform distribution:

    $$
    p(x)=\frac{1}{b-a},\quad h(X)=\log_2(b-a)
    $$

  • Gaussian distribution:

$$
p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp!\left(-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

$$
h(X)=\frac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)
$$

The entropy of a Gaussian source depends only on the variance $\sigma^2$, rather than on the mean $m$. This is consistent with the specific meaning of the differential entropy of a continuous signal: its entropy is relative.

(4) Differential Entropy and Entropy Rate of a Waveform Source

  • For a random process {x(t)} limited to bandwidth F and duration T, its samples form an $N=2FT$-dimensional random vector:

$$
h(X_1, X_2, …, X_N)
$$

  • The entropy rate, or amount of information per unit time, is:

    $$
    h_t(X) = 2F \log_2(b-a)\ \text{比特/秒}
    $$

Wait—is this formula restricted to the uniform distribution?

Section 3: Properties of Continuous-Source Entropy and the Maximum Differential-Entropy Theorem

1. Properties

  1. Additivity:

    $h(X,Y)=h(X)+h(Y|X)=h(Y)+h(X|Y)$

  2. Concavity and the extremal property:

    Entropy is a concave function of $p(x)$.

  3. Possibility of negative values:

    Because it is “relative entropy,” it does not possess the non-negativity of discrete entropy.

  4. Transformation property:

    If $Y=f(X)$ is a one-to-one transformation,

    $$
    h(Y)=h(X)+E[\log_2 |J(\frac{X}{Y})|]
    $$

    where $J$ is the Jacobian determinant.

2. The Maximum Differential-Entropy Theorem

(1) Under a Peak-Power Constraint

If the source output $X\in[a,b]$, then:

  • Entropy is maximized when $p(x)$ is a uniform distribution.

  • The maximum entropy is:

    $$
    h_{\max} = \log_2(b-a)
    $$

(2) Under an Average-Power Constraint

If $E[X^2]=P$, meaning that the average power of the output signal is constrained to P, then:

  • Entropy is maximized when $X$ follows a Gaussian distribution.

  • The maximum entropy is:
    $$
    h_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e P)
    $$

In other words:

Uniform distribution ↔ constrained peak value

Gaussian distribution ↔ constrained average power

The two cases correspond respectively to two common kinds of noise: uniform noise and Gaussian noise.

This is consistent with the idea that noise is a maximally uncertain random process, while the greatest amount of information can be obtained only from events with the greatest uncertainty.

Section 4: Transformation of Continuous-Source Entropy

After the source $X$ undergoes the linear transformation $Y=kX+a$:

For the probability density function,

$$p_Y(y_1 y_2 \cdots y_N) = p_X(x_1 x_2 \cdots x_N) \left| J\left( \frac{X}{Y} \right) \right|$$

For differential entropy,

$$
h(Y)=h(X)+\log_2|k|
$$

Section 5: Categories of Continuous and Waveform Channels

1. Definitions

  • Waveform channel: both the input and output are random processes, ${x(t)}$ and ${y(t)}$.

  • Continuous channel: the input and output are continuous random variables $X,Y$.

After sampling under restrictions of duration and bandwidth,

$$
{x(t)} \to (X_1, X_2, …, X_N), \quad {y(t)} \to (Y_1, Y_2, …, Y_N)
$$

a waveform channel can therefore be represented by an equivalent multidimensional continuous channel.

2. Classification by Statistical Properties

  • Memoryless channel: the output depends only on the corresponding input.

  • Channel with memory: the output depends on inputs at multiple moments.

3. Classification by Noise Type

Type Description Characteristic
Gaussian channel Noise follows a Gaussian distribution Commonly used model
White-noise channel Noise power spectral density is constant $N_0/2$
Coloured-noise channel Power spectral density varies with frequency Has memory
Additive channel $y=x+n$ Noise and signal are independent
Multiplicative channel $y=x·n$ Common in wireless communication

In an additive channel, the conditional entropy is:

$$
h(Y|X)=h(n)
$$

It is called noise entropy.

Section 6: Information Transmission Rates of Continuous and Waveform Channels

A Single-Symbol Continuous Channel

The average mutual information is:

$$
I(X;Y)=h(Y)-h(Y|X)=h(Y)-h(n)
$$

A Multidimensional Continuous Channel

$$
I(X;Y)=\int\int p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)},dxdy
$$

The information transmission rate per degree of freedom is:

$$
R = \frac{1}{N}I(X;Y)
$$

A Waveform Channel

The information transmission rate per unit time is:

$$
R_t = \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{T} I({x(t)}, {y(t)})
$$

Properties of Average Mutual Information

  1. Non-negativity: $I(X;Y)\ge 0$

  2. Symmetry: $I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)$

  3. Concavity: it is concave in the input distribution.

  4. Information cannot increase: the data processing theorem.

    If $X \to Y \to Z$, then $I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$

  5. Invariance under one-to-one transformations: such a transformation leaves $I(X;Y)$ unchanged.

Section 7: Channel Capacity of Continuous and Waveform Channels

Channel capacity is defined as the greatest achievable average mutual information:

$$
C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)
$$

A General Continuous Channel

$$
C = \max_{p(x)} [h(Y)-h(Y|X)]
$$

An Additive Continuous Channel

The noise is independent of the input, and $h(Y|X)=h(n)$; therefore,

$$
C = \max_{p(x)} [h(Y)-h(n)]
$$

An Additive Gaussian Channel: The AWGN Channel

The noise $n\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$.

The input power is constrained by $E[X^2]=P$.

The output is therefore $Y=X+n$.

According to the maximum-entropy theorem, the optimal input distribution is also Gaussian.

  • Output entropy:

    $$
    h(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e (P+N))
    $$

  • Noise entropy:

$$
h(n)=\frac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e N)
$$

  • Capacity:

    $$
    C = \frac{1}{2}\log_2\left(1+\frac{P}{N}\right)\quad \text{(比特/自由度)}
    $$

  • Capacity per unit time:

    $$
    C_t = F\log_2\left(1+\frac{P}{N}\right)\ \text{(比特/秒)}
    $$

This is the celebrated Shannon channel-capacity formula. It shows that, when average power is constrained and additive white Gaussian noise is present, the information transmission rate has an upper limit.

Section 8: The Significance of Shannon’s Formula

  • It reveals the fundamental restrictions that bandwidth and the signal-to-noise ratio place on the information transmission rate.

  • It provides a theoretical limit for the design of communication systems.

  • It identifies two ways to raise the communication rate:

    • increasing the bandwidth;

    • improving the signal-to-noise ratio.

  • It forms a foundation of modern digital communication and coding theory.
