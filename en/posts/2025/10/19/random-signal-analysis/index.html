Chapter 3 — Random Signal Analysis

Random signal analysis is actually the subject of another course. Here it is treated only briefly, almost as an outline; the teacher did not expect us to understand all of it at this stage either.

The Concept of a Random Process

What Is a Random Process?

A random signal in communication is unpredictable and therefore random, yet it still follows statistical regularities. We consequently need the mathematical concept of a random process to describe it. It is not deterministic.

A random process is the collection of the time evolutions produced by different outcomes of a random experiment. Put differently, it is a time function whose value at each moment remains uncertain and is therefore a random variable.

A sample function is a concrete realisation of a random process, whereas the sample space consists of all such realisations. Taken together, all the sample functions and their statistical characteristics constitute the random process.

A random process is essentially an extension of the concept of a random variable, with a time variable added.

General Representation of a Random Process

Like many other methods of analysis, this one moves from a single fragment to several fragments, and from the characteristics at one point to the relationships among multiple points.

Distribution Functions and Probability Densities

One-Dimensional Case

A random process can be characterised by a distribution function and a probability density. The underlying idea is as follows.

At any given moment, a random process is a random variable. If we take a slice at that moment and regard the various probabilities within the slice as an independent distribution, we can obtain the one-dimensional distribution function and probability density at that time.

The formulas are:

$$F_1(x_1, t_1) = P\left{ \xi(t_1) \leq x_1 \right}$$

$$f_1(x_1, t_1) = \frac{\partial F_1(x_1, t_1)}{\partial x_1}$$

Note that $t_1$ is treated as a fixed value in both the one-dimensional distribution function and the probability density.

n-Dimensional Case

A one-dimensional distribution or density function describes only the statistical characteristics of a random process at a single instant. This idea can then be extended to the probability distribution and density at any fixed set of n moments. The larger n becomes, the fuller—and more complicated—the description of the statistical characteristics will be.

$$F_1(x_1, x_2; t_1,t_2) = P\left{ \xi(t_1) \leq x_1,\xi(t_2) \leq x_2 \right}$$

I seem to remember that second-order quantities were usually enough in the random signal analysis course. For those of us working on communication, such precision is generally unnecessary; the numerical characteristics of a random process are sufficient.

Numerical Characteristics of a Random Process

Mean or Mathematical Expectation

For a random process, the mean is calculated at each time slice. It is a function of time, but it should be noted that this function is deterministic. I will omit the detailed calculation.

Variance

Omitted.

Both the mean and variance depend only on the one-dimensional probability density function, and they reflect the characteristics of the random process at each moment.

Correlation Function

The correlation function describes an internal relationship within a random process—namely, the relationship between two random variables belonging to the process at any two moments.

Autocorrelation Function

$$R(t_1, t_2) = E\left[ \xi(t_1)\xi(t_2) \right] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x_1 x_2 f_2(x_1, x_2; t_1, t_2) dx_1 dx_2$$

It reflects the correlation between the values of a random process at different times—admittedly a rather vague sentence—and is often used to analyse periodicity or stationarity in time-series signals. The following offers one way to understand where the formula comes from.

Consider what may happen when two sets of data are multiplied element by element and the products are then added.

If the signs in both sets are random, will the average product not approach zero? Once some relationship exists between their signs, however, the result of multiplying and adding will deviate to one side or the other of zero.

We can therefore match this multiply-and-add operation to the analysis of a random process. Is it not, in effect, an average of products? In this way, it can reveal the correlation between the values of the process at different moments.

Autocovariance Function

$$B(t_1, t_2) = E\left{ \left[ \xi(t_1) - a(t_1) \right] \left[ \xi(t_2) - a(t_2) \right] \right} = R(t_1, t_2) - a(t_1)a(t_2)$$

By the same reasoning, the autocovariance function improves upon the autocorrelation function by adopting a centred form, thereby preventing an offset from distorting the result.

Correlation Coefficient

$$
\rho(t_1, t_2) = \frac{B(t_1, t_2)}{\sigma(t_1)\sigma(t_2)}$$

If $\rho(t_1, t_2) = 0$, then $\xi(t_1)$ and $\xi(t_2)$ are said to be uncorrelated.

Examples of Random Processes

Stationary Random Processes

Definition

If the statistical characteristics of a random process are independent of the starting point in time and always obey the same statistical pattern, the process is strictly stationary.

Equivalently, every finite-dimensional probability density must be independent of the starting time. The one-dimensional probability density is independent of time t, while the two-dimensional distribution depends only on the time interval $\tau$. The criterion is shown below:

The mean of a strictly stationary process is constant, and its autocorrelation function depends only on the time interval.

This condition is, of course, relatively strict and rarely needed in ordinary work. We generally use wide-sense stationarity instead.

A wide-sense stationary process need only satisfy two requirements: its mean is constant, and its autocorrelation function depends solely on the time interval.

Property

Ergodicity

Take any one realisation of a random process, and over time it will pass through all the possibilities contained in that process.

To put it more vividly, imagine turning on a switch in an unchanging environment and never turning it off. The random signal it produces over time contains all the information.

This is convenient because temporal characteristics can replace statistical ones. We no longer need to know the distribution function of the random process: whenever an average is required, a time average may replace a statistical average.

Note that an ergodic process must be stationary, but a stationary process is not necessarily ergodic. The two conditions are not mutually necessary and sufficient.

Gaussian Random Processes

Definition

If every n-dimensional distribution of a random process is normal, the process is called a Gaussian random process, or a normal process.

Important Properties

  • The n-dimensional distribution of a Gaussian process is completely determined by the mathematical expectations and variances of the n random variables $x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n$, together with the normalised covariance function between every pair of variables;

  • A wide-sense stationary Gaussian process is also strict-sense stationary;

  • If the values of a Gaussian process at different moments are uncorrelated, they are also statistically independent:

$$f_n(x_1, x_2, \cdots, x_n; t_1, t_2, \cdots, t_n) = f(x_1, t_1) f(x_2, t_2) \cdots f(x_n, t_n) $$

  • For Gaussian variables, being uncorrelated is equivalent to being independent;

  • A Gaussian process remains Gaussian after a linear transformation, or after passing through a linear system, although its numerical characteristics change;

  • The algebraic sum of several Gaussian processes remains Gaussian, although its numerical characteristics change:

Suppose

$$X_1 \sim N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) ， X_2 \sim N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) $$

Then

$$aX_1 + bX_2 \sim N(a\mu_1 + b\mu_2, a^2\sigma_1^2 + b^2\sigma_2^2) $$

Narrowband Random Processes

The output produced when a random process passes through a narrowband system centred at the frequency $f_c$ is a narrowband process.

A narrowband system is one whose passband width satisfies $\Delta f<<f_c$, where $f_c$ is far from zero frequency.

In practice, most communication systems are narrowband. Any signal or noise that passes through a narrowband system must itself be narrowband; if the signal or noise is also random, it is called a narrowband random process.

If the waveform of one realisation is observed on an oscilloscope, it appears as shown in Figure 2-4: a sinusoid whose frequency is approximately $f_c$ and whose envelope and phase vary slowly and randomly. The slow variation matters; otherwise, the bandwidth of the baseband signal would not remain narrow.

A Sinusoid Plus a Narrowband Gaussian Process

This is an application of the preceding sections.

One common method of modulation uses a sinusoid—the simplest waveform—as the carrier. The receiver then accepts a composite waveform containing the modulated sinusoidal signal and narrowband Gaussian noise.

Gaussian White Noise and Band-Limited White Noise

Definition

White noise is noise whose power spectral density is constant at every frequency. Thus:

Two-sided power spectral density: $P_n(f) = \frac{n_0}{2} (-\infty < f < +\infty )$

One-sided power spectral density: $P_n(f) = n_0 (0 < f < +\infty)$

Here, $n_0$ is a positive constant.

Autocorrelation function of white noise: Taking the inverse Fourier transform of the two-sided power spectral density gives the correlation function:

$$ R(\tau) = \frac{n_0}{2} \delta(\tau)$$
