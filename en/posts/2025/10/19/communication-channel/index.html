Chapter 4 — Communication Channels

Definition of a Channel

A channel is a path through which a signal travels, and it is indispensable in a communication system.

Channel in the Narrow Sense

In the narrow sense, a channel refers to the transmission medium—the physical path between the transmitter and receiver.

  • Guided media: optical fibre, twisted-pair cable, and coaxial cable
  • Unguided media: radio and infrared waves

Channel in the Broad Sense

In the broad sense, a channel is an equivalent model that incorporates some of the other equipment in a communication system:

  • A modulation channel extends from the output of the modulator to the input of the demodulator, and concerns modulated signals.
  • A coding channel extends from the output of the encoder to the input of the decoder, and concerns digital sequences.

Modulation-Channel Model

A modulation channel transmits modulated signals, so the main concerns are signal distortion and the influence of noise. Because the instantaneous value of a modulated signal varies continuously, it is also called a continuous channel—or, in some contexts, simply a channel.

Coding-Channel Model

A coding channel, also called a digital channel, contains and depends on a modulation channel. Its interference is reflected in bit errors, and its principal performance measure is therefore the bit error rate.

Depending on whether the transition probability between successive symbols is dependent on earlier symbols, coding channels may be further divided into channels with memory and channels without memory.

Performance Parameters

The reliability of a communication system is mainly affected by two factors: noise and imperfections in the transmission characteristics of the channel.

Characteristics of Constant-Parameter Channels

Examples

Twisted-Pair Cable

Twisted-pair cable can be used for both analogue and data transmission. Its bandwidth depends on the thickness of the cable and the transmission distance.

It is divided into unshielded twisted pair (UTP) and shielded twisted pair (STP); the latter uses foil shielding to reduce interference.

Coaxial Cable

Baseband coaxial cable: 50 $\Omega$. It transmits baseband digital signals for data communication and is widely used in local area networks.
10 Mb/s, 1 km

Broadband coaxial cable: 75 $\Omega$. It is mainly used in CATV systems to carry frequency-division-multiplexed analogue signals.
6 MHz/300–400 MHz, 100 km

Optical Fibre

An optical fibre is a double-layer cylindrical communication medium that carries information through light waves. It offers high transmission rates, a broad bandwidth, low loss, strong resistance to interference, good confidentiality, and low weight. Yet optical modulators and photodetectors are required at the two ends, making the system relatively complicated. Connections can also be troublesome, and the fibre itself is fragile—step on it once and it is gone.

Three wavelength windows are commonly used:

0.85 um: attenuation is high, so the transmission rate and distance are limited, but the equipment is inexpensive;

1.30 um: attenuation is low; without dispersion compensation or power amplification, the maximum range is 40 km in the worst case;

1.55 um: attenuation is low; without dispersion compensation or power amplification, the maximum range is 80 km in the worst case.

Terrestrial Line-of-Sight Radio Relay

Transmission between two ground stations, point to point.

Distance: 50–100 km; frequency: 2G–40 GHz

Its performance depends on the weather and operating frequency.

Applications include long-distance transmission of speech and television signals, as well as LAN interconnection between buildings.

Characteristics of Time-Varying Channels

Examples

Short-Wave Ionospheric-Reflection Channel

All right, this one certainly varies more than the previous examples.

The ionosphere is an atmospheric layer 60–600 km above the ground. Counting upward from the surface, the atmosphere can be divided into the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, ionosphere, and exosphere. The refractive index of the medium decreases with altitude, so the path of a radio wave gradually bends until total reflection occurs at a certain height.

Short wave: 3–30 MHz (100–10 m)

This mode of transmission has no blind zone and requires only simple, flexible equipment. Antarctic scientific expeditions, for example, use it as a means of communication. The ionosphere reflects the frequency bands used by ordinary radio broadcasting, but it cannot reflect radio waves with shorter wavelengths. Television happens to use shorter radio waves, which is why a television set cannot receive programmes from a distant station by this mechanism.

Transmission reliability is poor, and the operating frequency must be changed frequently.

Applications include short-wave broadcasting, emergency communication, disaster-response communication, and military communication.

The most basic reason short-wave communication attracts users is that long-distance communication requires only modest transmission power and moderately priced equipment. It also possesses a relay system—the ionosphere—that is not easily “destroyed”. Practice has shown that short-wave communication modernised with contemporary technology can provide high-quality, reasonably priced links with high availability. It nevertheless has several shortcomings, mainly in the following two respects:

  1. It cannot overcome the weaknesses inherent in the high-frequency medium itself;

  2. It cannot resist interception or various forms of deliberate interference.

Tropospheric-Scatter Channel

The troposphere is the part of the atmosphere below an altitude of roughly 10–12 km.

It is used with very-high-frequency and microwave bands.

Its advantages include strong survivability, good confidentiality, high mobility, and an ability to adapt to complex terrain.

It supports point-to-point communication in military applications, between islands and the mainland, and in emergency or disaster-relief communication.

Other Examples

Other examples include short-wave ionospheric reflection, VHF meteor-trail scattering, VHF and microwave tropospheric scattering, short-wave ionospheric scattering, and beyond-line-of-sight VHF diffraction.

Characteristics

With respect to signal transmission, the transmission medium has the dominant influence, whereas the influence of converters is secondary and may even be neglected. The main task is therefore to study the common characteristics of transmission media and their effects on signals.

Characteristics of Time-Varying Channels

In multipath transmission, the attenuation and delay of every path vary randomly. Both the attenuation imposed on the signal and the transmission delay consequently change over time.

In mathematical language:

We can see that R(X) may be regarded as a narrowband random process: multipath propagation turns a deterministic single-frequency carrier into a narrowband signal whose envelope and phase are both modulated—that is, a fading signal.

From the time-domain perspective, the result is multipath delay spread; from the frequency-domain perspective, it is frequency dispersion or broadening.

Statistical Characteristics

When $n$ is sufficiently large, $X_c(t)$ and $X_s(t)$ are stationary Gaussian processes, so $R(t)$ is a narrowband Gaussian process.

The one-dimensional distribution of $V(t)$ is a Rayleigh distribution, corresponding to Rayleigh fading:

$$f(V) = \frac{V}{\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\frac{V^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad V \geq 0, \sigma > 0$$

The one-dimensional distribution of $\varphi(t)$ is a uniform distribution:

$$f(\varphi) = \frac{1}{2\pi}, \quad -\pi \leq \varphi \leq \pi $$

Frequency-Selective Fading

This is the fading of certain components in a signal spectrum. After the frequency components of a signal arrive over multiple paths, their phases differ. Components with the same phase reinforce one another when their vectors are added, whereas components with opposite phases cancel. The amplitudes of different frequency components in the received signal therefore change, much as if the signal had passed through a selectively attenuating network; hence the term frequency-selective fading.

Coherence Bandwidth and Types of Fading

Frequency-selective fading in multipath propagation depends on the relative delay difference, represented by the maximum multipath delay difference $\tau_m$.

The coherence bandwidth is defined as:

$$\Delta f = \frac{1}{\tau_m} $$

When the signal bandwidth satisfies $B > \Delta f$, the waveform $R(t)$ is distorted, producing frequency-selective fading;

When the signal bandwidth satisfies $B << \Delta f$, the strength of $R(t)$ varies over time and depends on $\tau$, producing flat fading, or general fading;

To avoid obvious frequency-selective fading, $B$ should be smaller than $\Delta f$. Techniques such as diversity reception may also be adopted to stabilise the signal.

Improvement

Diversity

Concept

Diversity reception is an important technique developed in mobile communication to overcome various forms of fading and improve system performance.

Its basic principle is to separate the received multipath signal into several uncorrelated, or independent, signals, and then combine their energy according to a particular rule so as to maximise the useful signal energy at the receiver.

For a digital system, the objective is to minimise the bit error rate at the receiver.
For an analogue system, the objective is to improve the signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver.

Forms of Diversity

Time diversity uses time interleaving and channel coding to provide replicas of a signal in the time domain.

Frequency diversity transmits symbols on different carrier frequencies, thereby providing replicas in the frequency domain.

Space diversity is realised through multiple antennas.

Combining

Selection combining chooses the branch with the highest signal-to-noise ratio; naturally, the result will tend towards a limiting value at a later stage.

Equal-gain combining adds the signals from all branches directly with the same gain.

Maximum-ratio combining sets the gain of each branch in proportion to the signal-to-noise ratio of that branch.
