Unknown

The brush suddenly descends. Its point barely touches the paper, yet, not knowing where the stroke should go, it can only spread into an enormous stain of ink, like a question waiting for someone to answer.

Why have I taken up the brush again to write sentences like these?

Perhaps this apparently useless act of writing, this murmur witnessed by nobody, comes closer to genuine usefulness than anything else.

Philosophers often say that meaning can be assigned to everything: birdsong becomes a courtship song, and the opening flower a promise of pollination. Yet ask one further question—what is the meaning of reproduction? Every species will eventually become extinct, and the universe will ultimately fall silent. Before that vast and intricate emptiness, “meaning” begins to look painfully thin. Reproduction ends in extinction; accumulation ends in dispersal. What difference is there between measuring the universe’s boundless void with a system of meaning constructed by the brief human mind and illuminating an eternal night with the light of a firefly? Every solid interpretation is a castle made of sand before everlasting silence. The tide arrives, and no trace remains.

To grieve over boundless space and the passage of time, however, is not necessarily to lead oneself toward the abyss of nihilism. On the contrary, once the emptiness of life has been admitted, those moments formerly dismissed as “wasted time” acquire a peculiar radiance. Like the stain spreading across the paper, the useless branches of ink extending freely in every direction are precisely what give it a form that cannot be repeated.

If the tower of meaning is ultimately a castle in the air, might the experience of complete absorption and the warmth of a genuine connection with another person constitute a form of existence that is both more real and more durable?

Perhaps I took up the brush only to write a sentence like that.

Have not the past two months at the radio station been just such a real existence? In Room 407, meaning no longer requires a grand defence. Planning, publicity, and organisation become pure because these trivial tasks possess no larger purpose. I am not trying to erect an eternal monument in the hope that someone at the station will remember me many years from now. I am simply greedy for the warmth of existence at that moment—for proof that, here and now, I am standing in Room 407, still breathing, my heart still beating.

We usually decide whether something should be done by calculating advantage and disadvantage. Yet some actions need not be justified by utility, just as some relationships need not be weighed for profit and loss. At this moment, you are looking at my words and I am imagining your existence. Though we have never met, in some sense we are already keeping one another company. That warmth need not be converted into money. It is, in itself, the purest consolation.

This hunger for existence may also explain why, even when I no longer need extra points for postgraduate recommendation, I remain willing to spend additional effort. Once stripped of their utilitarian clothing, the days and nights spent conducting experiments and writing documents become small acts of resistance against emptiness. I still remember the evening when the patent draft was finally complete. After dinner with my teacher, I rode back from Paradise Walk. Night had deepened; the wind was strong; shadows of roadside trees moved against the ground. It felt as though a tiny candle had at last been lit on an endless wasteland. I knew the wind might extinguish it at any moment, but for that instant its light revealed my own hand and barely illuminated an inch of the road ahead. It proved that I was still alive in the pitch-black night.

Or perhaps I wrote these sentences merely so that I could take up the brush.

As a wild goose leaves a trace in passing, I too would like to leave a small claw mark in the increasingly crowded field of object detection—perhaps a line bearing my name in some obscure journal. Yet research into fine-grained detection resembles a forest drowned in fog. Every hypothesis opens another fork in the path; the farther one travels, the more lost one becomes. Every paper warns of the effort demanded by datasets, computing power, model design, and repeated experiment.

I ask and ask. The empty valley returns only an echo, and even that eventually fades.

More troubling still are the delay and estrangement within me. Ambition wants to build a city, while action collapses like running sand. I long for an imagined self who advances without pause, yet move steadily in the opposite direction.

Even in hardship, remain resolute; never let the ambition of the clouds fall.

Seen differently, is this anxiety not itself evidence of existence? It arises from a desire not yet extinguished, rather than from the stillness of surrender. It resembles a trapped animal struggling, not dead water reflecting the sky. The circling and collision may not be futile; perhaps they are the necessary hesitation and gathering of strength through which life searches for its actual form. What matters is not whether I reach some shore selected in advance, but whether, during this hesitation, I can still feel the candle within. It flickers, but it has not gone out.

Heaven and earth are the inn of all things; time is the traveller of a hundred generations. If all of us are passing through, then every step—fast or slow, singing or weeping—is already a response to nothingness. Perhaps true victory lies not in achievement, but in knowing that life is like a dream and nevertheless cherishing this good night enough to carry a candle through it.

Once, I too was the naive person holding the rough ruler called “meaning” and attempting to measure the limitless sky. Now I would rather be an ordinary traveller who settles, for a while, within time that can actually be touched. Whether before a microphone at the radio station, beside an instrument in the laboratory, or at a desk buried under books, the important thing is no longer the result of the measurement. It is the walking itself.

Along a long journey, one occasionally encounters an unexpected view. Beside the road, for example, a graceful reed may stand in silence, its mere existence making the surrounding wasteland feel less absolute. Like the distant beloved in an ancient poem, it remains on the opposite bank. I neither need nor wish to struggle upstream and approach it, thereby destroying the perfection of the distance. The act of looking is already a gift. Like a breath of clean wind crossing the wasteland, it does not disperse the long night, but gives the candle’s movement an accompaniment. That wordless brightness, that distant reflection, reminds me that the world still contains beauty worth contemplating in silence. This alone is the gentlest confirmation of existence.

There is no preventing flowers from falling; the returning swallows seem familiar. Every frustration, heaviness, and unattainable thing resembles a season of blossom already gone. The marks of ink, the laughter carried by radio waves, the solitary lamp burning late in the laboratory, and even a distant blessing offered to that reed are the swallows returning with undiminished warmth.

Perhaps I have written these words not to obtain an answer, but simply to confirm that I am passing through—truly, contradictorily, and passionately. I am still alive.

Do not go gentle into that good night. Rage, rage against the dying of the light.

I took up the brush for no other reason. I merely wrote these sentences.
