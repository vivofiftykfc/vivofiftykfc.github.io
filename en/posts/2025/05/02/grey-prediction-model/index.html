Grey Prediction Models: GM(n,m)

Grey Prediction Models: GM(n,m)

I did not need this model in the mathematical-modeling contest for which these notes were prepared. The detailed derivation below was generated by AI and has not yet been checked by me.

I. Background

1. The shortage of data

Mathematical-modeling problems often provide very few observations, perhaps four to ten periods, or incomplete information. Examples include:

  • a city’s GDP over the past five years;
  • three years of experimental records for a new material; and
  • the number of infections during the first seven days of an epidemic.

Conventional forecasting methods, including regression and time-series analysis, generally require substantial amounts of data. Grey prediction attempts to identify regularities when both the sample and the available information are limited.

2. Grey system theory

  • White system: information is completely known, as in a circuit system.
  • Grey system: some information is known and some remains unknown, as in an economic system.
  • Black system: information is entirely unknown, as with dark matter in the universe.

Grey prediction is designed precisely for a grey system. Its central idea is to process disordered raw data into a sequence with stronger regularity and then construct a differential equation to forecast its future development.

3. Suitable situations

The following view comes from Qingfeng’s mathematical-modeling course.

The choice of a forecasting model is open to reasonable disagreement:

  1. The observations should be nonnegative annual data. Monthly or quarterly data should instead be handled with the time-series models discussed in the preceding lesson.
  2. The data should pass a test for a quasi-exponential pattern. Apart from the first two periods, the smoothness ratio should be below 0.5 in at least 90 percent of the remaining periods.
  3. The series should be short and not strongly associated with other data. Ten periods or fewer are suitable, although a series can also be too short—for example, only three observations. A conventional time-series model is generally preferable when many periods are available.

II. The GM(1,1) Model in Detail

1. Data preprocessing

(1) Converting the direction of indicators

See the corresponding benefit-type transformations in the notes on evaluation models.

(2) Testing the data

  • Level-ratio test: determine whether the observations are suitable for a GM model.
    $$
    \sigma(k) = \frac{x^{(0)}(k)}{x^{(0)}(k-1)} \quad (k=2,3,…,n)
    $$
    Every ratio must lie within $(e^{-\frac{2}{n+1}}, e^{\frac{2}{n+1}})$.

2. Constructing the model in four steps

Step 1: First-order accumulated generating operation (1-AGO)

Purpose: transform fluctuating data into a monotonically increasing sequence.
$$
x^{(1)}(k) = \sum_{i=1}^k x^{(0)}(i) \quad (k=1,2,…,n)
$$

Example:

Sequence 1 2 3 4 5
Original data 2.87 3.28 3.34 3.39 3.68
Accumulated data 2.87 6.15 9.49 12.88 16.56

Step 2: Generate the adjacent-mean sequence Z

Purpose: construct a discrete approximation to the differential equation.
$$
z^{(1)}(k) = 0.5x^{(1)}(k) + 0.5x^{(1)}(k-1) \quad (k=2,3,…,n)
$$

Continuing the example:

| Z sequence | - | (2.87+6.15)/2=4.51 | (6.15+9.49)/2=7.82 | … |

Step 3: Establish the grey differential equation

$$
x^{(0)}(k) + a z^{(1)}(k) = b
$$
The parameters are estimated by least squares:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a \
b
\end{bmatrix} = (B^T B)^{-1} B^T Y
$$
where:
$$
B = \begin{bmatrix}
-z^{(1)}(2) & 1 \
-z^{(1)}(3) & 1 \
\vdots & \vdots \
-z^{(1)}(n) & 1
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
Y = \begin{bmatrix}
x^{(0)}(2) \
x^{(0)}(3) \
\vdots \
x^{(0)}(n)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Step 4: Solve the whitenization equation

The corresponding continuous differential equation is:
$$
\frac{dx^{(1)}}{dt} + a x^{(1)} = b
$$
Its solution is:
$$
\hat{x}^{(1)}(t) = \left(x^{(0)}(1) - \frac{b}{a}\right)e^{-a(t-1)} + \frac{b}{a}
$$

Restore the predicted values:
$$
\hat{x}^{(0)}(k) = \hat{x}^{(1)}(k) - \hat{x}^{(1)}(k-1)
$$

3. Testing the model

(1) Test for a quasi-exponential pattern—the crucial test

  • Smoothness ratio: (\rho(k) = \frac{x^{(0)}(k)}{x^{(1)}(k-1)})
  • Passing criterion:
    • the first two periods may be treated more leniently;
    • (\rho(k)<0.5) in the final 80 percent of the periods.

(2) Test of the development coefficient

Development coefficient (-a) Suitable forecasting horizon
<0.3 Medium or long term, 5–10 periods
0.3–0.5 Short term, 1–3 periods
>0.5 Residual correction or another model is required

(3) Residual test

$$
\text{平均相对误差} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=2}^n \left|\frac{x^{(0)}(k)-\hat{x}^{(0)}(k)}{x^{(0)}(k)}\right|
$$

  • Below 10 percent: high precision
  • 10–20 percent: acceptable
  • Above 20 percent: the model is not credible

IV. Common Pitfalls

1. Fewer than four periods of data

  • Possible response: average the results of three GM variants—the conventional, new-information, and metabolic models.

2. Negative predicted values

  • Cause: the data were not transformed into a suitable positive direction.
  • Solution: examine the preprocessing stage and translate the observations with (x’ = x - \min(x) + 1).

3. Distorted long-term forecasts

  • Response: use rolling forecasts, updating the model after each newly predicted period.

4. Improving precision

  • Residual correction: construct another GM(1,1) model for the forecast residuals.
  • Combined forecasting: form a weighted combination with ARIMA, exponential smoothing, or another model.
