Multiple Linear Regression

Multiple Linear Regression

Regression analysis is a central instrument of data analysis. By examining the relation between independent variables X and a dependent variable Y, it seeks to reveal how Y is produced and to make predictions. Multiple linear regression explains an outcome through a linear combination of variables and quantifies the weight of each factor. It is therefore widely applied in economics, management, engineering, and related fields.

I. Purposes

  1. Identify important variables: select the values of X that are significantly related to Y, through stepwise regression, for example.
  2. Determine direction: establish whether X has a positive or negative influence on Y.
  3. Estimate weights: use regression coefficients to represent the relative importance of the variables.

II. Construction

$$
Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \beta_2 X_{2i} + \cdots + \beta_k X_{ki} + \mu_i
$$
Here, $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta_j$ is a partial regression coefficient, and $\mu_i$ is the disturbance term.

  • Holding the other variables constant, $\beta_j$ means that each one-unit increase in $X_j$ is associated with an average change in $Y$ of $\beta_j$ units.
  • The data may first be standardized with a $Z-Score$. The larger the absolute regression coefficient, the more substantial the contribution.

III. Model Testing and Optimisation

1. Goodness of fit

  • Coefficient of determination $R^2$: represents the proportion of variation explained by the model. Since $R^2 \in [0,1]$, a higher value generally indicates a better fit.
  • Adjusted $R^2$: guards against an artificially high fit caused merely by adding too many independent variables.

2. Dealing with heteroskedasticity

  • Symptom: the residuals spread out as the predicted value increases.
  • Diagnosis: the Breusch–Pagan test or White test.
  • Solution: use OLS + robust standard errors or generalized least squares (GLS).

3. Multicollinearity

  • Symptoms: coefficient signs appear abnormal, or individual $t$ tests are insignificant even though $R^2$ is high.
  • Diagnosis: the variance inflation factor, with VIF > 10 commonly treated as severe.
  • Solutions: remove highly correlated variables, enlarge the sample, or reduce dimensionality through principal component analysis.

V. Applications and Extensions

1. Limitations

  • Endogeneity: omitted variables or measurement errors may bias the coefficients. More precisely, a model is exogenous when the error term $μ$ is unrelated to all independent variables. If they are related, endogeneity is present, and the estimates of the regression coefficients are neither unbiased nor consistent. Instrumental variables must then be introduced.
  • Overfitting: when there are too many variables, the model should be improved through cross-validation or regularisation such as LASSO or Ridge.
  • Misrepresenting nonlinear relations: variable transformations, such as logarithms, or interaction terms may be introduced.

2. Possible extensions

  • Combine regression with principal component analysis: reduce the dimensionality of high-dimensional data before regression so as to address multicollinearity.

  • Mixed-effects models: handle individual and temporal effects in panel data.

  • Combine it with machine learning: use a random forest to select important variables and then construct the regression model.

  • Take logarithms: a logarithmic form expresses the elasticity of the original dependent variable with respect to an explanatory variable—that is, percentage rather than numerical change. Its advantages include: (1) weakening heteroskedasticity; (2) allowing a variable that is not normally distributed to become approximately normal after transformation; and (3) giving the model a form with economic meaning.

  • Standardize in advance for easier interpretation: the data may first be processed with a $Z-Score$, after which a larger absolute coefficient indicates a greater contribution.

  • Introduce dummy variables:

    Qualitative variables such as sex and region must be converted into 0–1 dummy variables. For example:
    $$
    \text{工资} = \beta_0 + \delta_0 \text{Female}_i + \beta_1 \text{教育年限}_i + \mu_i
    $$
    $\delta_0$ represents the wage difference between women and men at the same level of education, while $ \text{Female}_i$ uses 0 and 1 to distinguish the two groups.

Case 1: Factors influencing the number of product reviews in e-commerce

  • Objective: examine how price, brand, place of origin, and other factors affect sales.
  • Method: standardized regression coefficients and dummy variables.
  • Conclusion: price has a significant negative effect, while imported brands enjoy a clear premium.

Case 2: Population prediction

  • Data: historical population and economic indicators.
  • Model: introduce a Logistic curve to fit the growth trend.
