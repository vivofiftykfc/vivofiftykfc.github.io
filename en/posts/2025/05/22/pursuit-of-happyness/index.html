The Pursuit of Happyness

(Piano version of A Father’s Way)

【Plot Summary】

Hello, listeners. Welcome to today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow on the Voice of BIT. I am your host, xxx.

Today, I would like to talk about an old film released in 2006: The Pursuit of Happyness, starring Will Smith.

The story takes place in San Francisco in 1981. Its protagonist, Chris Gardner, is a medical-equipment salesman who has invested all his savings in a portable bone-density scanner. The device is slightly more accurate than an X-ray machine, but it costs twice as much. The doctors’ response is simple: unnecessary and too expensive.

He therefore has no choice but to carry the forty-pound machine around every day, facing rejection again and again while continuing to try. His wife works two jobs to support the family, but eventually she can bear the situation no longer and leaves. Three months behind on the rent, he is evicted by his landlord. He and his son sleep in a toilet at a subway station, wait in long queues outside shelters, and even sell blood.

During that period, his only opportunity is an unpaid internship as a stockbroker. He works at the firm during the day and rushes to a shelter at night, while using whatever spare time remains to sell the scanners and obtain money for daily life.

In the end, he is hired and later establishes his own company. To be honest, when I watched this film in the past, I regarded it as a standard inspirational story: a poor man reverses his fate; a father’s love is as firm as a mountain; relentless effort finally carries him to Wall Street. Watching it now, however, I am most deeply impressed not by that “success,” but by the hands with which he covers his son’s ears in the toilet, and by the moment when he walks out of the office building, his eyes red, and applauds himself.

【Artistic Techniques】

The film’s director, Gabriele Muccino, is Italian and previously directed The Last Kiss. He brings traces of Italian neorealism into this film. Handheld cameras are used extensively, giving the images a slightly unsteady quality. The film was shot directly on the streets of San Francisco, and Will Smith was even asked to interact with homeless people there.

The young actor who plays the son is Jaden Smith, Will Smith’s own son. Many of the looks and physical gestures between them are not so much “acted” as genuine responses between father and son. This approach to casting is typical of the neorealist desire to “move life onto the screen”: the suffering in the film appears less like a performance and more like a record of reality.

【Thematic Analysis】

Why has this film remained popular for so long? I think it is because the film does not beautify suffering. It is not a bowl of chicken soup telling us that “effort always leads to success.” Instead, it presents the living and truthful story of a man who repeatedly fails, repeatedly loses what he has, and is repeatedly forced back into his original condition by life.

Chris loses a scanner, a shoe, and a place to live; he nearly loses custody of his son. To reach the queue outside the shelter, he runs wildly through the streets with the machine in his arms. To meet his targets, he gives up his breaks and spends two more hours on the telephone than everyone else each day.

Even the film’s title contains a hidden point: the word Happyness in The Pursuit of Happyness is misspelled; the correct spelling is Happiness. The error is said to come from a piece of graffiti mentioned in the real protagonist’s memoir. It seems to suggest that the “happiness” we pursue is itself imperfect. It is not a standard answer, but the trail of footprints left behind as we make mistakes and continue moving forward.

In other words, happiness is not the sound of a knock for which we wait. It is the road we see behind us when, after running until we are breathless, we finally turn around.

In the eyes of others, Chris is also a man of almost complete “disrepute”: a “deadbeat” who cannot pay his rent, an “incompetent husband” abandoned by his wife, a “madman” who chases swindlers and scanner thieves through the streets, and a person “not worth knowing well.”

Nor does the film give him a single person who is truly his “friend.” He does not even have the chance to ruin a friendship, because he does not qualify to be judged by friends in the first place.

Yet at the moment when his reputation is at its worst, he does not stop and beg anyone to change their view of him. Silently and stubbornly, he simply continues knocking on that door and fulfilling his responsibility as a father.

Perhaps we do not need to be declared “innocent” before we are allowed to live. Perhaps, when the entire world attaches the label “unworthy” to us, the only thing left for us to do is stop begging the world for a judgement that calls us “worthy.” We must move forward with all the charges laid upon us and all those labels marked “unworthy.” Only after we have travelled far enough can the labels be blown away by the wind.

【Reading the Details】

There is a shot in the film which many people have discussed, but which is worth mentioning again.

Chris cannot afford a hotel, so he hides with his son in a subway-station toilet. He holds the boy in his arms and braces the door with his foot while someone outside pounds upon it. He reaches out, covers his son’s ears, and cries without making a sound.

He could have broken down. He could have put his son aside, opened the door, and argued with the person outside, or simply walked out by himself. But he does not. He covers his son’s ears not because the boy might hear the abuse outside, but because he does not want his son to hear his fear. He must still fulfil a father’s responsibility.

At that moment, no one will applaud him. No one will tell him, “You are a good father.” No one even knows. He does all this simply because he is a father. In that toilet, he decides to reserve the last of his dignity for his son.

Many of our decisions are made in order to be seen. We lose weight so that we will look good in photographs, revise so that we will not be mocked, and apologise so that we will be forgiven. But what if no one sees? If nobody sympathises with you after you are rejected; if your friends remain distant even after you have corrected your mistakes and begun again; if you cry alone in a toilet while the people outside merely demand that you hurry up and leave—will you still make the right choice?

Chris’s answer is yes, because some things are not done for the sake of being seen. Even without an audience, Chris still chooses to be a person with dignity.

I also particularly like one detail at the end of the film. After Chris is hired, he leaves the building and enters the crowd. He does not laugh or shout, and his eyes are red. Then he raises his hands and slowly applauds himself a few times.

Many people interpret this as “he has finally succeeded.” Yet if we look more closely, those few claps are not powerful; they are even slightly hesitant. This is not the cheer of a victor, but a man who has just run out of a shelter, with the smell of the toilet still on his body, confirming that he remains alive. He is not applauding fate, God, or any audience. He is applauding himself.

At this moment, he still remembers that night in the subway-station toilet. He remembers the self who braced the door with one foot, covered his son’s ears, and wept in silence. Those memories will not disappear because of one letter of employment. Success is not an eraser. It cannot rub suffering away. It merely gives you the right to carry that suffering and continue along the road.

The real person upon whom the film is based later established a company and sold it for several million dollars. But all that happened later. In the subway-station toilet, he did not know what came later. He knew only that he had to survive that night.

In the film, Chris tells his son, “Don’t ever let somebody tell you you can’t do something. Not even me.” At the time, he himself is at the moment when he seems least capable of doing anything. Yet he does not stop. We do not know what he said to himself on the hardest nights. We know only that the next morning he put on that shirt, tied his tie, and continued towards the next door.

Thank you for listening to today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow on the Voice of BIT. See you next time.

What follows is the revised version—the broadcasting script.

(Piano version of A Father’s Way)

【Introduction】

Hello, listeners. Welcome to today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow on the Voice of BIT. I am your host, Zhang Hengwei. And I am your host, Hao Mingkai.

Today, we would like to talk about an old film released in 2006: The Pursuit of Happyness, starring Will Smith.

In most inspirational films, “success” tends to be a clearly defined destination: a reversal, a comeback, applause, and control over one’s own life.

What makes The Pursuit of Happyness truly distinctive, however, is that it spends almost its entire running time telling us that the normal condition of life is not success, but continuous failure and hope whose fulfilment is repeatedly delayed. The film is not explaining “how to succeed.” It is asking a heavier question: when a person remains unable to see any certain future, in what manner can he continue living?

【Plot Summary】

The film is adapted from a true story. Its protagonist, Chris Gardner, was originally a medical-equipment salesman. He invested all his savings in bone-density scanners, but weak market demand quickly drove him into financial collapse. His wife left, the rent went unpaid, and his life fell out of control. With his son, he struggled to survive in the cracks of the city of San Francisco.

At the hardest point, he received the opportunity to serve as an intern at a securities firm. The internship, however, was unpaid, and only one person out of twenty would be allowed to remain. During the day, therefore, he wore a shirt and tie and worked at the firm. At night, he queued with his son for admission to a shelter. In his spare time, he still had to sell the remaining scanners to obtain money for daily expenses.

In the end, he succeeded in staying. Later, he founded his own company and truly changed his fate.

Yet what I remember most clearly is not “success” itself, but the moments of embarrassment and distress: Chris running through the crowd with a machine in his arms; the silent tears he sheds as he covers his son’s ears in the toilet; and the sight of him leaving the office building, his eyes red, slowly applauding himself.

【Thematic Analysis】

What makes the film genuinely distinctive is that it treats failure not as an occasional event, but as a normal condition of life.

Chris repeatedly loses things, arrives late, and faces rejection. He must hurry to sell the machines, hurry to collect his son, and hurry to join the queue outside the shelter. The film contains hardly any truly relaxed moments. Yet he does not suffer frequent emotional explosions. He simply continues in silence: he continues selling the scanners, attending interviews, queuing for the shelter, and caring for his child. He seldom explains himself and rarely seeks understanding from other people.

This silence is not the result of a defect in his character. It is closer to a practical strategy for survival: when a person has neither the resources nor the opportunity to prove his worth, his only choice is not to waste himself upon explanations.

His condition does not follow the conventional inspirational logic of “persisting because one can see hope.” It is closer to the logic of reality: even when hope cannot be seen, life must still continue.

As Romain Rolland wrote, “There is only one heroism in the world: to see the world as it is and to love it.” People do not necessarily persist because they “see hope.” They persist because, even when they cannot see hope, they still wish to keep life from collapsing.

【Artistic Techniques】

The director of The Pursuit of Happyness is the Italian filmmaker Gabriele Muccino. The film displays a distinctly realist visual style. It makes extensive use of handheld photography, for example. The camera is not stable; when the characters run, the image sometimes shakes visibly. In the sequences involving the pursuit of a bus or taxi in particular, the camera remains close to Chris as he moves, and the audience is given almost no “safe distance.” The images are always urgent, crowded, and even slightly disordered.

This method does not turn running into a passionate moment of inspiration. Instead, it produces the hurried sensation of being driven by life. The characters are not running towards success; they are trying to avoid being left behind by reality altogether.

The film contains few of the bright and uplifting shots common in traditional inspirational cinema. Many scenes take place in narrow spaces such as rented rooms, subway stations, and shelters, and even the light is generally grey. This sense of reality is close to the spirit of Italian neorealist film: it places little emphasis upon “legends” and directs attention instead towards the struggles of ordinary people under the pressure of life.

Jaden Smith, who plays the son, is Will Smith’s son in real life. Many of the subtle looks and movements exchanged between father and son possess an effortless authenticity, reducing the traces of “performance” throughout the film.

【Reading the Details】

There is one shot in the film that many people remember.

Unable to afford a hotel, Chris can only hide with his son in a subway-station toilet for the night. He braces the door with his foot while someone outside knocks repeatedly. Holding the boy, he gently covers his ears, but cannot prevent his own tears.

The sequence contains almost no music. The narrow and enclosed space, the uninterrupted knocking, and Chris’s consistently restrained movements give the scene an almost suffocating reality. The director does not allow the character to collapse into loud sobbing, and the camera remains restrained as well. Yet the greater the restraint, the more strongly the audience feels a despair from which there is nowhere left to retreat.

More importantly, even in this condition, he still does one thing: he lets the child fall asleep first and refuses to collapse himself. At its centre, the scene is not really about “suffering.” It is about the attempt to preserve a minimum degree of order and dignity within a life that has fallen completely out of order. What moves us may not be how brightly a person shines after success, but his willingness to remain dignified when no one is watching.

【Reading the Ending】

At the end of the film, Chris is formally employed. He walks out of the office building and across the street. There is no exaggerated celebration; he merely applauds himself softly. The gesture is not grand and is even rather restrained, but its significance lies precisely in the idea of “confirmation”: it confirms that he has truly passed through a period from which there was no way back.

In the film, Chris once tells his son, “Don’t ever let somebody tell you you can’t do something. Not even me.” The sentence appears full of strength when heard by itself, but within the context of the entire film it is closer to a reminder he has struggled to recover from failure. Perhaps what truly matters is not the certainty that “success will come,” but whether, amid every uncertainty and failure, a person remains willing to move forward.

【Conclusion】

Thank you for listening to today’s edition of The Age of Light and Shadow on the Voice of BIT. See you next time.
