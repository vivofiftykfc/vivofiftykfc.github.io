Outlier Detection

I have once again encountered the problem of dealing with outliers. This post therefore gathers the relevant algorithms in one place, as comprehensively as I can manage.

1. Statistical Methods

(1) The modified Z-score

  • Suitable situation: univariate data whose distribution departs slightly from normality or contains a small number of disturbing outliers.
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD).
    2. Apply the modified Z-score: $ \text{修正Z} = \frac{|x - \text{Median}|}{\text{MAD} \times 1.4826} $.
    3. Choose a threshold, such as an absolute modified Z-score greater than 3, and regard values beyond it as outliers.
  • Underlying idea: replace the mean and standard deviation with the median and MAD, thereby reducing the influence of outliers upon the statistics themselves.
  • Limitation: the method is restricted to a single variable and may be insensitive to extremely skewed distributions.
  • Advantage: it is less easily distorted than the conventional Z-score and is suitable for contaminated data.

(2) The Grubbs test

  • Suitable situation: strictly normal univariate data in which a single outlier must be detected.
  • Procedure:
    1. Assume normality and calculate the Grubbs statistic for the maximum or minimum observation.
    2. Compare it with a critical-value table determined by the sample size and significance level α, and decide whether to reject the null hypothesis that no outlier exists.
  • Underlying idea: identify one outlier through hypothesis testing.
  • Limitation: strict normality is required, and observations can be tested only one at a time.
  • Advantage: its statistical meaning is explicit, which makes it suitable for scientific research.

2. Quantile-Based Methods

(1) The IQR or box-plot method

  • Suitable situation: univariate data following any distribution, especially a skewed one.
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate Q1, the 25th percentile; Q3, the 75th percentile; and IQR = Q3 − Q1.
    2. Define the limits as $ \text{下限} = Q1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}, \quad \text{上限} = Q3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR} $.
  • Underlying idea: use quantiles to define the “normal range” of the data, with relatively low sensitivity to the tails.
  • Limitation: anomalies that arise only from combinations of variables may be missed, and the multiplier of 1.5 is fixed.
  • Advantage: the method is simple, efficient, and free of distributional assumptions.

(2) Percentile trimming

  • Suitable situation: the empirical distribution is unknown and extreme values must be removed quickly, as in financial risk control.
  • Procedure: directly remove the lowest α percent and the highest β percent of the observations—for example, α=1% and β=1%.
  • Underlying idea: impose a hard cut at empirical quantiles, relying on domain knowledge to select the thresholds.
  • Limitation: normal data may be deleted, and the thresholds are subjective.
  • Advantage: it is fast and direct in noisy settings. Basically, it amounts to using a rather blunt instrument.

3. Distance-Based Methods

(1) Mahalanobis distance

  • Suitable situation: multivariate data whose variables are correlated, such as an unusual combination of height and weight.
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate the covariance matrix Σ and its inverse.
    2. Calculate the Mahalanobis distance of every observation: $ D = \sqrt{(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)} $.
    3. Set a threshold from the χ² distribution, using a confidence level such as 95 percent.
  • Underlying idea: use a generalized distance that accounts for correlations among variables and identifies outliers in a multidimensional space.
  • Limitation: the method depends on estimating the covariance matrix and becomes unstable in high dimensions; the sample size should exceed the number of dimensions.
  • Advantage: it can capture multivariate anomalies comprehensively.

(2) K-nearest-neighbour distance

  • Suitable situation: unstructured data or the detection of local anomalies in images, text, and similar material.
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate the average distance from each point to its K nearest neighbours.
    2. Treat a point whose distance exceeds the threshold as an outlier.
  • Underlying idea: detect anomalies through differences in local density; a point in a sparse region is more likely to be abnormal.
  • Limitation: computation is expensive, and K must be selected.
  • Advantage: no distributional assumption is required, making the method suitable for complicated data structures.

4. Density-Based Methods

(1) Local outlier factor (LOF)

  • Suitable situation: multivariate data in which samples with unusual local density must be detected—for example, an isolated point within an otherwise dense region.
  • Procedure:
    1. Estimate the local density of each point from the observations in its K-neighbourhood.
    2. The LOF value is the average ratio between the density around the point and those around its neighbours. A value far greater than 1 indicates an outlier.
  • Underlying idea: judge abnormality through relative density, addressing local patterns that a global method may overlook.
  • Limitation: computation is expensive, and the result is sensitive to K.
  • Advantage: local anomalies in non-uniformly distributed data can be identified.

5. Machine-Learning Methods

(1) Isolation Forest

  • Suitable situation: high-dimensional or unstructured data, including logs and time series.
  • Procedure:
    1. Construct several random trees and isolate samples by repeatedly dividing the feature space at random.
    2. Outliers tend to be isolated through shorter paths, from which an anomaly score is calculated.
  • Underlying idea: an outlier is easier to “isolate” in feature space.
  • Limitation: the method may be insensitive to local anomalies and requires parameters such as the number and depth of trees.
  • Advantage: linear time complexity makes it suitable for large datasets.

(2) DBSCAN clustering

  • Suitable situation: density-based clustering in which noise points are automatically identified as outliers.
  • Procedure:
    1. Set the neighbourhood radius ε and the minimum number of observations, min_samples.
    2. Mark as noise, and therefore as outliers, points that cannot form part of a core cluster.
  • Underlying idea: points in low-density regions are treated as abnormal.
  • Limitation: the result is sensitive to ε and min_samples, and performance is poor in high dimensions.
  • Advantage: the proportion of outliers need not be specified in advance, and the method suits data with an unknown distribution.

Summary: Points to Note and Selecting a Method

Points to note

  1. Do not delete blindly: an outlier may be the very signal that matters, such as fraud or equipment failure. Domain judgment remains necessary.
  2. Analyse variables jointly: a univariate method may miss an anomalous combination, such as normal height accompanied by extremely low weight.
  3. Adjust thresholds dynamically: when streaming data or distribution drift is present, update the statistics through a rolling procedure such as a moving-window IQR.
  4. Verify visually: box plots, scatter-plot matrices, and t-SNE projections can assist the decision.

Guide to selecting a method

Feature of the problem Recommended methods
One variable, approximately normal Modified Z-score and the three-sigma rule
One variable, skewed distribution IQR and percentile trimming
Several correlated variables Mahalanobis distance and LOF
High-dimensional data with a demand for efficiency Isolation Forest
Local anomaly detection LOF and K-NN distance
No distributional assumption and a need for automation DBSCAN and percentile trimming

Central principles

  • Prefer simplicity: begin with IQR or a Z-score for univariate data.
  • Match the domain: percentile trimming may suit financial risk control, while LOF or Isolation Forest may suit industrial inspection.
  • Cross-check: combine statistical tests, such as a normality test, with feedback from the actual application when tuning thresholds.
  • Simple settings: IQR for resistance to extreme observations; Z-score under normality.
  • Complicated settings: LOF for local anomalies; Isolation Forest for high-dimensional data.
  • Use domain knowledge: select the method according to the actual context.
  • Validate the strategy: combine statistical testing with subject knowledge.

Of course, the decision in a real modeling contest still depends on which problem you chose, how much time remains, and how much of that time can reasonably be spent on outliers.
