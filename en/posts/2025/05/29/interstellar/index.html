Interstellar

Let me once again thank my partner for completing this programme with me. What follows is the part I wrote.

Introduction and Background

Interstellar is an original science-fiction adventure directed by Christopher Nolan. It was released on 5 November 2014 and has a Douban rating of 9.4.

Nolan explained his original impulse in roughly these terms: when he was a child, becoming an astronaut represented the noblest dream and the highest ambition. He missed the determination to keep exploring and to advance into the furthest reaches of the galaxy, and hoped that the film might begin a new age of outward expansion. Interstellar is both a tribute to the classic space film and a continuation of that childhood dream. As usual, Nolan favoured physical sets and IMAX film over complete dependence on computer-generated imagery, seeking a stronger sense of reality and immersion.

To keep the scientific theories in the film credible, Nolan invited Nobel laureate and theoretical physicist Kip Thorne to serve as scientific adviser, giving the wormholes, black holes, and other astrophysical phenomena a rigorous foundation. Thorne’s team used thousands of computers to solve simulations based on Einstein’s equations. The resulting image of the black hole also contributed positively to astrophysical research. Thorne later examined the principles behind the film one by one in his book The Science of Interstellar.

Reflections After Watching

The narrative structure of Interstellar is carefully designed and can broadly be understood as containing one visible line and one concealed line. In the visible story, Cooper saves his daughter and the human race by transmitting information from within the tesseract. Gravity is the medium of the message, while love is the motive for his action. Beneath it lies another story: humanity in higher dimensions saves its present-day ancestors by creating the wormhole and the tesseract.

The film’s foreshadowing is as rigorous and precise as a formula. At the beginning, books fall mysteriously from the study shelves, Murph speaks of a “ghost,” and patterns in the dust conceal the coordinates of the NASA base. What appear to be supernatural signs are all recovered at the end, when Cooper moves the world lines along the surface of the tesseract and Murph deciphers the trembling hands of the watch. The circuit closes. Nolan makes the five-dimensional bookshelf both a carrier of paternal love and a time capsule buried by humanity for its own rescue. In the spirit of Murphy’s law, every accident drawn together by gravity eventually becomes a necessary point of starlight.

The film also deserves attention for its handling of detail and its secondary characters. Consider Cooper’s two children. Murph is the pioneer who looks toward the stars and eventually receives the admiration of millions. His son, by contrast, is the guardian whose feet remain in the soil—indispensable, yet easily forgotten. Even his name is the thoroughly ordinary Tom. When their father leaves him the promise of “protecting the home,” this sole ordinary figure in the story carries humanity upon his back. His trust in his father is unconditional. Cooper asks only that he look after the family, and Tom remains with the old house and the fields for decades. Because of that persistence, Murph can return home and still find the watch her father gave her many years before, then decode his hidden message. The father’s spacesuit and the son’s plough shine with equal force: one brings data out of the black hole, while the other preserves the bridge across which that information can pass. Humanity cannot be saved by a handful of extraordinary heroes alone. It also depends upon countless ordinary people who stay at their posts, as Tom does.

After the film ended, I found myself returning to another question: why are people so fascinated by science fiction? Perhaps it is because such works act as prisms, refracting the deepest contradiction in the human soul. We are products of immense clouds of stardust, children of reason; yet among that dust, we are also the only beings moved to tears by starlight. In The Three-Body Problem, the Singer flattens the Solar System with a dual-vector foil and turns it into a flowing galaxy that might have come from Van Gogh. Cold reason and burning compassion meet for an instant in a brilliant final song. In Foundation, people living beneath the structure of psychohistory compose, through their own lives, an epic of faith and betrayal, power and feeling. Science fiction is never merely a fantasy about the future. It examines the reason and emotion within us. Reflected in its mirror, somewhere between the operation of a formula and the radiance of human character, we can finally see ourselves more clearly.

I think Interstellar is so powerful precisely because it sees that contradiction and writes it as an epic beneath the stars. Cooper floats against the surface of the tesseract and moves the world lines of the books with his fingers; suddenly, the cold formulas and the data from a black-hole singularity acquire bodily warmth. Every ray from the accretion disk becomes an echo of longing that crosses dimensions. Through this story, Nolan and Thorne show that science and technology, our strongest weapons in confronting nature, can also carve the shape of love when they turn toward humanity itself. As Carl Sagan wrote in Cosmos, the nitrogen in our DNA, the calcium in our teeth, and the iron in our blood were all formed from ancient stars. Technology and human feeling have never been opposites. Like the wave–particle duality of light, they are projections of the same substance in different dimensions.

As the credits rise into darkness with the roar of the organ, we beyond the screen remain trapped in the predicament called “humanity.” We are dust among the stars, yet also the only gods within that dust who attempt to understand it. Nolan tells us that when ripples of gravity spread through spacetime, only love can turn noise into a messenger; when molecules wander without direction through the void, only fingers interlocked beside a fire can define the temperature of existence. Perhaps the universe does not care how humanity opens a path for its species through a wormhole, or how line after line of equations and symbols is written upon a blackboard. Yet this romance, futile in the eyes of the universe, is exactly what enables us to cultivate an oasis called civilization in the desert of endless entropy. Let us raise our heads and look into the night. Every ancient patch of light seems to whisper: calculate the light-years, laugh, weep, and become the gentlest stardust in the universe.

Do not go gentle into that good night.

Old age should burn and rave at close of day.

Rage, rage against the dying of the light.
