Buddhism

I

Over the past two days, I have learned something about Buddhism. It is rather interesting.

My initial understanding was that Buddhism mainly does several things: it explains how suffering arises, discusses why the world and human beings are constantly changing, and then proposes a practical method of training through discipline, concentration, and wisdom. It develops a complete body of theory around a fairly clear question, and actual methods genuinely follow the theory. This makes it appear more complete than many of the philosophies I have encountered so far.

That description is broadly correct, although certain parts remain insufficiently precise.

Before discussing Buddhism, I can first explain how I now regard religion. I remain an atheist and have no interest in converting to any religion. Yet religion does not merely answer whether a god exists. Problems now handled separately by science, law, philosophy, and psychology were often treated together by ancient religions. Religion explained where the world came from and why people suffer; it prescribed what people ought to do; and through ritual, discipline, and community, it brought these ideas into everyday life.

This obviously creates serious problems. When one body of theory possesses final authority over facts, morality, and social order at the same time, it can easily become something that cannot be questioned. Nevertheless, religion has seriously considered questions that modern people still cannot avoid: why should a person restrain desire, how should suffering be endured, why does an individual owe responsibility to others, and why should people continue living when they know they will die?

Christianity is particularly concerned with sin, love, forgiveness, and salvation. Islam strongly emphasises that humanity is not the centre of the world and that power and wealth are accompanied by responsibility. The starting point of Buddhism is more direct:

If people clearly desire happiness, why do they continue to suffer?

II

The most basic structure of Buddhism is the Four Noble Truths: suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path.

In simple terms, Buddhism first recognises that human life contains suffering. It then explains that suffering does not arise from nothing, but has particular causes. Since suffering depends upon causes, it may cease when those causes cease. Finally, Buddhism proposes a practical path of cultivation.

Buddhism therefore does not merely explain suffering. Its purpose is to identify the process through which suffering arises and then find a way to stop that process.

Nor should “suffering” be understood simply as pain, poverty, and heartbreak. A person who obtains something desired will naturally feel happy; Buddhism does not claim that all such happiness is false. The problem is that everything obtained under particular conditions will change. Examination results change, bodies change, other people’s opinions of me change, and relationships change as well.

These things are not wrong in themselves, nor is the desire for them necessarily wrong. What most readily creates suffering is the hope that they will remain exactly as they are forever and continue to prove one’s worth.

A good examination result, for instance, is certainly worth celebrating. But if that result is not merely an outcome and must also prove that I am intelligent, excellent, and valuable, then the next time I cannot solve a problem, I will no longer face only one problem. Being liked also makes me happy. Yet if her affection must continually prove that I deserve love, one moment of coldness may be interpreted as a rejection of my complete person.

Thus, when Buddhism speaks of impermanence, it does not merely mean that everything eventually disappears. More importantly, everything changes within conditions and cannot provide a permanent guarantee.

Dependent origination makes the same point. A thing exists in its present form through many conditions. A tree depends upon a seed, soil, water, sunlight, and time. A person likewise depends upon a body, memories, family, language, social relationships, and previous experience. There is not first a completely independent person who then happens to be influenced by the outside world. Those supposedly external conditions already participate in forming the person.

At first, I wanted to understand dependent origination as a Markov process, but later discovered that the comparison cannot be made directly. The Markov property requires the current state to contain all effective information through which the past influences the future, whereas Buddhist ideas of karma, habitual tendencies, and seeds emphasise precisely that what the past leaves behind continues to affect the present. Describing dependent origination as a dynamic causal relationship is acceptable. Declaring that it simply is Markovian would amount to forcing an unfamiliar idea into the ancestry of a familiar concept.

III

When impermanence and dependent origination are applied to the human being, they lead to non-self.

Non-self does not claim that human beings do not exist, nor that, since there is no self, nothing matters. A person clearly possesses a body, memories, and a relatively stable character, and remains responsible for previous actions. Buddhism denies something else: whether, behind these continuously changing bodies, feelings, memories, and forms of consciousness, there really exists a completely independent and permanently unchanging self.

At least in experience, we cannot find it.

The person I was in childhood, the person I was while in love, and the person now writing this article certainly possess continuity, but they are not entirely the same thing. The body changes, memories are reinterpreted, and standards of judgement shift. I may continue using the name “I” to summarise them, but the constancy of the name does not prove that an identical core exists from beginning to end.

This closely resembles my earlier ideas about cognition.

Human knowledge of the world is always limited. You can know the name and appearance of a bird and study its habits, but the bird itself will never grow inside your mind. What you know is the understanding you have constructed from it, not the whole bird.

The same is true when people understand themselves. “I am intelligent,” “I am a failure,” and “I am a person without the ability to act” all arise from part of one’s experience, mixed with the emotions of the moment, other people’s judgements, and personal interpretation. They may identify something real, but they can never equal the complete person.

What Buddhism calls attachment to appearances probably means treating such partial and temporary knowledge as the fixed essence of a thing. One failure becomes “I am a failure”; one person who hurt me becomes “his whole being is evil”; not being chosen becomes “I do not deserve love.” The event occurred only once, but after interpretation it is written into a self that appears never to change.

Attachment to self is not simply selfishness. It is closer to the impulse to drag every event immediately back to “what does this mean for me?” Can this prove me? Is this denying me? What might I lose? What do other people think of me now? Attachment constructs a perfect image of the self, but this is only the self in one’s own eyes and differs from the real person. If, in order to protect what I think I should be, I ignore what I actually am and overlook the condition of non-self, I become distressed, twisted, awkward, and selfish.

People certainly need to protect themselves and make judgements about their own lives. The problem is that, if every event can ultimately become only material for self-evaluation, a person will find it difficult to see the things and people immediately before him.

IV

At this point, the process through which suffering arises becomes relatively clear.

First, an event occurs and the body produces a feeling. A person instinctively wishes to hold onto pleasant feelings and drive unpleasant ones away. Next comes an interpretation, and finally the interpretation becomes a judgement about the self.

Suppose I answer one problem incorrectly. The fact is merely that I could not solve this problem. My body first becomes irritated. Then my mind moves to the postgraduate entrance examination, to previous failures, and to whether I will accomplish nothing in the future. A few minutes later, the problem remains the same, but I have almost finished writing the conclusion to my entire life.

Buddhism does not demand that the discomfort in the first step never appear. The body reacts, and loss genuinely hurts. It attempts to deal with what is continuously added afterwards: I must escape this feeling immediately; I must prove that I am not a failure; this failure means I will never succeed.

Karma may also be understood from this point.

It need not first be imagined as an account book somewhere in the universe, according to which a bad act must eventually receive punishment. A more direct understanding is that every intentional act produces consequences and also changes which actions will be easier to perform in the future.

If anxiety makes me avoid something once, I feel slightly better in the short term and become more likely to avoid it when anxiety returns. If, whenever I cannot solve a problem, I begin studying a new field, then learning new things can gradually become an exceptionally sophisticated form of escape. Conversely, if an emotion arises and I do not immediately act upon it, pausing will become slightly easier next time.

A person does not first acquire a fixed character and then act. Much of what we call character is the result left by repeated actions.

V

This brings us to discipline, concentration, and wisdom.

Discipline does not merely suppress desire or produce a list of everything forbidden. It first asks people to reduce actions which they already know will make themselves and others more confused. Do not attack someone immediately when emotion rises; do not revise your complete life plan as soon as anxiety appears; when you want to escape, do not casually open a new topic. These actions seem ordinary, but they at least prevent suffering from continuing to spread through behaviour.

Concentration mainly concerns attention. The mind produces all kinds of thoughts, and that cannot be avoided. The problem is that we often treat every thought as a command that must be executed. While solving a problem, I suddenly think of my ex-girlfriend, then of what will happen if I fail the examination, then of the relationship between Buddhism and Yogācāra, then of whether I should start a new project—and I run after each thought.

Concentration does not mean the permanent absence of distracting thoughts. It means noticing that you have wandered and returning.

Wisdom, likewise, is not the ability to recite impermanence, dependent origination, and non-self. Genuine wisdom should occur when an event arises: what am I feeling now, what are the facts, and what interpretation have I added? Knowing that “failure” is only a judgement does not mean that, when I answer a problem incorrectly, I can really see how that judgement is appearing.

The most interesting aspect of discipline, concentration, and wisdom is therefore their refusal to believe that understanding alone changes a person.

This point is well suited to criticising me. I have written many articles about action, cognition, and self-analysis. Much of what they say is perfectly sound, and then the things I cannot do remain undone. Knowing a sentence is not the same as genuine change in emotion, attention, and action.

Buddhism does not merely give me another, more complete explanation. It says that explanation is not enough; I must practise.

VI

What, then, do these ideas actually teach me?

The first point is that they change the question I used to ask.

I often wondered how to prove my value. Results, ability, relationships, and work could all become evidence. If the proof was insufficient, I continued adding to it. Eventually, even the construction of a philosophical system could become one more element in proving that I really was capable of thought.

Buddhism does not provide a stronger proof. It asks instead: why must there be one permanently valid proof at all?

The postgraduate entrance examination, a project, and affection for another person may all be important, and failure has real consequences. Yet none of them alone can state everything I am, and no result can permanently guarantee that I will never become uncertain again.

The second point is that Buddhism makes me reconsider the statement that “a model is not reality.”

Previously, my principal concern was how much error human knowledge contains, how more information may be obtained, and how misjudgement may be reduced. Buddhism adds another layer: even when people know that their understanding is limited, they still hold tightly to a particular judgement because it maintains a stable self.

The question is therefore not merely whether the model is accurate, but why I need so badly for it to be true.

The third point is its emphasis upon action.

Discipline, concentration, and wisdom show that human understanding does not exist only in language. A person may say that others should be respected, yet become angry immediately upon rejection. He may know that failing to solve one problem does not equal failure in life, yet still escape at once when facing it. Such physical and behavioural responses come closer to what he presently and genuinely believes.

The final point, and the one I most need to guard against, is that failure can become an attachment to self, but so can growth, ideals, helping other people, and the pursuit of truth.

Do I want to accomplish something because the thing itself is worthwhile, or because I need a stronger version of myself to prove that my present life has not been wasted? In many cases both are present, and separating them completely would be unrealistic.

Buddhism offers no formula for the judgement. It merely reminds me not to assume that a nobler-sounding goal must therefore contain no attachment.

VII

I will not become a Buddhist because of this.

I can accept the explanations that impermanence, dependent origination, non-self, and discipline-concentration-wisdom offer for real experience, and I am willing to use them in examining my life. Rebirth, karmic retribution, and nirvana as a religious cosmology, however, require additional beliefs and arguments. Non-self does not automatically prove that a person continues in some form after death; nor does the resemblance between dependent origination and certain concepts in modern science turn it directly into a scientific conclusion.

Nor do I like reducing Buddhism to “take things lightly and accept whatever fate brings.” Letting go does not mean wanting nothing, still less remaining calm and doing nothing when confronted with injustice and pain. Outcomes still matter, responsibility remains, and harm suffered by another person does not automatically disappear because all things are empty.

For me, the most useful part of Buddhism is probably that it makes me ask less often “how does this prove me?” and look more closely at what the thing itself is; that it makes me less likely to turn a momentary feeling into a permanent conclusion, or to assume that explaining a principle means I have already changed.

Of course, this article has explained the principles once again.

Whether I can practise them, I do not know.

For now, solve the problems.
