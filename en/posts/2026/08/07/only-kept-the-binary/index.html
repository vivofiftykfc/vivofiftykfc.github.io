Everyone, Feel

I

I was cleaning my computer today and opened the directory where WeChat stores its files. It has grown beyond 120 gigabytes.

I worked through material from the end of 2024 to the present: an application for an undergraduate innovation project, analogue-electronics experiments, OpenAMP, drones, mathematical modeling, plans for the radio station, an MCM paper, a venue application for the Audio-Visual Evening, and the recent annotation tool. Between them lay piles of reimbursement documents, software archives, and material received from people I no longer remember. A single document often appeared in more than ten versions, which is, to be fair, exactly what one expects from a WeChat folder. Then I suddenly realized that I had once done a great many things.

In my memory, the innovation project had been reduced to its final acceptance; the integrated-circuit contest to a second prize in North China; the radio station to the title of station assistant, a collection of work, and a failed relationship; the MCM to an Honorable Mention. How the work was actually done, who did it with me, what we said along the way, and which absurd problems appeared—without the files, none of this would have occurred to me.

Only when I saw the drone documents did I remember that none of us knew how to obtain the equipment. We were debating whether to buy it ourselves, until we finally asked the teacher and learned that it could be borrowed. Reimbursement appears today as a single amount of money. At the time, the explanatory attachment alone passed through more than twenty versions.

All of that was eventually summarized in one sentence: I once worked on a drone-related project and never wanted to touch one again.

Some experiences did not even leave a sentence behind. Walking around Singapore with classmates, eating with a teacher, staying up late to write Tetris with GPT, recording A Cruel Angel’s Thesis, and attending my first interview must all have carried specific feelings. Yet if I do not deliberately return to them, “must have” is the most I can say. The feelings themselves are gradually fading.

Perhaps the problem is not that nothing happened during my three university years. The problem is that I did not preserve the raw data. That makes time appear to have passed impossibly quickly, leaving me with the thought that I have done nothing. In reality, I have done quite a lot.

Taking the thought further, my present self is continuous with the person I was a year ago, but we are not entirely the same person. His physical condition, attention, relationships, and circumstances have passed away. What I can now reach consists of several files, some indistinct memories, and a large body of explanations formed afterwards. The present judgment I make of him is therefore another lossy model. The problem is that I never seemed to admit the loss.

II

I am accustomed to compressing an experience into an indicator.

A competition becomes its prize; a project becomes its final acceptance; a course becomes its score; a relationship becomes a theory about intimacy and the self. Once the compression is complete, my mind concludes that the important part has been extracted and that the details no longer need to be preserved. This procedure is not wholly useless. Learning itself requires compression. After a project, one knows how to divide requirements, write a report, and adjust equipment, so the next similar task becomes easier. If every action required us to replay the whole of our original experience, we would probably accomplish nothing. The trouble is that after compressing it, I deleted the source file.

I retained only “what I learned,” not “how I lived through it.” Later, the ability came to seem natural, while the indicator itself no longer looked impressive. The entire experience was compressed once more into “I did not really do anything.”

Consider a second prize in North China. I was probably pleased when I first received it. Later, after seeing national prizes and people far more capable than I was, I looked back and decided that a regional second prize was nothing remarkable. The award had not changed; its frame of reference had. In the end, I used the standards of the present to overwrite the whole experience of the past and reached the conclusion that the period had produced no result. The conclusion may not be entirely wrong. How much it distorts, heaven knows.

Those particular afternoons within the project—several people in a laboratory studying an instrument nobody knew how to use, the first successful stream of data, the boredom of writing the report, the desire to swear while completing reimbursement—none of them is contained in the phrase “second prize.” Yet when I later judged the past, those three Chinese characters were all that remained in my hand. What judgment could possibly follow? None, except the feeling that I had done nothing.

III

This is also connected to the way I have long evaluated myself: I am always concerned with what kind of person I am at the present moment.

Am I capable? Have I grown? Am I stronger than before? Can what I am doing prove who I am? Will I become someone more impressive? It appears that I am attending to the present. In reality, I am continuously stepping aside to observe it and turning it into a report about myself. The thing itself readily disappears, leaving only the question, “What does this mean about me?”

This is the same problem of attachment to the self that I discussed in the essay on Buddhism. Whatever happens must immediately be brought back to me: can it prove me, does it deny me, and what sort of person am I after all?

I once thought that I was living in the present. Now, perhaps I was not. To live in the present must at least require the thing before one’s eyes to appear. My actual procedure was to cover an event with a judgment about myself the instant it occurred. Before it had time to become experience, it had already become material.

That movement probably runs in the opposite direction from phenomenology. Of course, by phenomenology I do not mean that a person can preserve perfectly pure raw data. The moment attention begins, and the moment language begins, experience has already been selected and organized. No absolute scene exists independently of body, language, and memory.

IV

What deserves still greater caution is that I do not merely forget the past. I then use the fragments left after forgetting to put the past on trial.

My earlier self finishes a project; I now say that it contained little technical substance. He feels happy about something; I now say that the award did not count for much. He genuinely felt certain things within a relationship; I can now explain all of it through attachment, need, projection, and the desire to prove himself.

Each explanation may be partly correct. But seeing a person’s weaknesses does not mean that weakness is all they possess; seeing their strengths does not mean that strength is all they possess. Likewise, knowing which problem remained after an experience does not mean that the entire experience consisted of that problem. Between the experience and the judgment stands the person I am now.

My present anxiety, aims, standards, and expectations of the future all enter my judgment of the past. Perhaps I think my earlier progress was too slow merely because I now need to prepare for the postgraduate entrance examination. Perhaps I find an award insufficient because the comparison has changed. Perhaps I regard my former self as childish simply because I now know things he did not.

The person I was was certainly not perfect. He skipped classes, gave up on things, behaved like an idiot, and wasted a great deal of time. Respecting experience does not require me to beautify the past. But he was not an outdated firmware release awaiting the upgrade that produced me. He saw what his circumstances allowed him to see, failed to know what they did not allow him to know, and felt certain things that I can no longer feel. I may analyse him, but possession of the later plot does not naturally give me greater authority to define the life he lived.

If the first premise is true—if everyone has the right to feel the world from within their own experience—then my former self also counts as a person.

He did not exist merely to manufacture the person I am today.

V

What felt truly strange while browsing the files was not the discovery that I had secretly been impressive and completed so many things.

If I merely reorganize the attachments into a portfolio, list more than a year of life as a dozen projects, and conclude that “I am actually not bad,” the underlying logic remains unchanged. I had too few indicators before; now I have more.

The real question is why I permit an experience to have meaning only after it has been converted into an indicator. An experience does not cease to have occurred merely because it produced no ability. A person cannot consist only of the part that can be placed in a summary or résumé. Or perhaps a person ought to remain sensitive and capable of feeling the world more fully. Though, now that I look at it, is this not a fairly common problem for an INTP—thinking so much that feeling becomes difficult?

Put differently, if I preserve only what can explain “who I am,” what survives may not be me at all. It may be a manual telling other people how I wish to be understood. This is what I now need to watch: while continually attempting to construct a self, I may have spent all this time deleting it.

VI

What, then, should come next?

I can begin by occasionally recording two minutes of audio: one scene from the day, one sentence someone actually said, and an approximate account of what my body felt at the time. A record of the good life, perhaps—like the day I happened to find a friend sitting on a garden bench, recording what had happened to him.

Gather slightly more information. Use one indicator less often to summarize an entire period of life. Before reaching a conclusion, admit that I may already have forgotten a great deal. Perhaps these are enough to begin with.

As for the tones of voice from the past that were never preserved, I probably cannot recover them.
