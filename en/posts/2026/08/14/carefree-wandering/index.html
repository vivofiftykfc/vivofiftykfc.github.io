Notes on Carefree Wandering

The first half of Carefree Wandering is mainly concerned with Zhuangzi’s view of the world and of cognition. Human beings always begin from themselves when trying to understand the world. Almost without noticing, they make themselves its measure, using their own experience, situation, and knowledge to confine everything that exists. This is, I think, one of the meanings behind the “distinction between the small and the great.” The enormous Peng, the cicada and the little dove, and many of the figures that follow all reveal the same tendency.

Zhuangzi therefore goes on to say: “The perfected person has no self; the spiritual person has no achievement; the sage has no name.” My present understanding is that we should try to step outside our habitual point of view. We should neither turn that viewpoint into an absolute nor mistake it for the world itself; at the same time, we should not allow external standards to imprison us in return. What Zhuangzi calls “carefree wandering” may be a condition of movement and freedom within the Dao, through which heaven, earth, and the myriad things continuously change. One does not cling to a fixed identity, scale, or judgment, but changes flexibly with the world until no single framework can hold one fast.

The second half first uses Xu You to develop the idea that “the sage has no name.” Xu You does not pursue titles that appear splendid but may have no genuine connection with his own life. Other people chase fame and profit, competing within a particular system of value. From his perspective, there is no compelling reason to enter the game at all; participation may simply bring disturbance, exhaustion, and injury.

“When the tailorbird nests in the deep forest, it occupies no more than one branch; when the mole drinks from the river, it fills no more than its belly.” What I truly require need only be enough to sustain my own life and allow me to remain free. You, Yao, may regard the empire, the throne, and the achievement of governing well as matters of the greatest importance. I have no obligation to enter your coordinate system, nor do I want to confine myself within identities such as “sage king” and “Son of Heaven.” What you value need not be what I value. What I seek is my own path of wandering.

The story of the spiritual person on Mount Gushe, meanwhile, criticizes the habit of standing within the boundaries of one’s own knowledge and experience and casually using them to judge everything in existence. Something should not be declared impossible merely because it exceeds what we have encountered. To put the point in a rather crude and not entirely accurate but intuitive sentence: “How can you prove that a god does not exist?”

This continues the earlier distinction between the small and the great. Human experience is always limited, yet limited experience readily disguises itself as the world in its entirety. It is precisely this intellectual complacency against which Zhuangzi warns us.

At the same time, the spiritual person of Mount Gushe represents an extreme condition of freedom imagined by Zhuangzi. To such a person, even the achievements of Yao and Shun, admired to the highest degree by ordinary society, weigh very little. The point is not necessarily that the spiritual person’s “ability” exceeds theirs. Rather, from the perspective of the freedom they seek, governing the world and accomplishing great deeds have ceased to be the most important standards of judgment.

The achievements of Yao and Shun may of course be pursued. There is simply no need to rely upon them as proof of oneself, or to let them harden into one’s identity. This is roughly what “the spiritual person has no achievement” means: not that such a person lacks ability or refuses to act, but that “what I have accomplished” no longer serves as the fundamental measure defining who I am.

At the end, the discussion returns to Huizi through the stories of the enormous gourd and the useless tree. Put in the bluntest possible way, the gourd story says: Huizi, you idiot, someone gives you this splendid enormous gourd, yet all you can think about is whether it can be used as a ladle. At this point you resemble a ladle yourself. Zhuangzi asks why Huizi did not use it as a vessel and float across the rivers and lakes. The problem was never the gourd. Huizi had already imprisoned it inside a fixed conception of “use.”

That was why the Confucian phrase “the gentleman is not a vessel” first came to mind. The comparison is not completely accurate, but there is some common ground. What Zhuangzi truly opposes is the attempt to use a rigid and predetermined social category to dictate what something “ought to be useful for.” First allow a thing to be what it is. Do not conclude that something is defective merely because it does not fit the purpose you had assigned in advance. Change the scale, the relation, or the mode of use, and what once appeared “useless” may become useful in another way.

If the enormous gourd means, “You think it is useless only because you do not know how to use it,” the enormous tree goes further: even if something truly does not count as useful by ordinary standards, so what? Precisely because the tree cannot be measured by the carpenter’s line, does not conform to his rules, and attracts no interest from him, it will not be cut down. It can grow undisturbed in “the village of nowhere, in the broad and boundless wild.” People may wander beside it or sleep beneath it, while the tree itself remains whole. This is approximately the “usefulness of the useless.”

Something regarded by society as “useless” does not therefore lack value. At times it is precisely because it remains on the edge, outside a utilitarian system, that it gains a space free from interference—a space more favourable to preserving itself and even to pursuing freedom.

Understood in this way, there are indeed parts of Zhuangzi’s thought with which I agree strongly.

The first is his warning about cognition: do not remain confined within your own field of vision and then assume that the world must be identical to what you see. The size, rightness, usefulness, and value of things often depend upon the position of the observer. The greatest danger lies not in possessing a scale, but in making that scale absolute—in mistaking one’s map for the world itself.

I also admire the lightness with which Zhuangzi regards his own existence. He no longer requires life to carry so many burdens of self-justification. A person need not become something, prove something, or leave something behind before earning the right to live freely. This lightness of spirit, this carefree wandering, is indeed an ideal condition that I deeply value.

Still, I probably cannot achieve so complete a freedom in Zhuangzi’s sense. At present I continue to feel that some of his thought contains a slight tendency to withdraw from the world. What I desire may be closer to “the greatest hermit lives in the marketplace,” or “doing nothing, yet leaving nothing undone.”

For now, I would summarize what Carefree Wandering has given me in four layers:

In cognition, do not measure other things solely by oneself.
In value, do not define oneself through reputation and achievement.
In action, do not cut everything down to fit a fixed purpose.
In existence, do not assume that because you are “useless” by conventional standards, you must be remade into something else.

These four layers eventually converge into the first, incomplete idea of “carefree wandering” that I can presently understand.

The size, rightness, usefulness, and value of all things depend upon the scale and relation occupied by the observer. What matters, therefore, is not the possession of an absolutely correct scale, but the refusal ever to make one scale absolute.

If, upon reaching such an understanding, one can still enter the world, act, and create without allowing those things to bind one again—then that is probably the form of carefree wandering I myself would rather pursue.
