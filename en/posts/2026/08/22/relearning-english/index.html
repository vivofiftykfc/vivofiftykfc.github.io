Relearning English

Over the past few days, I have started learning English again. By accident, I discovered that thinking in English is quite a good way to stop my mind running in circles.

Perhaps my vocabulary is simply not large enough to let my thoughts roam freely. In Chinese, one small event can recurse through emotions, the past, and the future; in English, all that remains in my head is: who did what, and why?

As I continued, English also began to seem less difficult than I had imagined. Language is, after all, a tool for stating facts and expressing relationships. Grammar provides the structures shared by sentences; vocabulary fills those structures with whatever we actually want to say; and other words establish cause, contrast, and condition between the parts. Taking the process apart slowly—watching one sentence grow and several sentences join into a complete thought—is surprisingly satisfying.

Learning a language again from the beginning really does feel like retraining expression. Many things that I once said by intuition now require me to see the subject, verb, and object clearly, and to identify exactly where the premise of a judgement lies. In this respect, at least, English has forced me to become a little more rigorous.

Of course, the side effect of training my expression this way is also obvious: I already talked like I was writing a paper, and now I sound even more like one. How to learn to speak like a normal human being can wait until later.

For now, here is the core outline of English grammar that I have put together.

I. The Skeleton of a Sentence

The subject, verb, and object or complement form the main skeleton of a sentence. Modifiers and adverbials expand it outwards, while clauses place an entire proposition inside one of its grammatical positions.

The most basic English sentence structures can be divided into five patterns:

Pattern Structure Example
SV Subject + Verb Birds fly.
SVO Subject + Verb + Object I like music.
SVC Subject + Linking Verb + Subject Complement She is happy.
SVOO Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object I gave him a book.
SVOC Subject + Verb + Object + Object Complement I found the book useful.

These five patterns provide the skeleton. One particularly important addition here is the subject complement. In She is happy., happy is not an object. It describes the state of the subject, She, so it is a subject complement, also called a predicative complement.

English can treat an action, or even a whole proposition, as a “thing” and place it inside a sentence. Once this becomes clear, noun clauses cease to look quite so mysterious.

III. Modifiers: Expanding Nouns

A modifier of a noun answers two questions: Which one? What kind of one?

These forms look different, but they all do the same job: they add information to a noun.

IV. Adverbials: Expanding an Action or a Whole Sentence

An adverbial answers questions such as: When? Where? Why? How? Under what condition? For what purpose?

Logical relations such as cause, condition, concession, time, and purpose mostly appear here.

V. Complements: Completing the Meaning

With some verbs, a noun alone is not enough. Something else must explain what the subject or object is, what state it is in, or what it does.

This is where linking verbs, subject complements, object complements, infinitives, and participles appear. Before memorising the terminology, I can ask a simpler question: Whom does this part describe, or whose state does it complete?

VI. The Verb System

Many changes in an English sentence are controlled by the verb. Instead of memorising the so-called twelve or sixteen tenses as separate tables, it is more useful to begin with four dimensions:

  • Tense: whether an event is located in the past or the present.
  • Aspect: whether the event is viewed as ongoing, completed, or in a general state.
  • Voice: who performs the action and who undergoes it.
  • Modality: attitudes such as ability, obligation, necessity, and possibility.

These are not several unrelated tables. They are different ways of describing the same action from different positions in time, state, and attitude.

VIII. Three Types of Subordinate Clause

The three main types of subordinate clause simply hand an entire clause the work that would otherwise be performed by a word or phrase.

Clause Function in the larger sentence Example
Noun clause Subject, object, or subject complement I think that he is right.
Relative clause Modifier of a noun The man who came yesterday is my teacher.
Adverbial clause Adverbial of cause, condition, time, concession, and so on I stayed home because it was raining.

IX. Logic between Sentences

Only when connecting words join several propositions together do we move from “making sentences” to expressing a complete line of reasoning.

Logical relation Common expressions
Cause and effect because / so / therefore
Contrast and concession but / however / although
Condition if / unless
Purpose to / in order to / so that
Addition and progression and / also / moreover / not only…but also
Comparison than / as…as / compared with
Example for example / such as
Explanation and reformulation in other words / that is / which means
Inference therefore / thus / as a result

For a beginner, mastering cause and effect, contrast, condition, and purpose is already enough to express many complete ideas. Two common Chinese-influenced structures also deserve attention: English does not normally use because...so... together, nor does it normally use although...but... together. One side of the connection is enough.

XI. Points Not Yet Developed

The outline above explains the core architecture of English grammar: what skeleton a sentence has, how information is added, how clauses expand it, and how several propositions form a line of reasoning. It does not, however, cover every point of grammar. The following topics still belong on the map, but have not yet been developed one by one.

Area Points not yet developed
Noun system Count and non-count nouns, singular and plural, possession, articles, determiners, quantifiers, and pronouns
Adjectives and adverbs Position in the sentence, comparatives, superlatives, degree modification, and as...as
Preposition system Temporal, spatial, and abstract relations, together with prepositional collocations
Verb system Transitive and intransitive verbs, verb valency, auxiliary verbs, phrasal verbs, and subject–verb agreement
Tense, aspect, and voice The precise meanings of different tense–aspect combinations, the uses and limits of the passive, and sequence of tenses
Modality Differences among can/could/may/might/must/should when expressing ability, possibility, inference, obligation, and shades of attitude
Sentence transformations Negatives, yes–no questions, wh-questions, imperatives, and exclamatives
Special constructions Existential there, anticipatory and dummy it, cleft sentences, inversion, ellipsis, and substitution
Details of non-finite verbs Choosing between to do and doing, present and past participles, absolute constructions, and dangling modifiers
Details of clauses Differences among that/whether/what, restrictive and non-restrictive relative clauses, relative pronouns, and relative adverbs
Conditionals and the subjunctive Real and unreal conditions, the subjunctive, and mixed conditionals
Reported speech Direct and indirect speech, together with changes in person, tense, time, and place during conversion
Organisation between sentences Coordination, parallelism, reference, ellipsis, substitution, punctuation, and information cohesion
Actual use Collocation, register, politeness, stress, intonation, and information focus

Some of these topics belong to grammar in the narrow sense, while others approach vocabulary, pragmatics, and discourse organisation. I have placed them together for the moment because, when English is actually used, they do not appear in separate textbook chapters.

Once I had sorted out this grammatical framework, I went back to scrolling through some videos. I automatically began analysing the structure of the sentences and the logic of the way their authors wrote, only to discover how much of the dialogue said almost nothing and how thin its content was. But that, too, can wait for another time.
