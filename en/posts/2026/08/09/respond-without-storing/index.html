Neither Escort nor Welcome; Respond without Storing

Yesterday, I read an article which said that some people enjoy analysing, making deductions, and constructing theories. At first, their object of study is the external world. Gradually, they also place themselves inside it: why do I think this way, why do I need an answer, and who is the person observing me? Whenever an explanation is obtained, the explanation itself becomes a new problem. In the end, the observer continues to observe the observer, and reflection reflects upon reflection reflecting. Reason not only deals with problems; it can also turn its own process of dealing with them into another problem, and this process has no necessary end.

People imagine that they are seeking truth, when what they sometimes seek is only certainty. The answer itself need not be beautiful; it is simply that having no answer causes unease. They do not necessarily have to understand the world, but cannot accept that, for the moment, the world refuses to obey an explanation. Then “why do I need certainty?” also becomes an object of analysis, and even “I should accept uncertainty” can turn into a new demand.

This first made me think of Buddhism. Yet after discussing Laozi and Zhuangzi, the feeling remained somewhat different. They do not seem interested in installing another, more advanced observer above reason. Their questions are these: when is a form of knowledge already sufficient? Why do people always want to treat their own distinctions as the world itself? And after understanding, can they first go and live?

The Laozi begins, “The Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao; the name that can be named is not the constant name.” Of course, translating this directly into “language determines the boundaries of thought” would move far too quickly. The early Wittgenstein was concerned with how propositions picture facts; the later Wittgenstein with how language is used within life.

Even so, the claim that “names cannot exhaust reality” gives me enough to consider for a long time. Student, engineer, successful person, failure—each name provides information. Without concepts, people could neither communicate nor act. Trouble begins when a name no longer merely describes certain facts, but requires the facts to obey it. Then we have fallen into the trap of language.

“Being and non-being generate each other; difficult and easy complete each other; long and short give form to each other; high and low incline towards each other” adds another layer. Many judgements are not fixed properties carried independently by an object, but arise within relations and comparisons. High requires low. Success requires a scheme for distinguishing failure. Excellence first requires the question: excellent according to which standard? This does not mean that every evaluation is false. Scores genuinely affect admission, and whether a project runs is governed by highly specific standards. But the distance from “this result is poor” to “this entire person is poor” is actually enormous.

When I then read that “reversal is the movement of the Dao,” certain things connect. A person who wants to control everything perfectly is eventually led by the desire for control. A person who wants to remain constantly lucid ends up continually checking whether he is lucid. A person who wants to prove that he is sufficiently excellent must ask before every action whether “this can prove me.” When a force is pushed to an extreme, it may indeed begin producing its opposite.

I naturally understand this through feedback and reaction. The analogy helps me enter the text, and should stop there. Laozi was not writing control theory. He was saying only that excessive effort is not merely exhausting. It can sometimes alter the thing itself, until the action gradually serves what it originally intended to escape.

Hence wuwei, or non-coercive action.

It obviously does not mean doing nothing. Describing it as “less control” is still insufficient, because less control can itself become a more sophisticated technique of control. In the Laozi, wuwei is also connected with spontaneity, desire, cultivation, and politics. It was not written specifically as a commentary upon modern procrastination. What I initially took from it was probably only one sentence: “Do not add anything else.”

Yet stopping here would reduce Laozi to a technique for managing anxiety, as though the entire meaning of wuwei were to make me consume myself less.

Laozi also pursues something positive called ziran. Here, it does not mean forests and rivers, nor doing whatever one pleases. It is closer to “being so of itself”: the countless things are not forcibly prescribed by an external will, but grow and change according to their own conditions. Laozi repeatedly writes of infants, the uncarved block, valleys, and water, probably because all approach a state of life not yet excessively shaped by reputation, desire, and artificial standards. What he seeks to reduce is not action itself, but the human impulse to prescribe a shape for everything.

“Giving birth without possessing, acting without relying upon achievement, helping to grow without ruling” may come closer than “doing nothing.” Something is nurtured without being claimed as property; a task is completed without grasping its credit; growth is assisted without thereby obtaining the right to rule. Human beings still participate in the world, but do not announce that the world belongs to them because they have participated.

Thus, wuwei in the Laozi is not merely individual cultivation. In discussing politics, the text says, “I practise non-action and the people transform themselves; I value stillness and the people correct themselves.” This ancient political vision obviously cannot be transferred to the present without distinction. At the very least, however, it shows that Laozi was not seeking an individual more skilled at self-management, but an order that does not depend upon constantly stimulating desire, manufacturing competition, and imposing intervention. If the ruler undertakes less self-important shaping, people may regain room for “self-transformation.”

Seen in this light, “do not add anything else” is no longer merely negative subtraction. It creates room for something to happen by itself.

“Being provides advantage; non-being provides use” probably describes the same kind of empty space. A wheel turns because the centre of its hub has not been filled; a vessel holds something because its interior is empty. “Non-being” here is neither deficiency nor the destruction of everything already present. It merely reminds me that certain functions occur precisely where a space has not been occupied.

Whenever I discover a problem, I feel that I ought to add something: one more explanation, one more plan, one more review, one more angle from which to inspect myself. Yet some problems do not lack a step; they have already been crowded full of steps. Writing another analysis of “how to stop excessive analysis” does nothing except add another layer to the recursion.

I am therefore now more willing to ask whether this step actually addresses reality, or merely relieves the anxiety created by my inability to control reality.

The two are often entangled. I may not be able to distinguish them.

Still, having spoken of Laozi and Zhuangzi together, I should pause here. Laozi retains a direction of “return”: return to the uncarved block, the infant, and softness, withdrawing from a condition disturbed by desire and artificial standards. Zhuangzi does not necessarily promise one authentic position to which everyone ought to return. He asks not only how to return to nature, but who is standing where when the words nature, correct, and original are spoken.

One speaks of returning; the other of wandering. They are not two successive stations along the same road.

In the “Discussion on Making All Things Equal,” the problem returns to the person who is knowing the world. “There is a ‘this’ and a ‘that’ for every right and wrong.” Each person begins from a “this,” seeing his own “right” and another’s “wrong.” This does not mean that truth and falsehood do not exist, still less that every opinion possesses equal quality. A frog at the bottom of a well does not know the sea. The existence of its own perspective does not suddenly give it as much information as someone who has seen the sea.

The real difficulty of Zhuangzi is that he restores the position of the judge which an analysis had removed. What I see also depends upon where I see it from, what I have experienced, what language I use, and what kind of life I am presently defending. I may still say that something is wrong, and must remain responsible for the statement. I can no longer pretend, however, that I speak from an absolute position which does not exist.

The expression “pivot of the Dao” is especially interesting. A pivot is the axis of a door. It stands upon neither side, but allows the door to turn. It does not demand that a person possess no position forever. I may make a judgement now and act accordingly; when new facts arrive, I may also turn. This does not mean occupying every perspective in advance. It means only admitting that I stand here now, and that here is not the centre of the universe.

Then again, this reminder about perspective cannot be reduced to the sentence “everyone has a point.” I have just written that “everyone possesses the right to experience the world,” but not everyone’s judgement produces equal consequences. The partial view of someone who controls institutions, resources, and the authority to define things is more likely to become the conditions under which other people must live. Particular harm cannot be erased by saying that “each side is limited,” nor can responsibility. Knowing that one occupies a position should restrain absolutism, not provide an excuse.

Zhuangzi’s loosening of the “self” cannot be identified directly with the Buddhist idea of non-self either. “I have lost myself” and “the perfected person has no self,” at least in this reading, are closer to refusing attachment to one particular self. Student, lover, son, a person preparing for an examination—each identity is real and participates in forming me. None has the right to possess me alone, and none may announce that the whole person has failed merely because it temporarily fails.

Connected with “depending upon nothing,” this can easily be presented as “needing nothing.” But how could a concrete person possess no dependencies? People need food and sleep, money, a body, and assistance from others. Their room for choice is also constrained by real conditions. As I wrote in the article on feminism, telling someone to become courageous and true to herself does not create freedom from nothing when she lacks safe housing and the resources for life. Zhuangzi should not be used to cancel those conditions.

I therefore dare to offer only a highly personal translation: “depending upon nothing” does not mean having no conditions, but refusing to mortgage the right to exist completely to any single condition. The postgraduate entrance examination remains important, but passing it does not grant me permission to exist. Relationships remain important, but being chosen by someone does not issue the certificate proving whether I deserve love. Ability must still grow, but its strength does not determine whether I may first experience the world as a human being.

This certainly represents one level of free wandering, yet now it seems insufficient. Behind “do not let conditions define me” there remains a very solid self: external identities may change, but inside them there seems to be one true self that must be protected whole. Zhuangzi may not intend to preserve even that “true self” for me.

The opening of the “Free and Easy Wandering” is strange. The giant Kun and Peng still require sufficiently deep water and powerful wind before taking flight; the cicada and little dove cannot fly high, but have their own suitable elm and sandalwood trees; Liezi can already ride the wind, yet Zhuangzi still says that “he remains dependent.” Thus, the essay does not first imagine someone who needs no conditions and then call him free. On the contrary, it spends a long time describing how all beings depend upon something before asking: “If one rides the proper course of heaven and earth, and commands the changes of the six energies in order to wander through the inexhaustible, upon what would such a person depend?”

I now prefer to place the emphasis upon the word “wander.” Free wandering does not mean finally locating a position that will remain correct and safe forever. It means not being nailed permanently to any position. Heaven, earth, and the six energies continue to change, while people continue to act through them. The difference is that a person no longer requires one condition to support him forever, but retains the capacity to turn, move, and respond again when change arrives.

Seen this way, “the perfected person has no self, the spiritual person has no achievement, and the sage has no name” does not eliminate people, action, and names. Having no self means no longer making one fixed self the centre of everything. Having no achievement means not freezing oneself through achievement after acting. Having no name means using names without allowing them to become the final judgement. This is less a defence of a true self untouched by influence than the preservation of life’s capacity to continue changing.

At its end, “Free and Easy Wandering” deliberately offers two “useless” things. The enormous gourd is too heavy to hold water and, when divided into ladles, fits nothing. The tree-of-heaven grows contrary to every carpenter’s rule and is ignored by the woodcutter. Huizi sees only that they fail to meet existing purposes. Zhuangzi asks why the great gourd cannot float upon rivers and lakes, and why the tree cannot be planted in the land of nothing whatever, so that one may wander freely and sleep beneath it. Not every existence must first prove that it has the same kind of use. Being declared useless by one purpose sometimes means only that it can travel somewhere that purpose has never imagined.

Free wandering is therefore not a flight from reality out of nothing, still less the declaration of spiritual freedom behind a closed door. It must still “ride things” and pass through particular conditions; only the “wandering mind” is no longer detained by them. The external world changes me, relationships change me, and every failure and success leaves something behind. Zhuangzi probably does not guarantee that a complete self will survive the change. His question is closer to this: if I already exist within change, why must the preservation of my present self become the measure of every change?

Of course, everything written so far remains inside my head. Yet Zhuangzi also describes Cook Ding cutting up an ox: “I meet it with the spirit and do not look with the eyes; sensory knowledge stops and the spirit moves as it desires.” This sounds like mystical intuition, but beneath it lie Ding’s many years of experience with the knife. Even reducing it to the bodily intuition produced by expertise would still make the passage too narrow. Ding also says, “There are spaces between the joints, while the knife-edge has no thickness; use what has no thickness to enter where there is space.” His knife remains as new after nineteen years not because he controls the ox more completely, but because he does not strike directly against tendons and bones. He travels through the gaps which already exist within the thing. Action no longer depends upon pressing forward with greater force. It makes itself thin and passes through where the thing allows passage.

Fasting of the mind and sitting in forgetfulness are the same. They are not anti-intellectual slogans; they merely prevent existing judgements from occupying every position the moment reality appears. To act “according to what is already so,” one must still encounter things, be limited by them, and be corrected by them. Without practice, so-called movement with the tendency of things may simply follow one’s own imagination. This joins closely with my existing understanding: reality exists independently; people use body, experience, and language to form limited knowledge, then act to encounter it; reality pushes back, allowing knowledge to be corrected again.

Laozi and Zhuangzi have not proved that models should not be built, nor told me to follow feeling from now on. What they add is an answer to what should happen after a model has completed one task.

My default answer was always further improvement. Discover a deviation and update; discover that the update also contains deviation and reflect upon the update. Openness slowly became a task that was never allowed to end: I must remain open, must constantly revise, must guard against becoming rigid. Eventually, even “continue using it without fully believing it” became a self-image that required continual proof. Laozi and Zhuangzi provide no still higher method of remaining open. They merely say that, sometimes, I can stop inspecting for a while.

I am probably incapable of truly stopping the construction of models. Perhaps even this article is another outbreak of the same symptom. But can I at least stop turning every event immediately into a judgement about “what kind of person I am”?

This also explains why the eight characters I finally remembered came from “Responding to Emperors and Kings”:

Neither escort nor welcome; respond without storing.

The original says that “the perfected person’s mind operates like a mirror”: it does not follow what leaves or anticipate what arrives; when something comes, it reflects it, and when it passes, nothing is retained. In conversation, we interpreted this as follows: do not force the past into the present, and do not cover the present with the future in advance; respond when something comes, and stop grasping it once it has passed. Reading the words through past and future is the modern use I have taken from them, not the only interpretation of the original. More directly, the source says: do not follow, do not anticipate; respond upon arrival and retain nothing after departure.

The word “respond” is also important. A mirror does not avoid contact with external things, nor remain cold towards everything. On the contrary, whatever comes is reflected. It avoids injury not by escaping events, but by refusing to detain them permanently in the mind after they have passed. This clearly does not oppose preparation and review. It says only that preparation need not mean living through failure in advance, and review need not preserve one outcome as a permanent identity.

In the end, many events never occur only once inside my head. Before they happen, I simulate them; while they happen, I interpret them; after they happen, I continue writing them into a self-narrative. Eight characters cannot solve these things. They merely offer an order I did not previously know very well: allow the event to arrive, respond to it genuinely, and finally permit it to pass.

Looking back now, Laozi and Zhuangzi seek more than an “admission that knowledge is limited.” Laozi worries that people use names, desire, and coercion to shape the countless things into what they want before those things can unfold. He therefore returns to ziran, allowing things to arise and transform by themselves, acting without possession. Zhuangzi worries that people become trapped in one position by prejudice, identity, and achievement. He therefore seeks not merely to loosen judgement, but to “wander,” allowing the mind to continue moving with the changes of things.

One says: do not seize the countless things and turn them into mine. The other says: do not seize myself and turn me into a thing. The two finally meet in refusing to let what has already formed block the life still occurring.

Non-action cannot excuse procrastination; the equality of things cannot abolish truth, falsehood, and responsibility; dependence upon nothing cannot erase the real limits imposed by resources, institutions, and other people. If I cannot solve a problem, that means I genuinely cannot solve it now. If I injure someone, I remain responsible for the consequences. Laozi and Zhuangzi remove only the additional layer added too rapidly afterwards: I cannot solve this problem, therefore my complete life is a failure; I did not control this event, therefore something must be wrong with the world.

Knowledge is meant to participate in reality, not replace it. Once used, it can sometimes be placed to one side.
