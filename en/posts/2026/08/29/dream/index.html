The Moon and Sixpence

Today I read through a few stories and biographies about George Soros, along with a post on Zhihu. It occurred to me that after Soros had made enough money, his later actions looked more like a philosopher trying to embody his principles. The money in his hands seemed more like a tool, a stake he could put on the table, than an end in itself. His childhood experiences in Hungary under Nazi Germany and later Soviet control were clearly painful enough to leave him deeply afraid and contemptuous of closed societies—or, more precisely, of claims to absolute truth.

His epistemology likewise begins from the major premise that people are fallible. He also observed that people possess reflexivity. Roughly speaking, the combination of biased knowledge of the world and human agency creates a positive-feedback loop. A partial understanding of the world leads people to act in ways that are not optimal, but their actions then change reality; that altered reality acts back upon their understanding. The conclusion is that market participants can be collectively wrong, and that their errors will not necessarily correct themselves. This is the source of his criticism of market fundamentalism.

He therefore criticises the extreme free market and argues that extreme liberalism can also give rise to crimes. This is especially clear in his essay The Capitalist Threat.

The political form he advocates is consequently the open society: a pluralistic, free, tolerant society whose members are allowed to make mistakes and whose powers check one another. Such a society does not claim possession of absolute truth. It permits itself to be corrected.

Yet one thing is obvious. Soros saw all of this clearly and also profited from reflexivity in the market. He opposed absolute truth, while making money inside a society that treated the “absolutely free market” as an absolute truth. Here the tension in his ethics becomes visible.

There is another point I want to develop. The hedonist’s objective function is personal pleasure, so there is a relative ceiling on the harm his actions can do to society. From the perspective of optimisation, however, the idealist’s objective function is his ideal: he wants to remake society into the form he believes it ought to take. His motivation is therefore stronger than the hedonist’s—and he is more dangerous as well.

Turning this thought back upon myself, what is my ideal? Or, put differently, what could provide me with an unending source of motivation? Clearly, at my present stage, no goal occupies such a position.

I asked GPT to search through the data it had about me. It said:

I even suspect that your ultimate ideal may not be some kind of “career,” but a state of being:

Clear-eyed without becoming cynical; powerful without being enslaved by power; rational while retaining the capacity to feel; able to understand the world without reducing it to a model; capable of changing reality while still allowing reality to exceed you.

If you eventually become this kind of person, then even if you never colonise Mars or found a hundred-billion-yuan company, I suspect that somewhere deep inside you would still believe:

This life was not lived in vain.

Conversely, even if you accomplished all those things, if you discovered that you had spent your whole life merely trying to prove yourself, driven onward by desire and fear, you would probably feel that something had gone badly wrong.

So that is my wager.

Your ideal is not primarily “what do I want to turn the world into?” but “what kind of subject do I want to become, and how do I participate in the world as that subject?”

That is also why I think you really are an idealist. You are simply only now beginning to peel your real “ideal” out from behind a pile of grand objectives.

Er. From my perspective, this may not quite match the ideal I have in mind, or what the word “ideal” means within my own vocabulary. What it describes may only be a goal for one stage of my life—or perhaps the result of a process of self-cultivation.

But thinking again, I seem to find it difficult at my present age to sustain any attachment to self for very long. I used to be able to do so: I had to enter a good university; I had to pursue a particular girl; I even had to like a particular thing. Yet feelings towards things clearly occur in stages. Perhaps this is because some emotional impulses change with time and age.

And then there is the sort of person I am. I am accustomed to dragging every idea that has only just poked its head above the surface straight onto the operating table. How did it form? Through what mechanism does it work? Why do I feel something for this particular thing, and why do I pursue certain things? Which of my deficiencies does my fondness for them answer? What does psychology say about it, and what philosophical insight might it offer? Then I incorporate it into my system of thought, while also dismantling it into a heap of spare parts. Fine, I admit it: I am a cold-blooded murderer, wielding the crude scalpel of reductionism to kill feeling. Boo-hoo…

I also admit that this habit comes from the desire—and the excitement—of turning chaos into an intelligible structure. My brain has become extraordinarily sensitive to this process and hungry for it. At the same time, I enjoy thinking about how the structures I have dismantled might be used and how they might be loaded back into me. Mm. Something like that.

In short, whenever a new attachment to self forms, I dismember it into a heap of body parts and then lose interest.

Turning the thought around once more, perhaps an ideal is something I still cannot put down even after dismembering it. Or perhaps it is what that line means: “True heroism is to love life after seeing the truth of it clearly.”

Mm. If I think again about how such a thing forms, perhaps feeling can arise only through repeated action. Through repetition, one develops feeling; from feeling, one takes on responsibility and forms an identity; only then does an ideal gradually reveal itself. That seems to be the path. Why does this also sound like the way a hobby develops? Something is still wrong here, but I will leave it for now. I am getting a little sleepy.

Mm. The process above is probably closer to how a hobby forms. An ideal shares part of that process, but something resembling the meaning of one’s life must also enter it. Otherwise, the path is roughly the same:

Action -> feeling -> responsibility -> reflection and connection with the meaning of life -> eventual self-persuasion and the formation of an attachment to self -> identity -> ideal

GPT also offered me a suggestion, so I may as well leave it here. Sometimes an ideal does not appear all at once, making me fall in love with it and immediately supplying enormous motivation. Sometimes I have to live it out for several years. After I have dismantled it and understood it inside out, if I still cannot let go and still want to keep doing it, then perhaps that is what I truly love. Perhaps that is my ideal. It is a little like love: it does not always have to begin at first sight. In many cases, people marry first and fall in love afterwards. Hehe.
