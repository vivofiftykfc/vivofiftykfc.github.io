A Crash Course in Facial Aesthetics

This is a scientific guide extending from aesthetic principles to changes that can be made after birth. The first part asks “why,” examining the four underlying channels through which the brain evaluates a face. The second asks “how,” playing every available card, from body-fat percentage to hairstyle, clothing, and social presence. Prologue: Aesthetic Judgement Is a Skill That Can Be Learned

“Beauty is difficult.” — Plato, Hippias Major

“Aesthetic judgement is subjective; different people prefer different things.”

This is probably the most frequently repeated claim in discussions of appearance. It sounds fair, inclusive, and unlikely to cause offence. Yet it is wrong—or, at least, only half right.

Personal preferences certainly differ, just as some people prefer coffee and others tea. When we discuss “handsomeness” and “beauty,” however, studies across cultures and historical periods repeatedly reveal one fact: human judgements of faces follow highly consistent underlying rules.

When a member of a Nigerian tribe, an office worker in Tokyo, and a university student in Stockholm evaluate photographs of faces belonging to different ethnic groups, the correlations among their ratings of “attractiveness” reach 0.7-0.9 (Langlois et al., 2000). This agreement crosses race, culture, sex, and even age. Infants only three months old look for longer at the faces which adults have rated more highly (Slater et al., 1998).

Three popular myths require correction:

Myth One: “Aesthetic judgement is subjective and cannot be explained.” In reality, while subjective elements exist, the underlying mechanisms—detection of symmetry, calculation of proportion, and assessment of sexual dimorphism—are hard-wired cognitive modules whose operation is highly consistent.

Myth Two: “A handsome face is inherited and cannot be changed.” Genetics certainly establishes a broad range, but within that range, body-fat percentage, muscular definition, the condition of the skin, hairstyle, habitual expressions, and posture each exert an influence no weaker than that of the skeleton itself.

Myth Three: “Money is all that matters; whether someone is handsome is irrelevant.” This is less an aesthetic judgement than a cynical simplification of social rules.

The position of this book is perfectly clear:

Aesthetic judgement follows rules, and most aspects of appearance can be improved after birth.

We shall answer two central questions:

  1. What exactly is “handsomeness”?—what does the brain actually assess when it evaluates a face?
  2. What can I do?—once the rules are understood, which particular and practical directions are available?

Chapter 1: Symmetry and Averageness—the Brain Sweeps for Mines

“Symmetry is the receipt for health.” — Evolutionary psychologist Randy Thornhill

1.1 Symmetry

The Developmental Stability Hypothesis

The Developmental Stability Hypothesis tells us that the degree of symmetry between the two sides of the body provides an external measure of whether development proceeded smoothly.

Development encounters various forms of “noise”: parasitic infection, malnutrition, inbreeding, genetic mutation, and maternal stress or illness during pregnancy. Every disturbance leaves a minute trace upon the face.

Research finding: a cross-cultural study discovered a highly consistent preference for “symmetrical faces” among participants in Britain, Japan, China, India, and South Africa (Perrett et al., 1999).

A Common Misunderstanding about “Perfect Symmetry”

No perfectly symmetrical human face exists in nature. Every face is slightly asymmetrical, and research employs the concept of a degree of symmetry. Interestingly, a perfectly symmetrical face is instead regarded as unnatural, because it lies beyond the biological range of variation in human faces.

1.2 The Averaging Effect

During the nineteenth century, the researcher Francis Galton conducted an experiment in which he superimposed photographs of several criminals. He discovered that the resulting average face appeared more regular than any of the originals. An average face is a face “without an obvious problem”: its nose is neither too large nor too small, and its chin neither excessively receding nor excessively projecting.

Limitation: The Problem of Distinctiveness

An average face has one fatal weakness: it lacks distinctiveness. A face that truly reaches the highest level must build upon the foundation of “averageness” while making a controlled departure in certain directions. That is precisely the subject addressed by sexually dimorphic characteristics.

1.3 Their Essential Meaning

This judgement requires only approximately 13 milliseconds (Olson & Marshuetz, 2005).

Chapter 2: Sexual Dimorphism—the Theory of Honest Signals

“A peacock’s tail is not used for flight, but for display. Its display is credible because it is costly.” — Amotz Zahavi

2.1 What Are Sexually Dimorphic Characteristics?

A List of Masculine Characteristics

During puberty, rising testosterone promotes the growth of male facial bones in particular directions: stronger brow ridges, a squarer and broader chin, higher and wider cheekbones, a larger nose, more obvious mandibular angles, and deeper eye sockets.

A List of Feminine Characteristics

Oestrogen acts in a different direction: a rounder facial outline, a thinner and narrower chin, fuller lips, larger eyes, a smoother region around the brow ridge, and a more delicate skin texture.

How Testosterone Shapes the Skeleton

Testosterone does not simply “make you more manly.” During puberty, it acts directly upon the growth plates of the facial skeleton. Facial bones differ among individuals in their degree of response, which explains why some people possess high testosterone without displaying especially prominent masculine facial characteristics.

2.2 The Theory of Honest Signals

If masculine features are so “attractive,” why have all men not evolved the most exaggerated square chins and heavy brow ridges? The answer is that the cost is too high.

Testosterone is an immunosuppressive hormone: high levels suppress the operation of the immune system. This means that if a male with high testosterone during puberty lacks a sufficiently strong immune system, he will fall ill repeatedly during development.

In the language of game theory, this is an “honest signal.” Only an individual of sufficiently high genetic quality can bear the immune cost imposed by high testosterone.

The Handicap Principle, proposed by the Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi, provides the best framework for understanding this phenomenon. Masculine facial characteristics are, in essence, the human version of the peacock’s tail.

2.3 Dependence upon Context

Women do not possess one fixed “aesthetic preference,” but adjust their strategy flexibly according to their physiological condition:

Stage Preference Underlying Strategy
Ovulation, with high probability of conception A more masculine face Short-term mating strategy—obtain high-quality genes
Luteal/non-ovulatory phase A softer, more feminine face Long-term mating strategy—find a partner willing to invest resources

2.4 Cross-Cultural Observations

One notable characteristic of East Asian cultures is that they accept soft male faces to a far greater degree than Europe and North America do. Possible reasons include the stronger emphasis agricultural civilisation places upon communal cooperation, the magnified risks of high aggression in densely populated societies, and traditional admiration for “cultivated elegance” and “delicate good looks.”

Every culture is sensitive to sexually dimorphic characteristics, but each culture calibrates a different optimal degree.

Chapter 3: Proportion and Line—the Brain Conserves Energy

“The brain is a lazy organ: its highest principle is not the pursuit of beauty, but the reduction of processing cost.”

3.1 Three Sections and Five Eye-Widths

Three Sections

The three-section principle divides the frontal face vertically into equal thirds: the upper section, from the hairline to the glabella; the middle section, from the glabella to the base of the nose; and the lower section, from the base of the nose to the tip of the chin. Ideally, the three areas are roughly equal in height.

Five Eye-Widths

The five-eye principle divides the frontal face horizontally into five equal parts: facial width is approximately equal to the width of five eyes.

The brain requires no ruler to calculate proportion. Facial recognition involves the fusiform face area, or FFA, which automatically extracts the relative positions and sizes of different parts while processing a face. This process occurs before attention.

3.2 The Truth about the Golden Ratio

The real status of 1:1.618 in facial aesthetics is as follows:

  • It genuinely exists—the proportions of some faces widely regarded as beautiful approach the golden ratio
  • It is not a “formula for beauty”—many highly attractive faces do not conform to the golden ratio

The prominent position of the golden ratio in aesthetic literature is to a considerable extent the result of publication bias.

Processing Fluency Theory

When the brain processes a stimulus, a smooth and effortless process produces a positive aesthetic experience. The advantage of the golden ratio is that 1:1.618 is a simple numerical relation: simple relation = easy for the brain to process = low processing cost = a feeling of “comfort.”

3.3 Smoothness of Line

Three principal forms of smoothness are critical: the outer contour (the complete outline from hairline to chin), the profile line (the S-shaped curve from forehead to chin), and internal transition lines (the transitions among different regions).

As a person ages, full contours are replaced by hollows and angles. The brain may have learned to use “smooth lines” as a proxy for “this person is not old.”

East Asian aesthetics places far greater weight upon smooth lines than European and North American aesthetics do. The preference for a V-shaped face, inward movement of the cheekbones, and filling of the temples all express this tendency.

3.4 Evenness of Skin Tone

If any aesthetic indicator is genuinely universal across cultures, it is that “an even skin tone” is more fundamental than “light skin.”

Preference Cross-Cultural Consistency Evolutionary Logic
Even skin tone Extremely high Even skin tone = no infection = health
Light skin Medium, limited to East Asia Lightness may be associated with signals of social class

The Brain’s Algorithm for Evaluating Facial Attractiveness

Chapter 4: Attention Allocation and Peak Shift—the Contest between Distinctiveness and Risk

4.1 The Effect of Visual Focus, or the Attention-Seeker Principle

The visual system automatically assigns its budget of attention to the most striking characteristic. The greater the difference, the greater the weight.

Positive version: an excellent feature becomes the anchor of the complete face. Negative version: one obvious small defect can similarly absorb all attention.

Facial aesthetics is not a scoring system, but a system of attention allocation.

4.2 The Peak Shift Effect

During development, the brain constructs a statistical template of a “normal human face.” When it calculates the distance between a particular face and the average template, if the departure occurs in a pleasing direction, the departure itself becomes a source of attractiveness.

This produces an inverted U-shaped curve: insufficient departure = too close to average; appropriate departure = peak attractiveness; excessive departure = disgust is triggered.

4.3 The Contrast Effect

A face permits only one feature to stand out—that is distinctiveness. If two or more features stand out together, the face slips from “distinctive” towards “genuinely ugly.”

Distinctiveness = peak shift in one direction, with all other aspects remaining average or better.
Ugliness = simultaneous departure in two or more directions, each competing for attention.

4.4 Baby Schema

Large eyes, a small nose, a round face, and a high forehead—across species, these characteristics trigger an adult desire to protect.

Baby schema + sexually dimorphic characteristics form the strongest combination in aesthetics. This is why the “beautiful youth” type of face appeals to every age group.

4.5 Facial Width-to-Height Ratio and Edge Contrast

A high fWHR, or broad face, is perceived as more dominant; a low fWHR, or narrow face, is perceived as gentler.

Edge contrast refers to the transitional region between hard and soft tissue. Edge contrast suits men, producing strength; a smooth transition suits women, producing softness.

Chapter 5: A First-Principles Question—Where Does Aesthetic Judgement Begin?

5.1 Tracing Upwards

Every aesthetic preference is, in essence, the brain performing an ancient task of detection: assessing genetic quality and the expected return from a potential mate.

The underlying derivation of every aesthetic principle ultimately points towards two drivers: sexual selection, or reproductive value, and survival selection, or survival value.

5.3 Cognitive Spillover

If sexual and survival selection are the final sources of aesthetic judgement, why are people moved by non-living things? What do sunsets, mountains, and symphonies have to do with genetic quality?

This article supports the idea of Cognitive Spillover: the brain’s aesthetic system is a detector that remains continuously online. When it receives no input related to sexual selection, it does not shut down. Instead, it projects its remaining processing capacity upon every object available to perception.

Chapter 6: Foundational Improvement—Complete the Free Steps First

Before spending ten thousand yuan upon your face, reduce your weight to a standard body-fat percentage. This has greater leverage than any medical-aesthetic procedure.

6.1 Body-Fat Percentage—the Greatest Lever

Body-Fat Range Condition of Jawline Overall Facial Appearance
Below 12% Sharp jawline Clearly defined, approaching a model’s appearance
Around 15% Visible jawline Clean and sharp; the best range for most people
18%-20% Unclear jawline Insufficient three-dimensional definition
Above 20% Jawline disappears Puffy appearance

For most Asian men, reducing body fat from 20% to 15% improves facial attractiveness more than any skincare product, hairstyle, or adjustment of clothing.

6.2 Skin Management

Order of priority: sun protection > moisturising > cleansing > acne control > everything else.

The three skincare essentials for men: facial cleanser, specifically an amino-acid cleanser + moisturising lotion/cream + sunscreen, SPF30+.

6.3 Sleep, Stress, and Swelling

Long-term sleep deprivation → increased cortisol → redistribution of facial fat, increased sebum, and reduced collagen synthesis.

6.4 Minimum Standards of Cleanliness

Item Standard
Bathing At least once per day
Oral hygiene Brush for two minutes + floss + clean the tongue
Fingernails Trim short, leaving no more than 1 mm
Nasal hair Not visible outside the nostrils
Lips Neither dry nor peeling

A sense of neatness = the absence of obvious negative details that attract attention.

Chapter 7: Refining the Head—Your Face Can Be Adjusted

The correct combination of hairstyle, eyebrow shape, facial hair, and glasses can approach the effect of surgery upon the soft tissues of the face, at less than one-tenth of the cost.

7.1 Hairstyle

Facial Characteristic Hairstyle Strategy
High forehead/receding hairline Use a fringe or side part for coverage
Long face Retain volume on both sides
Broad/round face Add height on top and tighten the sides
Broad jaw/square face Add volume on top to balance the lower facial section

7.2 Eyebrow Shape

For men: remove stray hairs and give the eyebrows a clear shape. Have them shaped once, then maintain them yourself every week.

7.3 Facial Hair

First principle: if there is insufficient density, do not grow it. A clean shave is ten thousand times better than a sparse beard.

Facial Defect Facial-Hair Strategy
Receding chin Grow a goatee or retain greater length at the chin
Unclear jawline Keep a line of short hair along the edge of the jaw

7.4 Masseter Muscles and Expression

Chewing on only one side over a long period causes facial asymmetry. Record a video upon your phone while behaving naturally: most people will be shocked by their default expression, which looks as though they are angry.

7.5 Glasses

Align the upper edge of the frame with the eyebrows. Above the eyebrows → a strange expression; below them → a tired appearance.

Chapter 8: The Body—the Frame around the Face

Training the lateral deltoid through lateral raises is the single most efficient movement for changing bodily proportions.

Do not overtrain the trapezius—a highly developed upper trapezius shortens the visual length of the neck.

With the head carried 5 cm forward, your face appears 5%-10% heavier than it really is. The simplest correction is to stand against a wall for five minutes every day.

The language of the hands: clean and neatly trimmed nails = “I take care of my own life.”

Chapter 9: Clothing and Environmental Signals

Fit matters more than brand. Spend 500 yuan upon an ill-fitting garment → passers-by will assume it was borrowed.

The three-colour principle: main colour 60% + secondary colour 30% + accent colour 10%. Wear no more than three colours across the complete outfit.

Layering: three layers > two layers > one layer. Every additional layer suggests that you have given the outfit more thought.

Small details: fingernails, nasal hair, shoes, collars, and smell. Each seems unimportant in isolation, but together they form the boundary between a polished and a careless appearance.

Chapter 10: Invisible Weapons

Three levels of scent: cleanliness, the foundation → the smell of laundry detergent, a social signal → perfume, the background music.

Voice: speed, 120-160 Chinese characters per minute; pitch, no rise at the end of a sentence; resonance, chest resonance.

The golden ratio of eye contact: look into the other person’s eyes for 60%-70% of the time while speaking.

The key to a smile is not duration, but timing. Smile when meeting, when hearing something interesting, and when saying goodbye.

Chapter 11: Overall Order of Priority

Complete the free steps first. Before spending any money, finish everything that costs nothing.

Most people skip the first step and rush directly into a medical-aesthetics clinic. They spend the money, but the effect is completely cancelled by a high body-fat percentage and unhealthy habits.

Final Chapter: The Ultimate Answer to Every Principle of Aesthetics

First, aesthetic judgement follows rules. It is not mysticism, but the result of sexual selection, survival selection, cognitive processing, and cultural learning operating together.

Second, rules are meant to produce understanding, not anxiety.
