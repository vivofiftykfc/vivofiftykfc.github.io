Chapter 1: The Past and Present of Eyewear

Have you ever considered that the small object resting upon your nose every day carries more than two thousand years of civilisation? From the first convex crystal lens polished by a craftsman of the Eastern Han dynasty to today’s smart glasses, which can be customised, used to control myopia, and even connected to a network, every stage in the evolution of eyewear reflects humanity’s persistence in pursuing the simple desire to “see more clearly.” Understanding this history not only gives you greater confidence when making a purchase, but also reveals that glasses have never been an ordinary commodity.

1.1 Timeline: From Crystal Lenses to Smart Glasses

Eastern Han—the Earliest Known Prototype of Eyewear

In 1980, a gold ring containing a convex crystal lens was excavated from the Eastern Han tomb of Liu Jing, Prince of Guangling, in Hanjiang County, Jiangsu Province. Approximately 2 cm in diameter and 0.5 cm thick, it could magnify an object four to five times. Archaeology currently recognises it as the earliest prototype of a human “magnifying glass,” dating from almost two thousand years ago. Although it was probably a royal object for amusement rather than daily wear, it demonstrates that Chinese craftspeople had mastered the polishing of crystal lenses as early as the first or second century AD. [Source: archaeological report of the Nanjing Museum]

Eleventh Century—Europe’s “Reading Stone”

In medieval European monasteries, monks used a translucent quartz sphere known as a Reading Stone. They placed it upon a manuscript to magnify the text. This was the earliest European prototype of spectacles.

Southern Song—“Aidai”

The word “aidai” appears in documents of the Song dynasty: “When an old person cannot distinguish fine writing, covering the eyes with this makes it clear.” Lenses were polished by hand from crystal or agate, while frames were made from tortoiseshell, copper, or ivory. This was not simply a magnifying glass, but a genuine visual aid held before the eyes. Zhao Xigu, a scholar of the Southern Song, wrote in Dongtian Qinglu: “Aidai: when an old person cannot distinguish fine writing, covering the eyes with this makes it clear.” This is one of the world’s earliest unambiguous written records of reading glasses.

1268-1289—Modern Spectacles Are Born in Italy

Italian craftspeople—sometimes said to include Salvino D’Armate—joined two convex lenses with a hinge so that they could rest upon the bridge of the nose. The design is regarded as the formal birth of “modern spectacles.” Venice and Florence became early centres of spectacle production.

Late Ming and Early Qing—Sun Yunqiu and History of Mirrors

Sun Yunqiu (1628-1662), a Chinese pioneer of optics, invented a method of eye examination that matched lenses of different powers to the condition of the patient’s vision—an early form of “eye examination and prescription.” His History of Mirrors systematically summarised the manufacture of optical devices and contained “seventy-two methods” for producing lenses, already covering myopic lenses, reading glasses, magnifiers, and other categories. Regrettably, the original work has been lost and survives only in partial quotations.

1784—Franklin’s Bifocals

Benjamin Franklin cut two pairs of lenses in half and joined them, using the upper section for distance and the lower for near vision. He thereby invented the bifocal. This ingenious creation remains a source of inspiration for progressive multifocal lenses.

1825—Correction of Astigmatism

The British astronomer George Biddell Airy produced the first spectacles with cylindrical lenses for the correction of astigmatism. From that point onwards, people with astigmatism no longer had to “make do.”

The 1940s—The Arrival of CR-39 Resin Lenses

The creation of CR-39, Columbia Resin formula number 39, was a revolutionary step in the history of lens materials. It weighed approximately half as much as glass, did not break easily, could be tinted, and was convenient to process. Lenses changed from “fragile heavy objects” into “light everyday products.” CR-39 remains widely used in entry-level lenses today.

The Present—Defocus-Control Lenses, Orthokeratology, Smart Glasses, and 3D-Printed Customisation

Lens technology entered a period of rapid expansion during the twenty-first century:

  • Defocus-control lenses, including HOYA MiyoSmart and Essilor Stellest, use the principle of “peripheral defocus” to slow the progression of myopia among children and adolescents, marking a milestone in myopia control
  • Orthokeratology lenses, or Ortho-K, are worn at night to change the shape of the cornea, providing clear daytime vision without glasses
  • Smart glasses, such as Ray-Ban Meta, integrate cameras, speakers, and AI assistants, transforming glasses from a “tool” into a “wearable device”
  • Custom 3D-printed frames/lenses use three-dimensional scans of an individual’s face to achieve a completely personalised fit

1.2 Four Lines of Evolution

Across more than two thousand years of development, four clear lines of evolution can be identified:

Materials: Crystal → Glass → Resin → Functional Polymers

From natural crystal and agate to manufactured glass, and then to CR-39 resin, polycarbonate, and high-index resin, every improvement in materials has produced a lighter, thinner, and safer experience. Functional polymers can now combine such properties as photochromism, blue-light filtering, and relief from fatigue.

Form: Single Lens → Two Lenses without Temples → Two Lenses with Folding Temples → Modern Frames

From a single handheld lens to two fixed lenses without temples, resembling pince-nez, and then to folding temples and frames resting upon the ears, the ultimate purpose of formal evolution has been to make you almost forget the glasses exist once you put them on.

Function: Reading Alone → Myopia/Astigmatism/Progressives/Control/Intelligence

Spectacles were initially created only for presbyopia. Today they cover single-vision correction of myopia and hyperopia, correction of astigmatism, bifocal and progressive multifocal vision, myopia control for young people, specialised driving lenses, blue-light filtering for screens, and photochromic protection outdoors. One pair may combine several functions and can even take photographs or provide navigation.

Production: Hand Polishing → Mechanisation → Custom Free-Form Surfaces

Production has moved from the laborious hand-polishing of crystal, through mechanised mass production at the end of the nineteenth century, to digital customisation based upon today’s Free-Form technology. Every lens can be optimised pixel by pixel according to your prescription, wearing angle, and frame curvature. Your lenses can exist for you alone.

1.3 Core Insight: Glasses Are Not an Ordinary Commodity

Once you understand this history, one point becomes clear: spectacles cross more than two thousand years and embody the combined wisdom of East and West.

Their optical principles stand at the intersection of geometrical and physiological optics; their materials science involves polymer chemistry and surface engineering; their wearing experience concerns ergonomics and neuroaesthetics; and their appearance upon your face belongs to visual communication and personal image management.

Therefore, when somebody tells you that “glasses are just two pieces of glass and two wires,” you may answer with a smile: behind those “two pieces of glass” lie two thousand years of human exploration.

Let us now enter the true soul of eyewear—the lens. Every historical development described above is ultimately compressed into the thin transparent medium before your nose, less than one centimetre thick.

Chapter 2: Lenses—the Soul of Eyewear

Frames determine style; lenses determine quality. This is almost an iron law within the optical industry. No matter how expensive the frame may be, the transparent disc is what truly determines whether you see clearly, feel comfortable, or suffer tired eyes. This chapter provides an in-depth explanation of every core lens parameter and technology, so that sales terminology can no longer leave you confused.

2.1 Four Core Parameters

Refractive Index

Refractive index is the most frequently discussed lens parameter. It measures the degree to which light bends as it passes through a lens. The higher the index, the thinner the lens required to produce the same corrective power.

Refractive Index Reduction in Thickness Compared with 1.56 Suitable Prescription
1.56 (standard) Baseline 0-300°
1.60 (medium index) Approximately 15-20% thinner 300-500°
1.67 (high index) Approximately 30% thinner 500-800°
1.71 (very high index) Approximately 35% thinner 500-800°
1.74 (ultra-high index) Approximately 40%+ thinner Above 800°

Important principle: the stronger the prescription, the higher the refractive index should generally be. For low and medium prescriptions, however, there is no need to pursue a high index blindly, since an increased refractive index is also accompanied by a lower Abbe number.

Abbe Number—the Critical Parameter Ignored by 90% of People

Named after the German physicist Ernst Abbe, the Abbe number measures the degree of chromatic dispersion in a lens. The higher the value, the lower the dispersion and the better the optical quality. When you view an object with high contrast, dispersion produces coloured fringes around its edges, especially in peripheral vision.

Abbe-Number Range Rating Difference You Can Perceive
>40 Excellent Almost no dispersion
35-40 Good Extremely slight dispersion, unnoticed by most people
30-35 Average Sensitive users may see slight coloured edges
<30 Poor Obvious rainbow fringes around lights at night

A natural conflict exists between refractive index and Abbe number: the higher the index, the lower the Abbe number. A 1.50 lens may possess an Abbe number of 58, while a 1.74 lens generally measures around 33. Your choice is therefore a trade-off: if you want an ultra-thin lens, you must accept some reduction in optical quality. One compromise is that, unless the prescription is especially strong, 1.60, with an Abbe number around 40, often provides the best overall experience.

Light Transmittance

Light transmittance determines how “bright” the world looks through the lens. Uncoated CR-39 transmits approximately 92% of light, with the two surfaces of each lens reflecting about 4% apiece. High-quality coatings can raise transmittance above 98%. Mingyue’s PMC ultra-clear lens achieves an astonishing 98.6%, placing it at the highest level of the industry. [Source: Mingyue Lens official technical white paper]

Every one-percentage-point increase in transmittance produces a perceptible difference in brightness. This explains why coated lenses appear more “transparent” than uncoated ones.

Coatings—the “Skin” of a Lens

Coatings form the most complicated and easily underestimated field in modern lenses. They do not alter corrective power, but determine durability, clarity, and ease of maintenance. See 2.3 Coating Technology for details.

2.2 The Golden Rules for Choosing Refractive Index

Your Prescription Recommended Index Purchasing Advice
0-300° 1.56 or 1.60 If the budget permits, choose 1.60 directly: better material, higher Abbe number, and greater durability
300-500° 1.60 The king of value: the best balance between thickness and optical performance
500-800° 1.67 or Mingyue 1.71 Mingyue 1.71 has an Abbe number of 41, almost ten points higher than the 32 of 1.67
Above 800° 1.74 There is no alternative when thinness is the priority; ZEISS or Essilor is recommended

An Important Reminder

Choosing a smaller frame reduces thickness more effectively than choosing a higher refractive index. A frame 51 mm wide with 1.60 lenses can produce thinner lenses than a 55 mm frame with 1.74 lenses. The reason is simple: edge thickness is directly related to frame width, and the effect is quadratic. If your prescription is strong, therefore, the first step should not be paying more for 1.74, but selecting a moderately sized frame.

2.3 Coating Technology in Detail

The Basic Three, Supplied as Standard

1. Hard Coating
This prevents scratches. Resin lenses are essentially polymers, softer than glass and easily scratched. Through dip coating or vacuum deposition, a hard coating forms a dense hardened layer upon the lens surface and significantly improves resistance to scratching. Every resin lens should be hardened—an unhardened lens will be covered in fine marks within six months.

2. Anti-Reflective Coating, or AR Coating
This is a multilayer coating that reduces light reflected from the lens surfaces. Its functions include reducing glare from lamps and vehicle headlights at night; making the lens look more “transparent,” so that others do not see obvious reflections upon your glasses; and increasing light transmittance. Anti-reflective coatings are multilayer interference systems, generally formed from alternating layers of materials such as silicon dioxide and titanium dioxide. This is the most important coating upon a modern lens.

3. Anti-Smudge Coating / Top Coat
Also known as a top coat or hydrophobic and oleophobic coating, it prevents dust, fingerprints, and oil from easily adhering to the surface, while also simplifying cleaning. Water beads and rolls away instead of leaving a residue. Fluorine-based anti-smudge coatings are currently the mainstream technology.

The truth about lens sales: hardening + anti-reflection + anti-smudge are already basic standard equipment, not “upgrade options.” If a shop charges extra for the three, you should reconsider its professionalism.

Optional Features According to Need

Blue-Light-Filtering Coating
This filters harmful blue light in the 380-445 nm band. It should be considered objectively:

  • When it is needed: daily screen time of at least six hours, obvious visual fatigue, or electronic devices used at night disrupting sleep
  • When it is not needed: ordinary office work + occasional phone use, for which a normal anti-reflective coating is sufficient
  • Note: the base colour is slightly yellow; good blue-light lenses show very little yellowing. Designers and photographers who require high colour accuracy should choose carefully

Photochromic Coating
The lens is transparent indoors but automatically darkens under ultraviolet light, providing two functions in one pair. Mainstream technologies include silver halides and spiropyran compounds. Points to examine: the speed of darkening and clearing, uniformity, and the purity of the colour indoors. Essilor Transitions is currently the world’s most mature brand of photochromic technology.

Ultraviolet-Protection Coating
Ultraviolet damage to the eyes is cumulative: long-term exposure can cause cataracts, macular degeneration, and other eye diseases. High-quality lens materials already provide UV400 protection, blocking more than 99% of UVA and UVB, and do not necessarily require an additional coating. When ordering glasses, simply confirm that UV400 protection is included as standard.

2.4 Functional Lenses

Myopia-Control Lenses for Children and Adolescents

These are designed according to the theory of “peripheral defocus.” Numerous microlenses distributed across the lens allow light to form a clear image upon the central retina while producing a signal of “myopic defocus” in the periphery, thereby inhibiting excessively rapid axial elongation.

Brand Product Principle Reference Price per Pair
HOYA MiyoSmart Array of 396 microlenses 3,000-4,500 yuan
Essilor Stellest 1,021 aspherical microlenses 3,000-4,500 yuan
ZEISS MyoVision Peripheral-defocus design 2,000-3,500 yuan

Important: control lenses require professional fitting and regular review; they are not something you can simply “buy and forget.” At the same time, ensuring two hours of outdoor activity every day is the cheapest and most effective means of controlling myopia. [Source: Chinese Ophthalmological Society, 2022]

Anti-Fatigue Lenses

These are suitable for people aged 30-45 who spend long periods focusing at close range. A small positive addition, generally +0.50D to +0.75D, is placed in the lower half of the lens so that the eyes exert less effort while reading. This is not a progressive lens, but it can relieve accommodative fatigue associated with computer vision syndrome.

Progressive Multifocal Lenses

These are designed for people over forty who possess myopia or hyperopia together with presbyopia. The lens is divided vertically into three regions: a distance zone at the top, a progressive corridor for intermediate vision, and a near zone at the bottom. The real technical content lies in the design of the “progressive corridor”: a wider corridor is more comfortable, but also more difficult to adapt to. This explains the reputation of progressive lenses from HOYA and ZEISS, which have accumulated decades of design and digital-modelling experience in controlling aberrations across the transitional region.

The core principles for selecting progressive lenses are:

  1. Choose a major brand—progressives are the lens category that places the greatest demands upon technical ability
  2. Choose an experienced optometrist—progressives require measurement of more than ten parameters, including near pupillary distance, fitting height, vertex distance, and pantoscopic tilt
  3. Prepare mentally for an adaptation period of three to seven days—an initial “swimming” sensation or peripheral blur is normal

Driving Lenses

These lenses are optimised specifically for driving:

  • ZEISS DriveSafe uses a special coating, Luminance Design®, to improve the perception of contrast under low light, rain, fog, and night conditions, while reducing glare from LED headlights and street lamps
  • Essilor Varilux driving lenses follow a similar technical route and emphasise glare suppression

Suitable users: people who frequently drive at night, are sensitive to glare, or must concentrate upon driving for long periods.

Blue-Light-Filtering Lenses

Choose these according to need rather than following the trend blindly. If you face a screen for more than six hours per day and suffer from visual fatigue or sleep problems, blue-light filtering is an additional protective layer worth considering.

Every brand offers a separate blue-light series, while a blue-light coating may also be applied to a standard lens. The two methods differ in filtering effectiveness and control of base colour. Ask the shop to show you physical samples of both for comparison.

2.5 Brand Matrix

The Three International Giants

ZEISS—the Ceiling of Optics

The German firm ZEISS, founded by Carl Zeiss in Jena in 1846, represents the “ceiling” of global optics. Its advantages in lenses include:

  • Precision measurement: ZEISS i.Profiler Plus is among today’s most precise systems of vision examination
  • Free-Form design: ZEISS pioneered Free-Form technology; the SmartLife series represents its digital customisation
  • Optical design: industry-leading control of aberrations, width of visual field, and optimisation of dynamic vision

Recommended series:

  • SmartLife: ZEISS’s principal line, offering a wide field, low peripheral distortion, and strong value
  • SmartLife Individual: digital customisation, optimised point by point according to the wearer’s parameters
  • DriveSafe: designed for driving at night

Essilor—the King of Coatings

The French company Essilor is the world’s largest lens manufacturer and has now merged with the Italian company Luxottica to form EssilorLuxottica. Its central strength lies in coating technology:

  • Crizal series: Essilor’s signature coating technology. The latest Crizal Rock reaches the highest level in resistance to wear, anti-reflection, anti-smudging, and anti-static performance
  • Crizal Easy: a value-oriented choice with excellent basic protection

Essilor is also a leader in functional lenses. Its Stellest control lenses are supported by a large body of clinical data from around the world.

HOYA—the Progressive Specialist

The Japanese company HOYA possesses almost a century of experience in optical glass. Within spectacle lenses, it specialises in progressive multifocal designs, whose corridor width, aberration control, and speed of adaptation are widely regarded as industry-leading.

HOYA’s MiyoSmart has become an extremely popular product in myopia control for children and adolescents, with global sales exceeding several million pairs. [Source: official global data from HOYA]

Two Leading Chinese Brands

Mingyue Lens—the King of PMC Transmittance

Mingyue is one of the leaders of the Chinese lens industry, and its recent technical progress has been remarkable:

  • The PMC (Premium Multi-Coated) ultra-clear series achieves transmittance as high as 98.6%, almost reaching the theoretical limit
  • The 1.71 series is Mingyue’s proudest product. Between 1.67, with an Abbe number around 32, and 1.74, around 33, Mingyue developed a 1.71 lens with an Abbe number as high as 41, obtaining both “thinness” and “high optical quality.” The technology is advanced even by global standards

If your prescription lies between 500° and 800°, Mingyue 1.71 offers an optimal combination of price and optical quality. This is a common view among many industry professionals and experienced enthusiasts.

Conant—the King of Mass-Produced 1.74

Shanghai Conant is among the few Chinese companies capable of producing 1.74 high-index lenses at scale. Its 1.74 lenses are highly competitive in value, costing approximately one-third to one-half as much as ZEISS 1.74 and allowing users with strong prescriptions to “afford an ultra-thin lens.”

The Value Choice: KOC Solution

The Korean company KOC Solution is one of Asia’s largest lens manufacturers. Its central strength is simple: low prices and stable quality.

  • U2 coating: a basic hardened anti-reflective coating of stable quality, suitable for users with limited budgets
  • U6 coating: adds blue-light filtering to U2 and offers excellent value

KOC is the main recommendation in many small and medium-sized optical shops. If your budget is limited and your prescription moderate, within 500°, it is a reliable option.

Avoiding Traps in the Brand Matrix

Large brands often possess several sub-brands, producing a trap easily overlooked by consumers:

  • Under ZEISS: only ZEISS Vitaline is a reliable entry-level line; exercise caution around other inexpensive lenses described as “under/in partnership with/authorised by ZEISS”
  • Under Essilor: only Essilor Wanxin is a reliable entry-level line
  • Mingyue Lens owns several sub-brands; look specifically for the primary “Mingyue Lens” brand

Other products with foreign-sounding names presented as “German” or “French” brands are often described by industry insiders as “white-bag lenses.” They appear cheap, but are actually low-quality lenses wrapped in a foreign name, without any guarantee of optical quality or coating durability.

Ultimate Advice: Ask for the Original Packaging

Whatever brand you select, always request the original lens packaging once the transaction is complete. It displays:

  • Lens brand and series
  • Refractive index
  • Coating information
  • Anti-counterfeiting code

This is the most direct evidence that the lens you receive is what the seller promised. Some shops substitute a different product: you pay for ZEISS but receive another lens. Do not immediately throw away the packaging. First scan the code through the brand’s official WeChat account or website.

2.7 List of Traps to Avoid

The optical industry suffers from severe information asymmetry. The following traps are the most common in the field of lenses:

1. Avoid “white-bag lenses”
Lenses without brand markings and supplied in plain white paper bags are known in the industry as “white-bag” products. They generally come from unbranded contractors, with unstable optical parameters and coatings of poor durability. Unless the budget is extremely restricted, they are not recommended.

2. Avoid miscellaneous products “under a premium brand”
Large brands often own many sub-brands. Most products described as “a series under XX” or “a collaboration with XX” are merely private-label goods and have no direct relationship with the quality of the major brand. Identify the brand’s central product lines.

3. Avoid the claim that “blue-light filtering solves everything”
Blue-light filtering is useful, but not universal. For ordinary users whose screen time does not exceed four hours per day, the benefit is minimal. Do not sacrifice Abbe number, which is low in many blue-light lenses, or accept serious yellowing solely for “blue-light protection.”

4. Avoid the belief that “a higher refractive index is always better”
This is among the most common errors. A 1.74 lens is thinner than 1.60, but has a lower Abbe number and costs several times as much. For prescriptions below 300°, 1.74 is completely unnecessary; below 500°, 1.60 often provides the best combination of price and optical quality.

5. Avoid “free eye examinations”
Free examinations often involve two problems: outdated equipment and examiners without systematic training. A professional medical eye examination requires 30-45 minutes, including history-taking, objective and subjective refraction, trial wear, and prescription.

6. Avoid ordering online from the prescription power alone
Online glasses may be cheap, but they omit critical parameters such as fitting height, vertex distance, pantoscopic tilt, and face-form angle. Progressive and control lenses should especially be fitted in person. Low-power single-vision myopia may be ordered online, but the seller should still provide a complete parameter-measurement service.

7. Avoid extremely expensive “coating upgrades”
As noted above, hardening + anti-reflection + anti-smudge are already standard and should not attract extra charges. Some shops package free standard features as an “upgrade bundle” costing several hundred yuan. Before ordering, ask which coatings are included in the price and which require additional payment.

8. Avoid failing to inspect the original packaging
This is the most important point: obtain the original lens bags, inspect the brand, index, and coating information, and verify them through the anti-counterfeiting code. This is the most basic protection for the money you spend.

Chapter 3: Frames—the Skeleton and Clothing of Eyewear

Core of the chapter: a frame is not an accessory subordinate to the lenses. It is both an engineering structure supporting the optical system and the outline of the “second face” you wear every day. Choose the wrong frame and even the most expensive lenses are wasted; choose the right one and your face becomes more harmonious, your bearing more distinctive, and your glasses more durable. This chapter examines materials, facial shape, dimensions, and brands to help you identify the most suitable frame quickly.

3.1 Comparison of Materials—Five Mainstream Frame Materials

Frame material determines three things: weight, which influences comfort; elasticity, which influences durability; and texture, which influences aesthetics.

Material Weight Elasticity Corrosion Resistance Price Suitable Users
TR90 Extremely light Good Medium Low Students/daily commuting
Pure titanium Extremely light Medium Excellent High Sensitive skin/business people
β-titanium alloy Extremely light Excellent, recovering after 180° Good Medium-high Sport/unrimmed enthusiasts
Acetate Relatively heavy Poor Good Medium Vintage style/personal expression
Memory titanium alloy Light Excellent Good High Careless users/those who often crush glasses

TR90—the First Choice for Students and Daily Wear

TR90, or Grilamid TR90, is a transparent polyamide developed by the Swiss company EMS and is currently the most common injection-moulded frame material. Its central advantages are extreme lightness + strong elasticity. A complete frame often weighs less than 10 grams and leaves no mark upon the nose even after a full day. It is also impact-resistant, more than twice as strong as ordinary plastic, and highly suitable for sport and daily commuting.

TR90 nevertheless has one weakness: only average resistance to corrosion. Long-term contact with sweat, cosmetics, and hair spray gradually removes the surface coating and exposes the colour of the material beneath. Fading and peeling generally begin after one or two years.

Pure Titanium—the Ultimate Answer for Sensitive Skin and Business Wear

Pure titanium, containing at least 90% titanium, is the “bespoke suit” of frame materials. Extremely light + exceptionally corrosion-resistant + non-allergenic, these three properties make it the only answer for many people with allergies. A large number are allergic to nickel alloys, especially where nose pads and temples touch the skin, whereas pure titanium causes almost no allergic response.

Pure titanium has medium elasticity. It cannot bend as far as β-titanium, but is extremely durable in normal use. Its greatest disadvantage is price: a genuinely pure-titanium frame generally costs 500-1,500 yuan from a Chinese brand, while imported brands such as LINDBERG begin above 3,000 yuan.

β-Titanium Alloy—the Transformer That “Bends 180 Degrees without Breaking”

β-titanium, an alloy of titanium and other metals, is lighter, more elastic, and thinner than pure titanium. Its defining characteristic is that the temples can bend through 180 degrees and still return to their original form. This makes it a first-choice material for sport and rimless frames.

One point requires attention: many frames labelled “titanium alloy” are mixtures of titanium and inexpensive metals rather than genuine β-titanium. A real β-titanium temple carries β-Titanium or Beta-Ti engraved upon its inner surface.

Acetate—a Textured Choice for Vintage Enthusiasts

Acetate, or cellulose acetate, is a thick plastic material pressed from multiple layers of colour. Its texture and lustre far exceed those of ordinary injection-moulded frames. The better the acetate, the more translucent the surface and the richer the layers of colour. Handmade acetate frames from Fukui Prefecture in Japan can achieve semi-transparent gradients and represent the ultimate pursuit of many vintage-eyewear enthusiasts.

Its disadvantages are weight, which leaves pressure marks upon the nose during prolonged wear, and poor elasticity, since it cannot be bent far and breaks relatively easily. It is suitable for intermittent wear—for example, while commuting before changing into a titanium frame at the office—or as a stylistic item in an “eyewear wardrobe.”

Memory Titanium Alloy—a Rescue for Those Who Constantly Crush Their Glasses

Memory titanium is a nickel-titanium alloy, or Nitinol, with a shape-memory effect. After being twisted and deformed, it can recover automatically in warm water or at body temperature. For anyone who regularly sits upon their glasses, pushes them into the bottom of a bag, or rolls over them while asleep, it is almost a rescue.

Memory-titanium frames are, of course, extremely difficult to adjust. Staff in an ordinary optical shop are reluctant to alter them, because the material will “remember” its previous shape. Make sure the fit is adjusted correctly when purchasing.

3.2 Guide to Facial Shapes—The Right Frame Can Reshape the Face

The central aesthetic principle for choosing a frame can be expressed in four words: contrast and balance. Put simply, use the shape of the frame to counter the characteristics of your face. Conflict produces tension; harmonious repetition produces monotony.

The Golden Formula: The Smaller the Decentration, the Thinner the Lens

This is especially important for strong myopia. The formula is:

(Lens width + bridge width - your pupillary distance) ÷ 2 ≤ 3

Your pupillary distance, or PD, is fixed and generally measures about 58-68 mm in adults. Subtract it from the total frame width, meaning lens width + bridge width, and divide the result by two. The result is called decentration. When decentration is no more than 3 mm, the optical centre of the lens remains close to the geometric centre of the frame, producing the thinnest and lightest lens with the best vision.

Facial shape is the beginning, not the end. Whether a frame looks attractive is actually determined by six aesthetic principles operating beneath the surface. This chapter offers no isolated conclusions; it discusses principles alone.

4.1 Principle One: Three Sections, Five Eye-Widths, and the Golden Ratio

The Three Sections—the “Area of Operations” for Glasses

Facial aesthetics possesses an old but exceptionally effective tool: three sections and five eye-widths.

The face is divided vertically into three equal parts. Glasses cross the middle section and cover the most important central third of the face.

The central conclusions are:

  • A higher upper rim → the perceived “beginning of the middle section” moves upwards → the middle section appears shorter → the face looks shorter
  • A lower upper rim → the perceived “beginning of the middle section” moves downwards → the middle section appears longer → the face looks longer

The Golden Position of the Upper Rim

According to research by Zhou et al. (2023) in the journal Computational Design Aesthetics, observers gave the highest aesthetic scores when the upper edge of the frame lay at approximately 20% of the vertical distance from the eyebrows to the eyes.

Why Do We Experience “Beauty”?

Aesthetic research contains one interesting finding: when people view an object regarded as “beautiful,” the ratio between high- and low-frequency components of the brain’s beta waves approaches 0.618, the reciprocal of the golden ratio. The golden ratio may be less a cultural construction than an underlying preference of the human nervous system.

Glasses are a reversible instrument of facial structural engineering. Through three means—geometrical form, colour contrast, and spatial position—they forcibly redistribute the visual weight with which another person observes your face.

The nose pads of European and North American frames tend to be positioned high and narrow. Upon an Asian face, the frame therefore slides downwards.

  • Look for “Asia Fit” or an Asian version
  • High-bridge nose pads solve the problem for a few yuan

Solution: select a frame with longer and more strongly curved temples.

The average PD among Asian people is approximately 58-64 mm, compared with 62-68 mm among Europeans and North Americans.

Conclusions:

  • Asian users with strong prescriptions should avoid oversized frames where possible
  • Small frame with high-index lenses—combine a small frame with a high refractive index

6.3 Four-Step Method for Trying Frames

Step One: View from a Distance—Overall Proportion

Stand approximately one metre from the fitting mirror. Frame width should roughly equal the width across your temples; frame height should not exceed one-third of facial length; and the lower edge should not fall below the wings of the nose.

Step Two: View from Close Range—Relationship with Eyebrows and Eyes

Move to approximately 30 cm from the mirror. The upper edge should run parallel to, or slightly below, the eyebrow line; it should not cover the eyebrows.

Step Three: View from the Side—Lens Thickness

Turn your head through 45 degrees and inspect lens thickness from the side. Strong prescriptions must always be examined from this angle.

Step Four: View in Motion—Dynamic Test

Lower, turn, nod, and shake your head. If the frame slides during the trial, it will only slide more seriously after purchase.

6.4 Five Checks for Comfort

  1. The frame does not touch the cheeks
  2. The temples do not pinch the ears
  3. The frame does not slide when the head is lowered
  4. Both eyes lie at the optical centres of the lenses—after putting on the glasses, the pupils should face the geometric centres of the lenses
  5. The nose pads fit the bridge—silicone pads are more comfortable than hard plastic

6.5 Complete Medical Eye-Examination Process

Stage One: History-Taking

An experienced optometrist first spends five to ten minutes discussing your visual habits, occupational requirements, and medical history.

Stage Two: Objective Refraction

An autorefractor, keratometer, and slit-lamp microscope provide the preliminary data.

Stage Three: Subjective Refraction—the Most Important Stage

Using a phoropter, the optometrist asks you to compare “which is clearer.” The process includes preliminary spherical correction, the red-green duochrome test, precise measurement of astigmatism through the Jackson cross-cylinder, binocular balance, and examination of the dominant eye.

Your role: do not guess which is clearer; simply report what you genuinely perceive.

Stage Four: Trial Wear

Walk while wearing the trial lenses for 10-15 minutes and test both distance and near vision. If anything feels uncomfortable, say so.

Stage Five: Prescription

A complete prescription contains sphere (SPH), cylinder (CYL), axis (AXIS), pupillary distance (PD), and near addition (ADD).

6.6 Avoiding Traps in Eye Examinations

  • ⚠️ Ordering glasses from an autorefractor printout alone = extremely irresponsible
  • ⚠️ Children below twelve must undergo cycloplegic refraction
  • ⚠️ Is the examiner qualified? Only a senior optician or higher qualification permits independent completion of a full medical examination
  • ✅ Advanced positioning equipment is worth the additional cost, including ZEISS i.T2 and Essilor Visioffice

7.4 Correct Habits—Details That Determine Lifespan

Putting On and Removing: Two Hands Are Standard

Always use both hands to put on or remove glasses. One-handed use stretches the temples unevenly and eventually twists the frame.

Storage: A Hard Case Is the Home of Your Glasses

Whenever the glasses are not being worn, place them inside a hard case.

Keep Away from Heat

The most dangerous environments are: beneath a car windscreen, which can exceed 70°C in summer > sauna > bathroom > radiator.

One Final Piece of Unfamiliar Knowledge: The Cloth Is Not for Wiping the Glasses

The real function of an eyeglass cloth is to serve as a cushion wrapped around the glasses inside the case. Every time the lenses are rubbed dry, accumulated dust upon the cloth scratches the coating.

Appendix F: Medical Foundations of the Eye

F.1 Basic Structure of the Eyeball

The eyeball is a precise refractive imaging system. Along the optical path, its principal structures are the cornea, pupil/iris, lens, vitreous body, retina, and optic nerve.

F.2 Common Refractive Errors

Type Cause Method of Correction
Myopia Eyeball too long or corneal curvature too steep Concave lens, or negative sphere
Hyperopia Eyeball too short or insufficient refractive power Convex lens, or positive sphere
Astigmatism Curvature of cornea or lens differs among directions Cylindrical lens
Presbyopia Reduced elasticity of the lens → inadequate accommodation Convex lens, or progressive multifocal

F.3 Quick Reference to Key Medical Terms

  • Dioptre (D): the unit of lens power; 1D = 100 degrees in the Chinese prescription system
  • Pupillary distance (PD): the distance between the centres of the two pupils
  • Accommodation: the ability of the crystalline lens to change shape and focus upon objects at different distances
  • Abbe number: an indicator measuring chromatic dispersion in a lens
  • Peripheral defocus: the state of focus in the peripheral region of the retina
  • Keratometry: the degree of curvature of the corneal surface

F.4 Recommended Frequency of Examination

  • Children, 6-18: comprehensive eye examination every 6-12 months
  • Adults, 18-40: every 1-2 years
  • Middle-aged and older people, 40+: annually, including intraocular pressure and fundus examination
  • People with diabetes: every 6-12 months, including retinal photography
