Reading Guide

This report consists of eleven chapters and an appendix, covering the complete chain from basic physiology to practical training. Chapter One: Exercise Physiology and Medical Foundations

Before formally beginning a fitness journey, understanding how the body’s precise machinery operates is far more important than lifting weights blindly.

1.1 Anatomy of the Musculoskeletal System

The seven major muscle groups are the chest (pectoralis major), back (latissimus dorsi and trapezius), shoulders (the anterior, lateral, and posterior heads of the deltoid), arms (biceps and triceps), legs (quadriceps, hamstrings, and gluteus maximus), core (rectus abdominis and external obliques), and forearms.

Types of Muscle Fibre

Type Contraction Speed Resistance to Fatigue Suitable Training
Type I (slow-twitch) Slow High Endurance training
Type IIa (intermediate) Fast Medium Combined strength and endurance
Type IIx (explosive) Extremely fast Low Heavy strength work and explosive power

1.2 Molecular Mechanisms of Muscle Growth

The Three Principal Drivers of Hypertrophy

  1. Mechanical tension—the strongest signal: heavy compound movements directly activate mechanoreceptors
  2. Metabolic stress—the science behind the pump: high-repetition training accumulates metabolic waste and triggers a cellular-swelling response
  3. Muscle damage—reconstruction produces growth: training creates microscopic tears; after supercompensation, the muscle fibres become thicker and stronger

1.3 Energy Metabolism Systems

Energy System Duration Typical Application
ATP-CP system 6-10 seconds Maximum loads and sprinting
Glycolytic system 30-90 seconds Strength training at 8-15RM
Aerobic oxidative system Several minutes to several hours Low-intensity aerobic exercise

1.4 The Endocrine System

  • Testosterone: the king of anabolic hormones. It rises briefly after heavy compound movements
  • Growth hormone and IGF-1: promote fat breakdown and tissue repair
  • Cortisol: a double-edged sword. A persistently high level → muscle breakdown and fat accumulation
  • Insulin: the manager of nutrient distribution. Greater sensitivity means a lower tendency to store fat

1.5 Adaptation of the Nervous System

During the first month of strength gains, the muscles have not yet grown larger, but have already become stronger—this is neural adaptation.

The mind-muscle connection has a scientific basis—when attention is concentrated upon the target muscle, activation measured by EMG may rise by 10-20%.

Chapter Two: The Underlying Logic of the Lean-Muscular Male Physique

2.1 Definition and Measurement

Body-Fat Range: 10-14%

Body-Fat Range Visual Appearance
10-12% Abdominal definition is visible; facial contours are clear
12-14% Abdominal muscles are faintly visible; clothes produce the best appearance
15-18% Abdominal definition is unclear, but the body looks generally solid

A body-fat percentage below 10% carries costs including reduced testosterone and weaker immunity.

Reference Muscle Mass for a 175 cm Man

A fat-free mass index, or FFMI, of approximately 19-20; upper-arm circumference of 35-38 cm; chest circumference of 95-100 cm.

2.2 Aesthetics of Key Proportions

The V-shaped physique and shoulder-to-waist ratio: a ratio above 1.6 produces an obvious inverted triangle. Increasing shoulder width by 4-5 cm while keeping the waist between 72 and 76 cm is sufficient to reach this standard.

The ideal head-to-shoulder ratio is 2.5-3.0:1. East Asian men commonly measure 2.1-2.4, so shoulder training offers an exceptionally high return.

2.3 Nature versus Training

The skeleton and the shape of muscle bellies are genetically determined, but most people remain far below their limits.

Body-fat percentage, muscular circumference, posture, flexibility, and neuromuscular efficiency can all be changed.

2.4 Metabolic Advantages

A lean-muscular physique possesses greater insulin sensitivity, so carbohydrates enter muscle glycogen stores in preference to fat storage.

Chapter Three: Self-Assessment of Constitution, Body Type, and Posture

3.1 Classification of Body Types

Body Type Characteristics Difficulty of Gaining Muscle
Ectomorph (long and lean) Narrow shoulders and rapid metabolism Difficult
Mesomorph (well proportioned) Broad shoulders and narrow waist Easy
Endomorph (rounded) Broad hips and a tendency to store fat Easy to gain muscle but difficult to lose fat

3.2 Estimating Body-Fat Percentage

Visual comparison + skinfold callipers + bioelectrical impedance—cross-check the result through several methods.

3.3 Postural Assessment

Upper crossed syndrome (rounded shoulders, forward head, and hunched back): stretch the chest and strengthen the upper back.

Lower crossed syndrome (anterior/posterior pelvic tilt): for anterior tilt, stretch the iliopsoas and strengthen the glutes and abdominal muscles; for posterior tilt, stretch the hamstrings and strengthen the iliopsoas.

Order of priority in postural correction: breathing pattern → pelvic position → thoracic mobility → scapular stability.

Chapter Four: Aesthetic Standards for a Lean-Muscular Male Physique—A Detailed Guide by Body Part

4.1 Shoulders—Three-Dimensional Capped Deltoids

The ideal ratio among the anterior, lateral, and posterior heads of the deltoid is 1:1.5:1. The lateral head determines shoulder width, while the posterior head must not be neglected.

4.2 Chest—Square Rather than Rounded

The upper chest is the first priority: it determines the visual effect around the collar of a T-shirt. Avoid excessive development of the lower chest.

4.3 Back—The V-Shaped Inverted Triangle

The latissimus dorsi determines the V-shaped outline, while the middle and lower trapezius and the rhomboids determine thickness. Width first > thickness > detail.

4.4 Arms—Filling the Sleeves

The target upper-arm circumference for a lean-muscular physique is 37-40 cm. The triceps account for two-thirds of upper-arm size and contribute more to “large arms” than the biceps.

4.5 Core—Definition First

Whether the abdominal muscles appear depends upon body-fat percentage; 10-12% is sufficient. The external obliques are easy to “overtrain”—excessive development damages the V-shaped outline.

4.6 Overall Bearing

Muscle alone does not equal attractiveness. An upright and confident person looks far better than a heavily muscled person with rounded shoulders and a hunched back.

Chapter Five: Aesthetic Methods—Scientific Observation and Judgement

Standardised circumference measurements (once per week): shoulders, chest, waist, upper arms, and thighs. Focus particularly upon the shoulder-to-waist ratio.

Standardised photographic protocol (every 2-4 weeks): use the same lighting and pose, taking front, side, and rear photographs.

Trust trends rather than individual points; trust data rather than emotion; trust time rather than shortcuts.

Chapter Six: Preparing for Fitness—From Zero to One

6.1 Health Screening

Complete a routine physical examination before training. Anyone with an old injury should adjust the choice of movements accordingly.

6.2 Setting SMART Goals

Short-term goals should be completely controllable—for example, “complete four training sessions every week without absence.” Accumulate confidence through victories over small goals.

6.3 Choice of Venue

Type Advantages
Commercial gym Wide range of equipment; convenient progressive overload
Dumbbells and barbells at home No commute; one-off investment of 2,000-8,000 yuan
Bodyweight training No cost, but limited opportunities for overload

Chapter Seven: The Training System—Daily Process and Detailed Movements

7.1 Training Splits

Model Frequency Suitable Stage
Full-body training Three times per week Beginner
Upper/lower split Four times per week Novice
Push/pull/legs split Four to six times per week Advanced

7.2 Progressive Overload

This is the principal driver of muscle growth. Increase the training demand each week through weight, repetitions, number of sets, or shorter rest periods.

7.3 The Five Golden Compound Movements

Squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, and pull-up—place them near the beginning of each session.

7.4 Training Intensity

For a lean-muscular route: compound movements at 8-12RM × 3-4 sets, and isolation movements at 12-15RM × 2-3 sets. Rest for 90-180 seconds between compound sets and 60-90 seconds between isolation sets.

Chapter Eight: Customising the Training Programme

8.1 Choosing a Split

Three sessions per week → full-body training; four sessions → upper/lower split; five to six sessions → push/pull/legs split.

8.2 Sample Programme: Four Sessions per Week

Day Content
Monday Upper-body push (bench press, overhead press, lateral raise, triceps)
Tuesday Lower body (squat, Romanian deadlift, leg curl)
Thursday Upper-body pull (pull-up, row, biceps, face pull)
Friday Lower body + assistance (deadlift, split squat, hip thrust, abdominals)

Chapter Nine: Nutrition—the Materials from Which the Body Is Rebuilt

Muscle gain: a caloric surplus of 200 to 300 kcal per day. Fat loss: a caloric deficit of 300 to 500 kcal per day.

Protein: 1.6-2.2 g per kilogram of body weight per day, divided among 3-5 meals.

Recommended supplements: protein powder (★★★★★), creatine (★★★★★, the strongest evidence base), caffeine (★★★★), and vitamin D (★★★★).

Chapter Ten: Integration with Daily Life—Muscle Growth beyond the Gym

Sleep: 7-9 hours; secretion of growth hormone reaches its peak during deep sleep.

Stress management: prolonged high stress → persistently elevated cortisol → muscle breakdown.

Daily posture: check your seated posture every hour—draw the shoulder blades back and tuck the chin slightly. All-day posture matters more than one hour of training.

Chapter Eleven: Advanced Development and Additional Notes

Breaking through a Plateau

Increase volume (total weekly sets +10-20%), change movement variations, schedule a deload week, and optimise nutrition.

Common Misconceptions

❌ “The more I train, the faster I grow”—recovery matters more than training
❌ “Every set must reach failure”—failure increases fatigue and is unnecessary for every set
❌ “Fat can be reduced locally”—it cannot; fat loss occurs throughout the body

A Sustainable Attitude towards Fitness

Fitness is a lifelong practice, not a sprint lasting several months. What matters is long-term average quality, rather than perfection in a single session.
