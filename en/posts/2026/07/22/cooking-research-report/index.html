A Research Report on Cooking

How to Read This Report

The report contains ten chapters. Beginning with a brief history of cooking, it proceeds through standards for a healthy diet, classifications of cooking methods and ingredients, seasoning, culinary logic, general procedures, global cuisines, and deeper questions. The first three chapters establish a conceptual framework; Chapters 4 to 7 address practical methods; and Chapters 8 to 10 provide a cultural perspective and a conclusion.

Chapter 1: A Brief History of Cooking

The history of cooking is, in essence, the history of human civilisation. Every major advance in cooking technology—from the use of fire and the making of pottery to metallurgy and molecular deconstruction—has profoundly altered the human body, social organisation, and culture.

1.1 Before Fire: The Age of Raw Food

Before humans learned to control fire, eating remained entirely within the biological realm. The raw-food era imposed a severe biological constraint on human evolution: the digestive system had to consume substantial energy in processing unmodified food, directly limiting the energy available for the expansion of the brain.

The earliest traces of controlled fire date back approximately 1.5 to 2 million years. Homo erectus achieved stable control of fire around 1 million to 500,000 years ago. The use of fire was the first and most important technological revolution in the history of human cooking.

1.2 The Revolution of Fire

The beginning of cooked food: direct roasting—the simplest method of cooking denatures proteins, melts fat, and hydrolyses collagen. In Catching Fire, Richard Wrangham estimates that cooked food reduced the energy required for digestion by roughly forty per cent, allowing the savings to serve directly as fuel for brain development.

Pottery: the birth of boiling and steaming—at Xianrendong Cave in Jiangxi, China, pottery fragments dating back roughly 20,000 years have been discovered. Pottery gave humanity its first genuine cooking vessels.

1.3 The Culinary Revolution of the Metal Age

The appearance of bronze and iron transformed cooking from merely making food edible into the deliberate art of preparing it. The heat-transfer properties of the iron wok made rapid stir-frying at high temperatures possible, giving rise to the wok hei characteristic of Chinese food culture.

1.4 The Age of Exploration: The Great Exchange of Species

The Columbian Exchange brought about the most far-reaching global reorganisation of food in culinary history. Chillies, tomatoes, potatoes, maize, and peanuts travelled from the Americas to the Old World. The chilli in Sichuan cuisine, the tomato sauce on Italian pasta, and the potato as a European staple all originated in this exchange.

1.5 The Industrial Revolution and Modern Cooking

The invention of canning, refrigeration, and freezing freed food from the constraints of season and region. Molecular gastronomy, meanwhile, has carried the scientific dimension of cooking to an extreme by treating the physical and chemical principles of food as creative instruments.

The central line of development is that methods of using energy determine methods of cooking. Fire gave rise to roasting, pottery introduced steaming and boiling, the iron wok made high-temperature stir-frying possible, and electricity and gas turned precise temperature control into an everyday practice.

Chapter 2: Why We Eat and What Constitutes a Healthy Diet

2.1 Three Dimensions of Eating

  1. Survival: obtaining energy and nutrients to sustain life.
  2. Pleasure: experiencing flavour and emotional satisfaction, which constitute the cultural value of food.
  3. Society: sharing meals as a central ritual for maintaining relationships.

2.2 Macronutrients

Nutrient Proportion of energy Main sources
Carbohydrates 50%-60% Grains, tubers, and legumes
Protein 15%-20% Meat, eggs, dairy products, and soybean products
Fat 20%-30% Cooking oils, nuts, and animal fat

2.3 A Broad Consensus on Healthy Eating

Different authoritative guidelines—including the 2022 Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents, the Mediterranean diet, the DASH diet, and Harvard’s Healthy Eating Plate—display a remarkable degree of agreement: base the diet mainly on natural foods, fill half the plate with vegetables and fruit, one quarter with whole grains, and one quarter with high-quality protein, while consuming a moderate amount of healthy fat.

2.4 Individual Adjustment

The individual dimension of dietary standards is often neglected. A marathon runner and an office worker have entirely different requirements. A genuinely healthy diet does not consist in blindly following one model, but in making flexible adjustments to one’s own circumstances within a framework of scientific consensus.

Chapter 3: Classifying Cooking Methods by Heat-Transfer Medium

Classifying cooking methods according to the medium of heat transfer is more explanatory and universally applicable than classifying them by cuisine or ingredient.

Heat-transfer medium Maximum temperature Typical methods Characteristics
Air (dry heat) Up to 250°C+ Roasting, grilling, and baking Surface dehydration and Maillard reactions
Water (moist heat) 100°C Boiling, stewing, and blanching Gentle heating and preservation of natural flavour
Steam 100°C Steaming Constant temperature and maximum nutrient retention
Oil Up to 180°C+ Pan-frying, deep-frying, and stir-frying Maillard reactions, caramelisation, and crispness
Solid metal High Griddle cooking and pan-frying Direct heat transfer and roasted aroma
Radiation High Microwave and infrared heating Rapid and internal heating
Combined two-stage methods Various Searing followed by stewing, among others Developing aroma before tenderising

The practical value of this system lies in a simple fact: once the relationship between medium and temperature is understood, the cooking logic of any dish can be analysed and inferred.

Why is red-braised pork first stir-fried and then stewed? Oil is used first to produce Maillard reactions and create a roasted meat aroma; water is then used to hydrolyse the collagen thoroughly. Why does a steamed fish require only seven or eight minutes? Steam at a constant 100°C allows the protein to set without excessive contraction.

Chapter 4: Categories and Representative Types of Ingredients

4.1 Grains and Staple Foods

Grains provide the foundation of dietary energy. Rice, wheat, maize, oats, and miscellaneous cereals differ markedly in starch gelatinisation temperature, protein content, and glycaemic index.

4.2 Vegetables and Fruit

Vegetables and fruit supply vitamins and dietary fibre. They may be classified by edible part into leafy vegetables, roots and tubers, fruits, fungi, and algae. Their water content, fibre structure, and suitability for particular methods vary enormously: leafy vegetables suit rapid stir-frying, roots and tubers suit stewing, and mushrooms readily absorb surrounding flavours.

4.3 Meat and Aquatic Products

Meat and aquatic products contribute high-quality protein and fat. The direction of muscle fibres, quantity of connective tissue, and distribution of fat in each cut determine whether it is best fried, stewed, or roasted.

4.4 Legumes and Nuts

These plant foods, rich in protein and fibre, provide an important supplementary source of protein in Chinese cuisine.

4.5 Seasonings and Spices

Seasonings carry cultural memory and encode flavour. Behind every seasoning stands a complete logic of taste.

Chapter 5: The Function of Seasoning

Seasoning is science rather than mysticism. Saltiness arises when sodium ions activate taste buds; sourness when hydrogen ions stimulate taste cells; umami from the synergy between glutamate and flavour-enhancing nucleotides; sweetness when sugars bind to receptors; and pungency when capsaicin activates the pain receptor TRPV1. Every taste has an identifiable physiological mechanism.

The essence of “harmonising the five flavours” is to make these signals form a layered, progressive, and balanced experience in the mouth.

Different civilisations have developed distinctive systems of seasoning:

  • Chinese cuisine: spring onion, ginger, garlic, and soy sauce, with an emphasis on wok hei and compound seasoning.
  • French cuisine: butter, cream, and herbs, pursuing emulsification and refined sauces.
  • Indian cuisine: combinations of spices whose timing constructs successive layers of flavour.
  • Japanese cuisine: dashi, soy sauce, and mirin, honouring the simplicity of an ingredient’s natural taste.

Chapter 6: The Logic of Selecting a Cooking Method

A dish = raw material × processing method × cultural context

The same piece of chicken becomes kung pao chicken in China, coq au vin in France, green chicken curry in Thailand, and oyakodon in Japan. An ingredient is merely the starting point; processing and cultural context are what truly define a dish.

The three-dimensional logic of culinary choice:

  1. Properties of the ingredient: cut, fibre direction, water content, and fat distribution.
  2. Matching the heat transfer: should aroma be sealed in or fibres softened? Is crispness or tenderness desired?
  3. Cultural tradition: systems of seasoning and habits of eating.

The principle of best fit is to choose the method that maximises an ingredient’s advantages and minimises its weaknesses.

Chapter 7: A General Cooking Procedure

7.1 Preparation

Selection → washing → cutting → marinating. Cutting determines the surface area exposed to heat and the speed of cooking, while marinating introduces flavour in advance and changes texture.

7.2 Cooking

Control of heat is the central technique of cooking. Rapid stir-frying over high heat retains moisture, while slow cooking over low or medium heat softens fibres. Maillard reactions require a surface temperature above 120°C—which is why a steak needs a brown crust and a fish must not be steamed for too long.

7.3 Finishing

The final stage includes seasoning, adding salt at the proper moment, thickening a sauce, plating, and garnishing. It determines the completeness of the finished dish.

Chapter 8: Food in Culture—A Tour of Global Cuisines

8.1 Chinese Cuisine: Wok Hei and Compound Flavours

The eight major regional cuisines each possess a distinctive system of flavour. Chinese cuisine seeks the integration of colour, aroma, taste, and form, supported by a complete range of techniques including stir-frying, flash-frying, steaming, boiling, stewing, and braising.

8.2 French Cuisine: The Art of Sauce

With butter, cream, and red wine as common foundations, sauce is the soul of French cuisine. From the five mother sauces to modern lighter alternatives, French cooking pursues refinement and layers of flavour.

8.3 Italian Cuisine: The Purity of Simplicity

Italian cuisine emphasises the natural taste of ingredients, with tomatoes, olive oil, cheese, and pasta at its centre. Italians believe that “good ingredients do not require complicated cooking.”

8.4 Japanese Cuisine: Natural Taste Taken to Its Limit

Dashi, soy sauce, and mirin form the seasoning framework of Japanese cuisine, which honours the idea of shun: ingredients at the height of their season. Sashimi and sushi demonstrate a pursuit of the ingredient’s original taste.

8.5 Indian Cuisine: A Symphony of Spices

Curry is a compound system of spices. Layers of flavour are built by controlling the sequence in which whole spices are toasted and powdered spices are added.

8.6 Thai Cuisine: A Balance of Sourness, Sweetness, and Heat

Fish sauce, coconut milk, chilli, and lime create a balance of sourness, heat, and umami, revealing the vitality of tropical ingredients.

8.7 The Mediterranean Diet: A Model of Health

Olive oil, seafood, vegetables, and grains define the Mediterranean diet, whose cardiovascular benefits have been supported by extensive research.

Chapter 9: Further Reflections—Deeper Questions

9.1 The Relationship between Cooking and Health

Cooking methods significantly affect nutrient retention. Steaming preserves more water-soluble vitamins than boiling: broccoli steamed for three minutes retains roughly eighty per cent of its vitamin C, while boiling for five minutes reduces the figure to about fifty-five per cent.

9.2 The Science of Molecular Gastronomy

Molecular gastronomy employs physical and chemical principles as culinary tools. Spherification, foams, sous-vide cooking, and rapid freezing with liquid nitrogen reconstruct the form and texture of food.

9.3 The Art of Fermentation

Fermentation is the advance processing of ingredients by microorganisms. It produces complex flavours, improves nutrition, and extends shelf life, from soy sauce and pickles to cheese and sourdough bread.

9.4 Kitchenware and Flavour

The wok hei of an iron wok, the mellow taste of soup from an earthenware pot, and the even reduction of sauce in a copper pan illustrate how cookware with different thermal conductivities produces measurable effects on flavour.

9.5 Sustainable Eating

Reducing food waste, choosing seasonal local ingredients, and adopting a more plant-based diet all reflect the fact that dietary choices are environmental choices as well.

Chapter 10: Conclusion and Appendix

10.1 Review of the Central Findings

The evolution of cooking is a miniature history of human civilisation. The way energy is used determines the way food is cooked.

There is no single standard for a healthy diet, but there is a broad direction of agreement. Prefer natural ingredients, with half a plate of vegetables and fruit, one quarter whole grains, and one quarter protein.

Classification by heat-transfer medium is the most fundamental system for classifying cooking. The relationship between medium and temperature determines an ingredient’s final texture.

A dish = raw material × processing method × cultural context.

Seasoning = the intersection of chemistry, physiology, and cultural tradition.

The best cooking = understanding ingredients + mastering technique + empathising with the diner.

Instead of memorising recipes mechanically, it is better to grasp the underlying principles. Understand why overnight rice makes better fried rice—retrogradation leaves its surface drier and the grains easier to separate—learn the principle of emulsification and you can prepare a stable salad dressing, and remember that Maillard reactions require a temperature above 120°C and you can sear an attractive crust on a steak. Cooking empowered by knowledge is genuine freedom.

The ultimate purpose of cooking is not to display technique, but to give the diner pleasure and satisfaction. Understanding ingredients, mastering methods, and recognising needs form a three-dimensional model of ability that applies equally to the home cook. Begin by understanding “why,” and every occasion on which you cook will become different.
