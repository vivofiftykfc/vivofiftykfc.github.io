An Investor's Cognitive Training Manual

This cognitive training manual is intended for individual investors. It begins with the foundations of finance, proceeds through the logic of market operation, asset allocation, and behavioural finance, and concludes with an executable twelve-month action plan. The main text remains available here as a web-readable Markdown version, while a compiled PDF can be downloaded below and at the end of the article.

Source note: this manual originated in a personal investment cognition project, and the PDF is its compiled output.

How to Read This Manual

The manual contains fifteen chapters and an appendix, covering the entire path from the formation of sound judgement to practical execution. The preface and Chapters 1 to 4 establish the conceptual foundation; Chapters 5 to 11 construct a methodology; Chapters 12 and 13 address the distinctive features of the Chinese market and provide a practical toolkit; and the final chapter presents an actionable twelve-month plan. Readers are advised to proceed in order and return to the final chapter for review after twelve months.

An Investor’s Cognitive Training Manual

Preface: Why You Must Begin Investing Now

You may believe that keeping money in a bank is safe. In reality, your purchasing power is evaporating at roughly three per cent a year. Thirty years from now, one hundred yuan today will purchase goods worth less than forty yuan at present prices. This loss will never appear on a bank statement, yet it is taking place all the same.

If the average growth rate of China’s M2 over the past twenty years were used to measure monetary dilution, the figure would be far more alarming. Even under the mildest assumption about inflation, the conclusion is already clear: leaving all your money in the bank guarantees that you will become poorer in real terms.

Most people avoid investment for one reason: they fear losses. This fear conceals a major cognitive trap: you equate “not losing money” with “being safe.” One hundred yuan in the bank may always remain one hundred yuan in nominal terms, but its value lies in what it can buy. Measured by value rather than denomination, your supposedly safe deposit is suffering a continuous and invisible loss of blood.

This leads to the first proposition of the book: investment is not something to consider only after you have become wealthy. On the contrary, it is precisely because you are not yet wealthy enough that you need investment more urgently than anyone else.

Chapter 1: The Systematic Framework of Economics and Finance

To understand investment, one must first understand how money operates. This chapter establishes the underlying framework of finance: money, interest rates, inflation, credit, risk, and return. Together, these concepts form the basic coordinates of the investment world.

The risk-free interest rate is the anchor of pricing: the expected returns of all assets fluctuate around it. A risk premium is the compensation you receive for bearing additional risk. Once these two ideas are understood, the pricing logic of nearly every financial product becomes intelligible.

Chapter 2: A Brief Financial History—There Is Nothing New Under the Sun

From tulip mania to the South Sea Bubble, and from the Great Depression of 1929 to the subprime mortgage crisis of 2008, financial markets have repeated similar patterns of frenzy and collapse. There is nothing new under the sun.

The lesson of history is that human greed and fear have never changed. Every bubble is accompanied by the declaration that “this time is different,” while every crisis marks the eventual outcome of unchecked greed. Understanding financial history provides investors with their first defence against collective mania.

Chapter 3: The Underlying Logic of Financial Markets

What drives a market? In the short term, sentiment; in the medium term, capital; and in the long term, fundamentals.

In the short term, prices are determined by marginal buying and selling pressure—sentiment, capital flows, and news dominate.
In the long term, prices return towards value—a company’s profitability and capacity for growth ultimately determine its share price.

Once you understand this separation of time scales, you will no longer be anxious about every day’s rise and fall, nor will you ignore a deterioration in fundamentals.

Chapter 4: The First Principles of Investment

Investment essentially means exchanging money today for greater purchasing power in the future. Reduced to first principles, only three variables remain:

Principal × Rate of Return × Time = Final Wealth

  • Principal: how much you can accumulate depends on your savings rate rather than your income alone.
  • Rate of return: sustained excess returns are extremely difficult to obtain; the market’s average return is already excellent.
  • Time: the only friend of compound growth, and the variable for which most people are least willing to wait.

Follow the principle of the circle of competence: invest only in assets you genuinely understand. This circle expands brick by brick; it cannot be drawn around an unfamiliar field overnight.

Chapter 5: Know Yourself

The first obstacle in investment is not the market but the investor. This chapter uses three tools for self-diagnosis:

  1. Personal balance sheet: assets minus liabilities equal net worth; first establish where you stand.
  2. Monthly cash-flow statement: how much you earn, spend, and retain. Those who do not know their income and expenditure will never save consistently.
  3. Risk-tolerance assessment: answer honestly what you would do after a twenty per cent loss, recalling how you actually reacted to losses in the past.

Your risk tolerance is the position you can continue to hold even when anxiety keeps you awake at night. Beyond that threshold, no expected return is worthwhile.

Chapter 6: What Are You Actually Buying When You Purchase a Share?

Buying a share does not mean buying a ticker symbol; it means acquiring partial ownership of a company.

Once this is understood, your investment framework shifts from “predicting whether the price will rise or fall” to “assessing the value of a business.” Is the company profitable? Where is its competitive moat? Is the management trustworthy? Is the current price high or low in relation to intrinsic value?

A share = part of a business + a tradable certificate whose price fluctuates. The former concerns investment; the latter invites speculation. Decide which source of profit you are actually pursuing.

Chapter 7: The Neglected Half of the Investment World

Shares constitute only half of the investment world. Bonds, money-market funds, gold, property, and insurance each possess distinctive characteristics of risk and return, and each performs a different function within a portfolio.

Bonds provide stability and defence, shares provide growth and offence, and cash provides flexibility and security. A complete understanding of investment requires attention to every major asset class rather than shares alone.

Chapter 8: Funds and ETFs—Why You Probably Should Not Select Individual Shares

For the overwhelming majority of people, active stock selection is a negative-sum game. Individual investors are comprehensively disadvantaged in information, expertise, and emotional control.

The solution lies in index funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Obtaining the market’s average return at the lowest possible cost is already enough to outperform most professional investors.

  • Broad-market indices, such as the CSI 300 and CSI 500, represent the market as a whole.
  • Regular investment in index funds is the simplest and one of the most effective ways for ordinary people to participate in the market.
  • Admitting that “I do not know which share will rise” is precisely where rationality begins.

Chapter 9: Asset Allocation—the Only Free Lunch in Investment

Asset allocation is the only free lunch in investment: it can improve the stability of returns without increasing risk.

Core-satellite structure:

  • Core holdings (60–80%): broad-market indices held for the long term.
  • Satellite holdings (20–40%): supplementary assets intended to seek excess returns.

Rebalancing means periodically selling assets that have risen more and buying those that have risen less, thereby restoring the original allocation. Over the long run, rebalancing is an important source of return: it compels investors to sell high and buy low without relying on forecasts.

A classic but rough formula is: the proportion allocated to equities ≈ 100 minus your age.

Chapter 10: Behavioural Finance—You Are Your Own Greatest Enemy

Research by Kahneman and Tversky demonstrates that the pain of losing one hundred yuan is roughly two to two and a half times as intense as the pleasure of gaining the same amount.

Loss aversion, chasing rising prices and selling after declines, overconfidence, anchoring, and herd behaviour are cognitive biases that make “buying low and selling high” contrary to human instinct. Half of the work of investment takes place in financial markets; the other half consists in resisting one’s own brain.

The answer is to use systems to restrain emotion. Automate regular investment, rebalance according to fixed rules, and keep an investment journal that records the genuine reason behind every decision. In other words, confine emotion within an institutional framework.

Chapter 11: An Investment System—Building Your Personal Investment Operating System

Scattered insights must be assembled into an executable investment system. A complete system contains four elements:

  1. Goal setting: specific and measurable financial objectives.
  2. Strategy selection: regular investment, a core-satellite structure, or rebalancing.
  3. Discipline in execution: fixed times, fixed amounts, and fixed instruments.
  4. Review and iteration: an investment journal, an annual review, and continuous learning.

Investment without a system is gambling; investment with a system deserves its name. The value of a system is that it tells you what to do under any market conditions.

Chapter 12: The Distinctive Features of the Chinese Market and a Guide to Avoiding Traps

The Chinese market follows some distinctive rules. Only by understanding them can investors avoid its characteristic traps.

  • Dominance of individual investors: fluctuations are greater and sentiment has a stronger influence.
  • Policy-driven features: policy significantly affects the market, requiring attention to macroeconomic conditions and policy signals.
  • Information environment: noise, rumours, and imitation are widespread.
  • Typical traps: fraudulent stock recommendations, peer-to-peer lending schemes, simulated trading platforms, and liquidation caused by leverage.

In the Chinese market, defence must come before offence. Survive first; seek profit afterwards.

Chapter 13: A Toolkit and a Path towards Lifelong Learning

This chapter provides a practical toolkit: selecting a brokerage, establishing a rhythm for gathering information, interpreting macroeconomic indicators such as the PMI, CPI, and aggregate social financing, and choosing classic introductory books.

A rhythm for consuming information: decide what to read daily, weekly, and monthly. Establish a stable framework instead of allowing fragmented information to carry you away.

Introductory reading list: Money Has No Smell, The Simple Things in Investment, and The Intelligent Investor, especially Chapters 8 and 20.

Final Chapter: Your Twelve-Month Action Plan

Among Chinese individual investors, more than sixty per cent stop trading within one year of opening a securities account. What they lack is not knowledge, but a sequence of actions that can actually be carried out.

Month 1: Lay the foundation—finish this book, complete the self-assessment, open a simulated account, and build an information dashboard.

Months 2–3: Take the first step—open a real account, make your first purchase of a broad-market ETF, preferably a CSI 300 ETF, begin an investment decision journal, and establish regular investment.

Months 4–6: Build the system—construct a core-satellite portfolio, conduct your first rebalancing, and read your first advanced book.

Months 7–12: Refine the practice—decide whether to expand your holdings, giving priority to sector ETFs over individual shares and limiting any single share to ten per cent of the portfolio; establish an annual rebalancing plan; read two or three books; and join a learning community.

Six Core Principles of This Book

Principle One: Defend before you attack. Insurance > savings > investment.
Principle Two: Admit what you do not know. You cannot predict every rise or fall; the only things you can control are allocation, cost, and emotion.
Principle Three: Discipline defeats intelligence. Persisting with a strategy that is merely sound is better than repeatedly switching among supposedly perfect alternatives.
Principle Four: Time is your friend; leverage is your enemy.
Principle Five: Record, review, and iterate. An error that is not recorded has been wasted.
Principle Six: Investment exists to support a better life, not the reverse.

Return to this chapter after twelve months and examine your progress. If you have completed every step, you will be capable of independently assessing the macroeconomic climate, constructing your own portfolio, and reviewing a full year of decisions. You will already belong among the most rational ten per cent of participants in this market—not because of how much you know, but because you possess a system, discipline, records, and the habit of reflection.

Welcome to that rational ten per cent. The road remains long, but you are already moving in the right direction.
