Radar Signal Processing

A Complete Beginner’s Guide

Intended audience: EE students with little or no prior knowledge
Coverage: Chapter 1, Introduction to Radar Systems, through Chapter 10, Representative Radar Systems
Last updated: June 2026

Chapter 1: Introduction to Radar Systems

Chapter overview: Have you ever wondered why bats do not crash into cave walls in complete darkness? How can an airport control tower know that an aircraft tens of kilometres away is approaching? How can a weather service predict the location of a typhoon? All three questions point to the same technology: radar. This chapter begins at the very beginning and leads you into the world of radar.

1.1 What Is Radar?

1.1.1 Beginning with a Bat

Imagine walking into a completely dark room with your eyes covered. Could you tell whether there was a wall ahead? You could not. A bat, however, could.

How does it manage this? It emits a high-frequency squeal from its mouth, too high for human ears to hear. The sound travels through the air, strikes a wall or a moth, and bounces back. By listening to the returning echo and measuring how long it takes to arrive, the bat determines the distance and direction of the object ahead.

Core idea: send out a “sound,” listen for the “echo” that bounces back, and use it to find the target.

Radar works in exactly the same way as a bat, except that:

  • a bat uses sound waves, whereas radar uses electromagnetic waves (radio waves);
  • a bat’s “call” has a frequency of tens of thousands of hertz, whereas radar “calls” range from millions to tens of billions of hertz;
  • a bat can “hear” across tens of metres, whereas radar can “see” across thousands of kilometres.

1.1.2 Where Does the Name RADAR Come From?

RADAR is an acronym for RAdio Detection And Ranging.

Breaking the name down:

Word Meaning In plain language
Radio Radio waves It uses electromagnetic waves, not sound waves
Detection Detection It finds out whether a target is present
And And The two functions are performed together
Ranging Range measurement It measures how far away the target is

1.1.3 What Can Radar Do?

Radar has three fundamental capabilities:

  1. Detection: Is there anything there?
  2. Ranging: How far away is it?
  3. Velocity measurement: How fast is it moving?

More advanced radars can do even more: form images with synthetic aperture radar (SAR), identify the type of target—for example, distinguish a fighter from an airliner—and track a target’s trajectory.

1.1.4 Radar in Everyday Life

Do you think radar is found only in the military? In fact, it is everywhere:

  • Air traffic control: monitors aircraft positions and prevents collisions;
  • Weather radar: detects the location and intensity of rainfall and typhoons;
  • Automotive radar: supports reversing sensors and adaptive cruise control, usually with millimetre-wave radar;
  • Police speed measurement: measures vehicle speed with Doppler radar;
  • Remote-sensing satellites: observe Earth in all weather using synthetic aperture radar (SAR).

In one sentence: radar actively transmits electromagnetic waves, receives their echoes, and extracts target information from them.

1.2 A Brief History of Radar

The point of studying history is not to memorize dates. It is to understand what problems gave birth to a technology.

1.2.1 The Discovery of Electromagnetic Waves: A Prerequisite

Before radar could exist, people first had to know that electromagnetic waves existed.

Timeline of key figures:

Year Person Contribution
1831 Faraday Discovered electromagnetic induction: a changing magnetic field produces an electric field
1865 Maxwell Predicted electromagnetic waves with a system of equations—the beauty of mathematics!
1887 Hertz First confirmed the existence of electromagnetic waves experimentally—the starting point of radar
1897 Marconi Achieved transatlantic radio communication

Key point: Hertz’s experiment is often regarded as the eve of radar’s birth. He showed that electromagnetic waves could be transmitted, propagated, reflected, and received—the four fundamental stages of radar.

1.2.2 The “Prehistory” of Radar, 1904–1934

Long before the word “radar” appeared, people had already tried to detect objects with radio waves:

  • In 1904, the German engineer Hulsmeyer invented the telemobiloscope, an instrument that used radio waves to detect ships on rivers. It was effectively the world’s first radar prototype. The technology of the day was too crude, however, and it attracted little attention.

  • In 1922, Taylor and Young of the US Naval Research Laboratory (NRL) noticed changes in a radio signal when a ship passed between a transmitter and receiver during a communication experiment. They realized that radio waves could be used to detect ships.

  • In 1925, Breit and Tuve measured the height of the ionosphere with pulsed radio waves. In essence, this was radar ranging.

1.2.3 The Birth of Radar Proper: Chain Home, 1935–1939

This was the most important turning point in radar history.

Background: Europe grew increasingly tense in the 1930s. Britain feared German bombers and urgently needed a way to detect hostile aircraft in advance.

One person was central to the answer: Robert Watson-Watt.

In February 1935, Watson-Watt submitted a famous memorandum to the British Air Ministry entitled The Detection of Aircraft by Radio Methods. It established that detecting aircraft with radio waves was feasible.

The Daventry experiment, 26 February 1935: at Daventry, Watson-Watt used a BBC short-wave broadcast transmitter with a wavelength of 49 metres as the signal source and successfully detected a Heyford bomber eight kilometres away. This was the first radar detection of an aircraft in human history.

Chain Home:

  • Watson-Watt began leading the construction of Chain Home in 1936;
  • by the outbreak of the Second World War in 1939, twenty radar stations had been deployed along Britain’s east coast;
  • operating frequency: 22–55 MHz, in the HF/VHF bands;
  • detection range: about 200 kilometres;
  • antenna height: 81.5 metres, roughly as tall as a thirty-storey building.

Why did Chain Home matter?
During the Battle of Britain, Chain Home gave the Royal Air Force about twenty minutes’ warning of the direction, distance, and number of incoming German aircraft. Britain could therefore use its limited fighter force to intercept the enemy as effectively as possible. Churchill said that the radar on which Britain depended during the war had been born in Watt’s mind.

1.2.4 Rapid Development during the Second World War, 1939–1945

War accelerates technological development. Radar advanced at an explosive pace during the Second World War.

In the United States:

  • In 1937, the brothers Russell and Sigurd Varian invented the klystron, which could generate high-power microwave signals.
  • SCR-268, introduced in 1938, was one of the earliest US military radars and guided searchlights.
  • SCR-270/271, introduced in 1940, was a long-range early-warning radar. An SCR-270 detected Japanese aircraft before the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, although the contact was unfortunately mistaken for friendly aircraft.

The MIT Radiation Laboratory, or Rad Lab:

  • Founded in 1940, it developed more than one hundred radar systems during roughly five years of wartime operation.
  • It developed microwave radar, including systems with wavelengths of ten centimetres or less in the X band.
  • It developed moving-target indication (MTI), which distinguishes moving targets from stationary ones.

Other countries:

  • Germany also had radar systems, including Freya and Würzburg, but overconfidence kept it from building a complete early-warning network like Britain’s.

1.2.5 From the Postwar Era to the Present

The 1950s:

  • The concept of synthetic aperture radar (SAR) emerged, allowing radar to form high-resolution, photograph-like images.
  • Radar became central to air-defence systems.

The 1960s and 1970s:

  • Phased-array radar appeared. Instead of mechanically rotating an antenna, it steered the beam electronically.
  • A representative system was the US AN/FPS-85 phased-array radar.

The 1980s and 1990s:

  • Digital signal processing (DSP) and FPGAs greatly increased radar processing capability.
  • Pulse-Doppler radar matured and could detect moving targets amid strong clutter.

From the 2000s to the present:

  • Active electronically scanned arrays (AESAs) give each antenna element its own transmit/receive module.
  • Cognitive radar adapts its operating parameters to the environment.
  • Frontier directions include terahertz radar and quantum radar.

1.2.6 A Brief History of Radar in China

Year Milestone
1952 Nanjing Research Institute No. 14 was founded, becoming the cradle of China’s radar industry
1953 China’s first radar was developed
1964 The Type 7010 radar became China’s first large phased-array early-warning radar, with a detection range of 3,000 km

The lesson of this history: radar has always advanced in three directions—seeing farther, seeing more clearly, and seeing faster. The trend continues today.

1.3 Fundamental Principles of Radar

We now come to the heart of the subject. How does radar work? Let us take it apart step by step.

1.3.1 Ranging: Radar’s Most Basic Capability

Everyday analogy:
You shout “Ah!” in a valley and hear an echo a little later. If you know the speed of sound, about 340 metres per second, and measure the interval between the shout and the echo, you can calculate how far away the mountain is.

For example, if the echo returns after two seconds, the sound has travelled 340 m/s × 2 s = 680 metres on its outward and return journeys. The mountain is therefore 340 metres away.

Radar ranging works in exactly the same way:

$$
R = \frac{c \cdot T}{2}
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning Unit Explanation
$R$ Target range metre (m) Distance from the radar antenna to the target
$c$ Speed of light metre per second (m/s) Propagation speed of electromagnetic waves, about $3 \times 10^8$ m/s
$T$ Round-trip time second (s) Interval from transmission to echo reception
Division by 2 Halving the round trip — The wave travels twice the range: outward and back

Question: A radar receives an echo 100 microseconds, or $100 \times 10^{-6}$ seconds, after transmitting a pulse. What is the target range?

Solution:

$$
R = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \times 100 \times 10^{-6}}{2} = \frac{3 \times 10^4}{2} = 15000 \text{米} = 15 \text{公里}
$$

An important concept: maximum unambiguous range

Radar transmits a train of pulses rather than a continuous wave. If a target is too far away, its echo may return only after the next pulse has been transmitted. The radar can no longer tell which transmitted pulse produced that echo, creating range ambiguity.

The maximum unambiguous range is determined by the pulse repetition interval (PRI):

$$
R_{\max} = \frac{c \cdot \text{PRI}}{2} = \frac{c}{2 \cdot \text{PRF}}
$$

Here, PRF (pulse repetition frequency) is the number of pulses transmitted per second.

Example: If PRF = 1000 Hz, or 1,000 pulses per second, then PRI = 1 ms.
$$R_{\max} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \times 0.001}{2} = 150 \text{公里}$$
Targets beyond 150 kilometres will therefore produce range ambiguity.

It is like a relay race: you must confirm the previous runner’s result before the next runner starts, or the records become confused.

1.3.2 Velocity Measurement: Extracting Motion through the Doppler Effect

Everyday analogy:
You are standing beside a road when an ambulance drives past with its siren on. As it approaches, the siren sounds sharper and higher; as it moves away, the siren sounds deeper and lower. This is the Doppler effect.

Principle:
When a wave source and an observer move relative to each other, the frequency received by the observer changes:

  • moving closer together → frequency rises and wavelength becomes shorter;
  • moving farther apart → frequency falls and wavelength becomes longer.

Radar uses this effect to measure velocity:

$$
f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda} \cos\theta
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning Explanation
$f_d$ Doppler shift Difference between received and transmitted frequencies (Hz)
$v$ Target velocity Radial velocity of the target relative to the radar (m/s)
$\lambda$ Wavelength Wavelength of the transmitted electromagnetic wave (m)
$\theta$ Angle Angle between the target’s direction of motion and the radar line of sight

When the target moves directly toward the radar, so that $\theta = 0^\circ$:

$$
f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}
$$

Example: An X-band radar operates at 10 GHz, giving a wavelength of three centimetres, and observes a car approaching at 72 km/h, or 20 m/s:

$$
f_d = \frac{2 \times 20}{0.03} \approx 1333 \text{ Hz}
$$

The received frequency is 1,333 Hz higher than the transmitted frequency. By measuring this tiny difference, the radar can calculate the car’s speed.

Important distinction:

  • range is obtained from pulse flight time in the time domain;
  • velocity is obtained from Doppler shift in the frequency domain;
  • the two pieces of information are “orthogonal” and can be acquired simultaneously.

1.3.3 Angle Measurement: Where Is the Target?

Knowing range and velocity is not enough. We must also know the target’s direction.

The basic principle of radar angle measurement: the antenna pattern

A radar antenna does not radiate energy uniformly in every direction. Like a flashlight, it has a main beam pointing in a particular direction. Transmitted energy and receiving sensitivity are both greatest there.

  • An echo is strongest when the target lies on the centre line of the main beam.
  • The echo weakens as the target moves away from the main beam.

By rotating the antenna mechanically or steering its beam electronically with a phased array, the radar finds the angle at which the echo is strongest and thus determines the target’s azimuth and elevation.

Several methods of measuring angle:

  1. Sequential lobing: transmit or receive at different antenna positions and compare changes in echo strength.
  2. Conical scanning: move the beam around the axis in a cone and infer target offset from the echo modulation.
  3. Monopulse measurement: compare several receive channels simultaneously after one transmission. This is the most accurate method.

Angular accuracy depends on antenna beamwidth:

  • a narrower beam gives more accurate angle measurement;
  • a larger antenna relative to the wavelength produces a narrower beam;
  • approximately, $$\theta_{3dB} \approx \frac{\lambda}{D}$$, where D is the antenna aperture.

1.3.4 Radar Cross Section: How “Conspicuous” Is a Target?

Everyday analogy:
Throwing a stone into water produces waves, but an aircraft carrier produces far larger waves than a duck. Radar echoes work in the same way: different targets reflect electromagnetic waves with different strengths.

Radar cross section (RCS) measures a target’s ability to reflect electromagnetic waves. It is denoted by $\sigma$ and measured in square metres ($\text{m}^2$).

Factors that affect RCS:

Factor Effect
Target size Larger targets are usually easier to detect
Target shape Planes and right angles reflect strongly; curved surfaces and stealth shaping reflect weakly
Material Metals reflect strongly; radar-absorbing materials reflect weakly
Aspect angle A target’s RCS can vary by more than a thousandfold with viewing angle
Frequency The same target has a different RCS at different radar frequencies

Typical RCS values:

Target Approximate RCS Note
Large airliner, such as a Boeing 747 100 $\text{m}^2$ Very easy to detect
Fighter aircraft, such as an F-16 3–5 $\text{m}^2$ Moderate
Stealth fighter, such as an F-22 About 0.01 $\text{m}^2$ Comparable to a bird
Stealth fighter, such as an F-35 About 0.001 $\text{m}^2$ Very difficult to detect
Bird 0.01 $\text{m}^2$
Insect 0.00001 $\text{m}^2$

The essence of stealth technology is reducing RCS. Shaping and radar-absorbing materials greatly weaken the returned echo and thereby shorten a radar’s detection range.

1.3.5 The Radar Equation: The Starting Point of Every Design

Radar engineers use the following equation to estimate how far a radar can detect:

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t G_t A_e \sigma}{(4\pi)^2 R^4}
$$

or, more commonly:

$$
R_{\max} = \left[ \frac{P_t G_t A_e \sigma}{(4\pi)^2 P_{r,\min}} \right]^{1/4}
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning
$P_t$ Transmitted power
$G_t$ Antenna gain
$A_e$ Effective antenna aperture
$\sigma$ Target RCS
$R$ Target range
$P_{r,\min}$ Minimum detectable receiver input power

Notice that range appears under a fourth root. Doubling the detection range therefore requires sixteen times as much transmitted power. This is why radar transmitters need enormous power; some reach peak powers on the order of megawatts.

1.4 Components of a Radar System

A complete radar resembles the human body. Let us examine its “organs”:

1.4.1 Transmitter: The Radar’s “Voice”

Function: generate high-power radio-frequency pulses and send them to the antenna for transmission.

Key requirements:

  • High power: required for long detection ranges, with peak power ranging from hundreds of watts to several megawatts;
  • High stability: frequency must remain precise and stable;
  • A suitable pulse width: determines range resolution;
  • A suitable PRF: determines maximum unambiguous range and Doppler measurement capability.

Two main types:

Type Principle Characteristics Typical power
Oscillator type A power oscillator, such as a magnetron, directly generates high-power pulses Simple and inexpensive, but with poor frequency stability Up to the MW level
Amplifier type A small signal is generated first and then amplified by a klystron or travelling-wave tube High frequency stability and suitable for coherent processing Up to the MW level

Pulse parameters:

  • Pulse width (tp): duration of a pulse, typically 0.1–100 microseconds;
  • Pulse repetition interval (PRI): interval between two consecutive pulses;
  • Duty cycle: $D = \frac{t_p}{\text{PRI}}$, typically 0.1%–50%.

1.4.2 Duplexer or T/R Switch

Function: allow the transmitter and receiver to share one antenna.

Why is it needed?

  • During transmission, it connects the antenna to the transmitter and protects the receiver from destructive high power.
  • During reception, it connects the antenna to the receiver and prevents transmitted energy from leaking into the receive channel.

Everyday analogy: it is like a revolving door—people cannot enter and leave through the same section at the same moment. A pulsed radar transmits and receives at different times.

1.4.3 Antenna: The Radar’s “Eyes”

Function: concentrate the transmitter’s RF energy in a particular direction during transmission and collect echo energy during reception.

Major antenna parameters:

  1. Antenna pattern: describes the antenna’s ability to radiate or receive in different directions.

    • Main lobe: the direction in which energy is most concentrated;
    • Side lobes: unwanted radiation in other directions; less is better;
    • Nulls: directions in which almost no radiation occurs.

  2. Gain (G): the antenna’s ability to concentrate energy.

    • Unit: dBi, relative to an ideal isotropic source;
    • higher gain means a narrower beam and longer viewing range.

  3. Beamwidth: the half-power width of the main lobe.

    • A narrower beam gives better angular accuracy and resolution.

Common antenna types:

Antenna type Characteristics Application
Parabolic antenna A dish that focuses electromagnetic waves Tracking radar and satellite communication
Slotted-waveguide antenna A series of slots cut into a waveguide Airport surveillance radar
Microstrip antenna Thin and inexpensive Automotive radar and consumer electronics
Phased-array antenna Many small elements with electronic scanning Aegis radar and the F-35 radar

1.4.4 Receiver: The Radar’s “Ears”

Function: amplify, frequency-convert, and filter the weak echo received by the antenna, producing a signal that the signal processor can handle.

The superheterodyne receiver is the classic receiver architecture, invented by Edwin Armstrong in 1918.

Basic principle:

  1. The radio-frequency signal received by the antenna is amplified by a low-noise amplifier (LNA).
  2. It is mixed with a local oscillator (LO) signal and downconverted to an intermediate frequency (IF).
  3. The IF signal is amplified and filtered, then digitized by an ADC.

Why use a superheterodyne architecture?

  • Amplification at a high RF frequency is difficult, unstable, and susceptible to oscillation.
  • Amplification at an intermediate frequency is easier and offers high gain and good selectivity.
  • Different signal frequencies can be received by selecting different local-oscillator frequencies.

1.4.5 Signal Processor: The Radar’s “Brain”

The signal processor is the heart of a radar system. It extracts target information from echoes and is usually implemented with an FPGA, DSP, or GPU.

Main processing tasks:

  1. Pulse compression: transmit a wide, high-energy pulse and compress it into a narrow, high-resolution pulse with a matched filter after reception. This resolves the conflict between high energy and high resolution.
  2. MTI/MTD, or moving-target indication/detection: use the Doppler effect to remove stationary clutter while retaining moving targets.
  3. CFAR, or constant false-alarm-rate detection: automatically adjust the detection threshold as noise or clutter changes in order to maintain a constant false-alarm probability.
  4. Target detection: compare a signal with a threshold and decide whether a target is present.
  5. Parameter estimation: calculate target range, velocity, and angle.

1.4.6 Display and Control Console

This component presents processed target information graphically to an operator.

Classic display modes:

Mode Full name Display method What does it resemble?
A-scope Range–Intensity Range on the horizontal axis and echo strength on the vertical axis An electrocardiogram
PPI Plan Position Indicator Radar at the centre and targets plotted on a map A 360-degree panoramic map
B-scope Range–Azimuth Azimuth on the horizontal axis and range on the vertical axis A rectangular range–azimuth map

1.5.3 Classification by Transmitted Waveform

  • Continuous-wave (CW) radar transmits continuously and measures velocity through the Doppler effect, but cannot measure range, or can do so only with difficulty.
  • Pulsed radar transmits a pulse train and measures range from echo delay. It is the most common type.
  • Frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar varies its frequency continuously and measures range and velocity at the same time. It is widely used in vehicles.

1.5.4 Classification by Antenna Scanning Method

  • Mechanically scanned radar rotates or swings its antenna mechanically. Its structure is simple, but scan speed is limited.
  • Phased-array radar steers its beam electronically with almost no inertia and can track multiple targets at once.
  • Digital beamforming (DBF) radar forms multiple beams in the digital domain and offers the greatest flexibility.

A concrete example: the AN/SPS-49 is a US Navy L-band long-range air-search radar. It has a detection range of about 460 kilometres and mechanically rotates its antenna six times per minute, making it a classic “mechanical scanning plus search radar” system.

Chapter Summary

Key Points

  1. The essence of radar: transmit electromagnetic waves → receive echoes → extract information. The principle is the same as a bat’s echolocation.

  2. Radar’s three fundamental measurements:

    • range: $R = cT/2$, using electromagnetic round-trip time;
    • velocity: $f_d = 2v/\lambda$, using the Doppler effect;
    • angle: using the antenna pattern.

  3. Key factors that affect radar detection:

    • greater transmitted power is better;
    • a larger antenna is better;
    • a target with a larger RCS is easier to detect;
    • less noise and interference is better.

  4. The eight major components of a radar system are the transmitter, receiver, antenna, duplexer, signal processor, data processor, display, and power/servo subsystem.

  5. Frequency selection is among the most important radar design decisions. It influences beamwidth, detection range, resolution, atmospheric attenuation, and almost every other performance measure.

Chapter Exercises

Problem 1: Radar Ranging

A radar receives an echo at $t = 200\ \mu\text{s}$ after transmitting an electromagnetic wave. Given the propagation speed $c = 3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, find the target range $R$.

Show the solution

$$R = \frac{c \cdot t}{2} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \times 200 \times 10^{-6}}{2} = 30000\ \text{m} = 30\ \text{km}$$

Problem 2: Maximum Unambiguous Range

A radar has a pulse repetition frequency of PRF = 800 Hz. Find its maximum unambiguous range $R_{\max}$. If a target is 250 km away, where will its echo appear?

Show the solution

$$R_{\max} = \frac{c}{2 \cdot \text{PRF}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 800} = 187500\ \text{m} \approx 187.5\ \text{km}$$

Because 250 km exceeds the maximum unambiguous range, the echo will be range-ambiguous. The displayed range will be:
$$R_{\text{显示}} = 250 - 187.5 = 62.5\ \text{km}$$

A target at 250 km will therefore be mistaken for one at 62.5 km.

Problem 3: Duty Cycle and Average Power

A radar transmits pulses of width $t_p = 1\ \mu\text{s}$ with PRI = 1 ms and peak power $P_t = 1\ \text{MW}$. Calculate (1) the duty cycle $D$ and (2) the average transmitted power $P_{\text{avg}}$.

Show the solution

(1) Duty cycle:

$$D = \frac{t_p}{\text{PRI}} = \frac{1 \times 10^{-6}}{1 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.001 = 0.1%$$

(2) Average power:

$$P_{\text{avg}} = P_t \times D = 1 \times 10^6 \times 0.001 = 1000\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{kW}$$

Problem 4: Doppler Shift

An aircraft flies directly toward a radar at a radial velocity of $v = 250\ \text{m/s}$. The radar operates at $f_c = 3\ \text{GHz}$. Find the Doppler shift $f_d$.

Show the solution

Wavelength: $\lambda = \frac{c}{f_c} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{3 \times 10^9} = 0.1\ \text{m}$

Doppler shift: $f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda} = \frac{2 \times 250}{0.1} = 5000\ \text{Hz} = 5\ \text{kHz}$

Chapter 2: Signal-Processing Theory

Chapter overview: A radar transmits and receives electromagnetic waves, which are essentially continuously varying signals, such as voltages that change with time. Modern radars, however, process signals with computers. A computer handles only digital values—zeros and ones—not continuously varying quantities directly. This raises a series of questions. How do we turn a continuous signal into numbers a computer can process? Does that conversion lose information? How can a computer quickly analyse a signal’s frequency components? How can we design a “frequency filter” that extracts the signal we want while removing what we do not want?

This chapter answers those questions. Although many equations appear, everyday examples will help build an intuition for every concept.

2.1 Discrete Signals and Systems

2.1.1 Continuous versus Discrete Signals

Everyday analogy:

Imagine recording the temperature throughout an entire day:

  • A continuous signal is like drawing the temperature curve continuously on infinitely long paper with an infinitely fine pen. The temperature at every instant would be recorded. In reality, this is impossible: you cannot record the temperature at every single instant.
  • With a discrete signal, you check a thermometer once an hour and write down 20°C at 8:00, 21°C at 9:00, 22°C at 10:00, and so on. You have discretely sampled the continuous temperature by recording values only at selected moments.

Formal definitions:

  • A continuous-time signal has a continuous independent variable, time, and is written as $x(t)$. It is defined at every time $t$.
  • A discrete-time signal is defined only at discrete moments and is written as $x[n]$. The integer $n$ identifies the sample number.

In a radar:

  • the echo received by the antenna is a continuously varying voltage—an analogue, continuous signal;
  • an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) samples that continuous echo at fixed intervals and turns it into a sequence of numbers—a discrete signal.

2.1.2 From Continuous to Discrete: Sampling

The conversion of a continuous signal into a discrete signal is called sampling.

Mathematically, sampling can be represented as:

$$
x_p(t) = x(t) \cdot \delta_T(t)
$$

where $\delta_T(t)$ is a periodic impulse train, a sequence of equally spaced impulses with interval $T_s$, the sampling period.

The sampled signal becomes a sequence of numbers:

$$
x[n] = x(nT_s), \quad n = \cdots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \cdots
$$

Key parameters:

Parameter Symbol Definition Relationship
Sampling period $T_s$ Time interval between two samples —
Sampling frequency $f_s$ Number of samples taken per second $f_s = 1/T_s$

Tip: sampling period and sampling frequency are reciprocals. A shorter interval means more samples per second.

2.1.3 Linear Time-Invariant Systems

Everyday analogy:

A “system” is like a black box: you put something in and it produces something at the output.

  • Linearity means that if you double the input sound, the output sound also doubles. If you input two sounds together, the output is the sum of their separate outputs, without cross-coupling.
  • Time invariance means that if you call “Hello” today and the box answers “Hi,” it will give the same answer to the same call tomorrow. Its behaviour does not change with time.

Formal definition:

A system is called a linear time-invariant (LTI) system if it has both of the following properties:

  1. Linearity: superposition and homogeneity
    $$
    \text{若 } x_1[n] \to y_1[n], ; x_2[n] \to y_2[n]
    $$
    $$
    \text{则 } a \cdot x_1[n] + b \cdot x_2[n] \to a \cdot y_1[n] + b \cdot y_2[n]
    $$

  2. Time invariance
    $$
    \text{若 } x[n] \to y[n]
    $$
    $$
    \text{则 } x[n - k] \to y[n - k] \quad (\text{对任意} k)
    $$

Why are LTI systems so important?

An LTI system can be described by convolution:

$$
y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] \cdot h[n-k]
$$

Here $h[n]$ is the system’s unit impulse response: its output when the input is an instantaneous impulse, equal to one only at $n=0$ and zero elsewhere. Once $h[n]$ is known, the response to any input is known completely.

Why this matters in radar:

  • Most radar processing modules, including filters and pulse compressors, are LTI systems.
  • A matched filter, which maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio, is a typical LTI system.
  • Convolution is among the most common mathematical operations in radar signal processing.

2.1.4 Description by Difference Equations

A discrete LTI system is commonly described by a linear constant-coefficient difference equation:

$$
\sum_{i=0}^N a_i y[n-i] = \sum_{j=0}^M b_j x[n-j]
$$

The most common form, with $$a_0 = 1$$, is:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{j=0}^M b_j x[n-j] - \sum_{i=1}^N a_i y[n-i]
$$

Two important types:

Type Condition Characteristics
FIR filter All $a_i = 0$ for $i \ge 1$ Output depends only on inputs; stable and capable of linear phase
IIR filter At least one $a_i \neq 0$ Output also depends on past outputs and may contain feedback

2.2 The Sampling Theorem

2.2.1 An Everyday Analogy: Filming a Movie

A film records 24 frames, or still photographs, every second. Each frame is one sample of continuous motion.

Suppose you film a rotating wheel:

  • If the wheel turns slowly, perhaps only a few revolutions per second, 24 frames can capture its motion.
  • If it turns extremely fast, the wheel may appear to rotate backwards on screen.

This is aliasing. When the sampling frequency is too low, high-frequency information disguises itself as lower-frequency information.

Core question: how high must the sampling frequency $f_s$ be if the original continuous signal is to be reconstructed perfectly from its samples?

2.2.2 The Nyquist Sampling Theorem: Low-Pass Sampling

The Nyquist sampling theorem, also called the Shannon sampling theorem, answers this question:

For a band-limited signal whose highest frequency is $f_H$, if the sampling frequency $f_s$ satisfies
$$f_s > 2f_H$$
the original continuous signal can be reconstructed perfectly from its discrete samples.

The value $$2f_H$$ is called the Nyquist rate.

Why does this condition work? Here is an intuitive explanation.

The original signal has the following spectrum:

After sampling, copies of the original spectrum repeat every $$f_s$$:

  • If $$f_s > 2f_H$$, there are gaps between neighbouring copies, and a low-pass filter can isolate the original spectrum in the centre.
  • If $$f_s \le 2f_H$$, neighbouring copies overlap, creating aliasing from which the original signal can no longer be recovered.

Aliasing is like two different signals becoming identical after sampling: you can no longer tell which one produced the samples.

2.2.3 Sampling in Radar: A Practical Example

Suppose the received radar echo has a bandwidth of 10 MHz, which determines range resolution. By the Nyquist theorem, the ADC sampling frequency must be at least:

$$
f_s > 2 \times 10\ \text{MHz} = 20\ \text{MHz}
$$

That means at least twenty million samples per second.

Common misconception: “If a radar operates at 10 GHz, must the sample rate be 20 GHz?”

Answer: No. The receiver first downconverts the high-frequency signal to an intermediate frequency or baseband. This lowers the carrier frequency while retaining the information inside the 10 MHz bandwidth. The ADC therefore needs to sample at twice the bandwidth, not twice the carrier frequency. This is the value of band-pass sampling and the superheterodyne receiver.

2.2.4 Band-Pass Sampling

Why is band-pass sampling needed?

In a real radar, a signal is often not a low-pass signal beginning at 0 Hz. It is a band-pass signal centred at a higher frequency. For example:

  • radar intermediate frequency: centre frequency of 70 MHz and bandwidth of 10 MHz;
  • occupied frequency range: 65–75 MHz.

Conventional low-pass Nyquist sampling would require a rate above 150 MHz, or $$2 \times 75$$ MHz. Such a rate places a heavy burden on the ADC.

The central idea of band-pass sampling:

Band-pass sampling permits a sample rate below twice the signal’s highest frequency, provided certain conditions are met. For a band-pass signal occupying $$[f_L, f_H]$$ with bandwidth $$B = f_H - f_L$$, the sampling rate $$f_s$$ must satisfy:

$$
\frac{2f_H}{m+1} \le f_s \le \frac{2f_L}{m}
$$

where $$m$$ is an integer in the range $$0 \le m \le \lfloor f_L/B \rfloor$$.

A simpler procedure:

  1. Determine the bandwidth $$B = f_H - f_L$$.
  2. Find the integer $$m = \lfloor f_L / B \rfloor$$, rounding downward.
  3. Obtain the usable range $$\frac{2f_H}{m+1} \le f_s \le \frac{2f_L}{m}$$.

A concrete example:

An IF signal has a centre frequency of 70 MHz and a bandwidth of 20 MHz.

  • $$f_L = 60$$ MHz, $$f_H = 80$$ MHz, and $$B = 20$$ MHz;
  • $$m = \lfloor 60/20 \rfloor = 3$$;
  • the range is $$\frac{2 \times 80}{3+1} = 40 \le f_s \le \frac{2 \times 60}{3} = 40$$;
  • therefore $$f_s = 40$$ MHz is the only choice for m=3.

Consider another value:

  • for $$m = 2$$, $$\frac{2 \times 80}{2+1} \approx 53.33 \le f_s \le \frac{2 \times 60}{2} = 60$$;
  • therefore $$f_s = 56$$ MHz would also work.

The essence of band-pass sampling is to exploit periodic spectral repetition so that a high-frequency spectrum “folds” into a lower-frequency region without overlap. It resembles filming a fast fan with a slow camera: although the sampling is “slow,” you can still interpret the motion as long as the blade positions do not overlap ambiguously.

Why band-pass sampling matters in radar:

  • It greatly reduces the required ADC sampling rate.
  • It enables direct IF sampling followed by digital downconversion (DDC) to baseband I/Q.
  • A typical modern radar uses IF band-pass sampling plus digital downconversion.

2.2.5 I/Q, or Quadrature, Sampling

Everyday analogy:

When you listen to a sound, you can hear both:

  • how loud it is, which corresponds to amplitude;
  • whether it comes from the left or right, which depends on phase information, such as the difference in arrival time at your ears.

Recording only loudness loses directional, or phase, information. In radar, losing phase information also loses Doppler information and makes velocity measurement impossible.

What are I and Q?

I means in-phase and Q means quadrature, or 90 degrees out of phase.

A radar signal can be written as:

$$
s(t) = A(t) \cos(2\pi f_0 t + \phi(t))
$$

It contains both amplitude $$A(t)$$ and phase $$\phi(t)$$.

To retain both completely, we decompose the signal into two orthogonal components:

$$
s(t) = I(t) \cos(2\pi f_0 t) - Q(t) \sin(2\pi f_0 t)
$$

where:

  • $$I(t) = A(t) \cos\phi(t)$$ is the in-phase component;
  • $$Q(t) = A(t) \sin\phi(t)$$ is the quadrature component.

How does a receiver obtain I and Q?

  1. Divide the input into two paths.
  2. Multiply the I path by $$\cos(2\pi f_0 t)$$ and apply low-pass filtering.
  3. Multiply the Q path by $$-\sin(2\pi f_0 t)$$, shifted by 90 degrees, and apply low-pass filtering.

Advantages of I/Q sampling:

Capability Amplitude-only sampling I/Q sampling
Measure amplitude ✅ ✅
Measure phase ❌ ✅
Distinguish positive and negative frequencies ❌ ✅
Detect Doppler shifts ❌ ✅
Determine the target’s direction of motion ❌ ✅
Signal-to-noise ratio Baseline 3 dB higher

Why I/Q sampling matters in radar:

  • It retains all amplitude and phase information and thus forms the basis of coherent radar.
  • It distinguishes positive from negative Doppler frequencies, revealing whether a target is approaching or receding.
  • Pulse-Doppler and MTI radar depend fundamentally on I/Q sampling.

2.3 DFT and FFT

The Fourier transform is one of the most important and elegant tools in signal processing. It answers the question:

What frequency components are contained in a signal that changes with time?

2.3.1 Why Move from the Time Domain to the Frequency Domain?

Everyday analogy:

  • In the time domain, you listen to a song and hear drums, guitar, and voice appear as time passes.
  • In the frequency domain, you inspect the song’s spectrum and find that the bass range from 50 to 200 Hz is mainly drums, the midrange from 200 to 2,000 Hz is mainly voice, and the treble from 2,000 to 5,000 Hz is mainly guitar.

The time and frequency domains are two different views of the same signal.

Why does this matter for radar?

Radar velocity measurement depends on a Doppler shift, which is a change in frequency. Such a change may be almost invisible in a time-domain waveform but appears immediately in the frequency domain as a shifted spectral line.

2.3.2 The Progression through Four Fourier Transforms

Different versions of the Fourier transform suit signals with different properties:

Transform Time domain Frequency domain Suitable signals
FT, Fourier transform Continuous and aperiodic Continuous and aperiodic Theoretical analysis; not directly computable
FS, Fourier series Continuous and periodic Discrete and aperiodic Theoretical analysis of periodic signals
DTFT, discrete-time FT Discrete and aperiodic Continuous and periodic Theoretical analysis of discrete signals
DFS, discrete Fourier series Discrete and periodic Discrete and periodic Periodic discrete signals
DFT, discrete Fourier transform Discrete and finite Discrete and finite Computable by a computer

The crucial leap from FT to DFT takes us from a mathematical expression requiring infinitely many continuous points to a computation over a finite collection of numbers.

2.3.3 Definition of the DFT

For a discrete sequence $$N$$ of length $$x[n]$$, the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}, \quad k = 0, 1, \cdots, N-1
$$

Its inverse, the IDFT, is:

$$
x[n] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k] \cdot e^{j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}, \quad n = 0, 1, \cdots, N-1
$$

Meaning of each symbol:

Symbol Meaning
$x[n]$ The nth sample of the input signal in the time domain
$X[k]$ The kth frequency component of the output spectrum
$N$ Total number of samples and output frequency bins
$e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}$ Twiddle factor, a sinusoid in complex form
$k$ Frequency-bin index corresponding to $f_k = k \cdot f_s / N$

What does the DFT do? It correlates the input with sinusoids at different frequencies to find how much of each frequency the signal contains.

  • $$X[0]$$ is the signal’s DC component, at 0 Hz.
  • $$X[1]$$ is the component at $$f_s/N$$.
  • $$X[k]$$ is the component at $$k \cdot f_s/N$$.

2.3.4 Compact Notation: The Twiddle Factor

For compact notation, define the twiddle factor as:

$$
W_N = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}}
$$

The DFT becomes:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot W_N^{kn}, \quad k = 0, 1, \cdots, N-1
$$

Important properties of the twiddle factor:

Property Formula Intuition
Periodicity $W_N^{k+N} = W_N^k$ One full rotation returns to the starting point
Symmetry $W_N^{k+N/2} = -W_N^k$ Half a rotation points in the opposite direction
Conjugacy $(W_N^k)^* = W_N^{-k}$ Complex conjugation
Reducibility $W_{mN}^{mk} = W_N^k$ Invariance under scaling

These properties provide the essential basis of FFT acceleration.

2.3.5 The Computational Cost of the DFT: Why Do We Need the FFT?

Computing a DFT directly from its definition requires:

  • $$X[k]$$ complex multiplications and $$N$$ complex additions for each $$(N-1)$$;
  • There are $$N$$ output values $$X[k]$$, so the total cost is approximately:
    • $$N^2$$ complex multiplications;
    • $$N(N-1) \approx N^2$$ complex additions.

What does this mean in practice?

N N² Scenario
64 4,096 One small computation
1024 1,048,576 A medium-length signal
4096 16,777,216 Samples from a typical radar pulse
65536 4,294,967,296 A longer signal—more than four billion multiplications

For a real-time system such as radar, an $$N^2$$ cost is far too high. A faster algorithm is essential.

2.3.6 The FFT: The Cooley–Tukey Algorithm

The fast Fourier transform (FFT) is not a new transform. It is a fast algorithm for computing the DFT.

Core idea: divide and conquer

Decompose one large DFT into two smaller DFTs, then decompose those two into four still smaller ones, continuing until only the simplest two-point DFTs remain.

More specifically, radix-2 decimation in time (DIT):

Suppose $$N = 2^m$$. Divide the sequence into its even- and odd-indexed samples:

  • even-indexed samples: $$x_e[n] = x[2n]$$;
  • odd-indexed samples: $$x_o[n] = x[2n+1]$$.

Then:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1} x_e[n] \cdot W_{N/2}^{kn} + W_N^k \sum_{n=0}^{N/2-1} x_o[n] \cdot W_{N/2}^{kn}
$$

$$
= X_e[k] + W_N^k \cdot X_o[k], \quad k = 0, 1, \cdots, N/2-1
$$

By symmetry, the second half is:

$$
X[k + N/2] = X_e[k] - W_N^k \cdot X_o[k], \quad k = 0, 1, \cdots, N/2-1
$$

2.3.7 The Butterfly Operation

The decomposition above corresponds to one fundamental computational unit, the butterfly operation:

The shape resembles a butterfly or bow tie, hence the name.

The complete process repeatedly halves an $$N$$-point DFT until it becomes $$N/2$$ two-point DFTs, over a total of $$\log_2 N$$ stages.

Comparison of computational cost:

$$
\text{DFT} : N^2 \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{FFT} : \frac{N}{2} \log_2 N
$$

N DFT multiplications FFT multiplications Speed-up
64 4,096 192 21 times
1024 1,048,576 5,120 205 times
4096 16,777,216 24,576 682 times
65536 4,294,967,296 524,288 8,192 times

Engineering significance of the FFT: modern digital signal processing would not exist without it. Radar systems must process millions of samples in real time; a direct DFT would take an unacceptable amount of time.

2.3.8 Frequency Resolution

This is among the most practical issues in radar signal processing.

Frequency resolution is the frequency interval between adjacent DFT output bins:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N}
$$

where $$f_s$$ is the sample rate and $$N$$ is the FFT length.

Intuitively:

  • $$f_s$$ determines the frequency range you can “see,” from 0 Hz to $$f_s/2$$;
  • $$N$$ determines how fine your frequency magnifying glass is by dividing the frequency range into $$N$$ parts.

Example:

  • sample rate $$f_s = 2$$ MHz and FFT length $$N = 1024$$;
  • frequency resolution $$\Delta f = 2\ \text{MHz} / 1024 \approx 1953$$ Hz;
  • two signals separated by less than 1,953 Hz therefore appear as one spectral peak and cannot be resolved.

Why this matters in radar:

Suppose we want to distinguish two targets whose velocities differ by 1 m/s, using a 10 GHz radar. Their Doppler-frequency difference is:

$$
\Delta f_d = \frac{2 \cdot \Delta v}{\lambda} = \frac{2 \times 1}{0.03} \approx 66.7\ \text{Hz}
$$

The frequency resolution must therefore be finer than 66.7 Hz:

$$
\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} < 66.7\ \text{Hz}
$$

If the pulse repetition frequency, which serves as the slow-time sampling rate $$f_s$$, is 10 kHz, then the required FFT length is:

$$
N > \frac{10000}{66.7} \approx 150
$$

At least a 256- or 512-point FFT is therefore needed.

An important trade-off:

  • Finer frequency resolution requires a larger N, which means a longer observation time $$T = N \cdot T_s = N/f_s$$.
  • In radar, observation time is limited by the time for which the beam illuminates the target.
  • Resolution is therefore limited by the coherent processing interval (CPI).

2.3.9 Windowing

The problem: a real signal has finite length, while the DFT assumes that the finite segment repeats periodically. If the signal is discontinuous at the segment boundaries, energy that should be concentrated at one frequency spreads into neighbouring frequencies. This is called spectral leakage.

The solution: before the FFT, multiply the signal by a window function that lets it decay smoothly to zero at the boundaries.

Common window functions:

Window Main-lobe width Side-lobe level Characteristics
Rectangular Narrowest −13 dB No extra weighting; severe leakage
Hann Wider −32 dB Commonly used compromise between resolution and leakage suppression
Hamming Wider −43 dB Similar to the Hann window
Blackman Widest −58 dB Least leakage but poorest resolution

Effect of a window on the spectrum:

The trade-off:

  • A rectangular window gives the best frequency resolution but severe leakage.
  • Other windows reduce leakage but worsen resolution.
  • The best window depends on whether the application values resolution or leakage suppression more highly.

Window functions in radar:

  • Hamming and Chebyshev windows are often used in MTI/MTD processing to reduce Doppler side lobes.
  • Taylor and Kaiser windows are commonly used to weight matched filters for pulse compression.

2.4 Digital Filters

2.4.1 What Is a Digital Filter?

Everyday analogy:

Imagine making coffee:

  • the filter is the paper that lets the desired coffee liquid pass but holds back the unwanted grounds;
  • the passband is like the holes in the paper, allowing selected frequencies through;
  • the stopband is like the solid material, blocking other frequencies.

A digital filter is a frequency-selective computation that extracts some frequency components from a digital signal and suppresses others.

2.4.2 FIR Filters: Finite Impulse Response Filters

Definition: the output of an FIR filter depends only on present and past inputs, not on past outputs.

Difference equation:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \cdot x[n-k]
$$

The $$b_k$$ values are filter, or tap, coefficients, and the filter has $$M+1$$ taps.

Structure:

Here $$z^{-1}$$ represents a unit delay, delaying the signal by one sampling period.

Advantages of FIR filters:

Property Explanation
Stability With no feedback, the filter cannot diverge
Linear phase Every frequency component has the same delay, so the waveform is not distorted—very important in radar
Simple implementation Only multiply–accumulate operations are required

A design method: the window method

  1. Specify the ideal frequency response $$H_d(e^{j\omega})$$.
  2. Calculate the ideal impulse response $$h_d[n]$$, generally of the form $$\sin(x)/x$$.
  3. Truncate it to a finite length with a window: $$h[n] = h_d[n] \cdot w[n]$$.

Example: designing a low-pass FIR filter

The frequency response of an ideal low-pass filter is:

$$
H_d(e^{j\omega}) = \begin{cases}
e^{-j\omega\tau}, & |\omega| \le \omega_c \
0, & \omega_c < |\omega| \le \pi
\end{cases}
$$

Its corresponding, infinitely long impulse response is:

$$
h_d[n] = \frac{\sin[\omega_c(n-\tau)]}{\pi(n-\tau)}
$$

Multiplying it by a window and truncating it gives an implementable FIR filter.

2.4.3 Digital Downconversion

Everyday analogy:

Suppose you move from Beijing to Shanghai. Homes in both cities cost millions of yuan, but you cannot simply treat a Beijing price as a Shanghai price when making a comparison.

Similarly, a radar echo has a very high frequency, from hundreds of megahertz to tens of gigahertz, and cannot be processed directly. It first needs downconversion, which “moves” the signal from a high frequency to a low one.

A digital downconverter (DDC) performs downconversion in the digital domain and is central to software-defined radio and modern radar receivers.

DDC structure:

Processing sequence:

  1. The ADC digitizes the intermediate-frequency signal.
  2. The digital signal is multiplied by a digital sinusoid generated by a numerically controlled oscillator (NCO).
  3. Low-pass filtering produces baseband I/Q signals.
  4. Later processing can operate at baseband, where a lower sample rate means less computation.

Why use a DDC?

  • The signal after ADC sampling may still be at a relatively high IF, perhaps tens of megahertz.
  • Direct processing requires a high clock frequency and many computational resources.
  • After moving the signal to baseband, the DDC can reduce the sample rate through decimation, greatly reducing the computational load.
  • At the same time, it produces orthogonal I/Q signals and preserves phase information.

2.4.4 CIC Filters: Cascaded Integrator–Comb Filters

A cascaded integrator–comb (CIC) filter is a special and exceptionally efficient decimation filter used widely in DDCs.

Why is a CIC filter needed?

After mixing and low-pass filtering in a DDC, the signal bandwidth is narrower. The sampling theorem tells us that we can sample it at a lower rate without losing information because its high-frequency components have been removed. This process is called decimation.

An anti-aliasing filter must be applied before decimation; otherwise, high-frequency components will alias after the rate is reduced.

When the decimation factor is large—for example, reducing 80 MHz to 2.5 MHz by a factor of 32—an ordinary FIR filter requires a prohibitively high order and too much computation.

The ingenious CIC architecture contains two parts:

  • Integrator: $$y[n] = y[n-1] + x[n]$$, an accumulator.
  • Comb: $$y[n] = x[n] - x[n-M]$$, a differentiator.
  • Decimator: retain one sample out of every R, written as ↓R.

Advantages of a CIC filter:

Property Explanation
No multiplications Only addition and subtraction are needed, making hardware implementation extremely simple and FPGA-friendly
Arbitrary decimation factor Only R needs to change
Predictable frequency response It has the shape of a sinc function

Disadvantages of a CIC filter:

  • Its passband droops and needs a later inverse-sinc FIR compensation filter.
  • Its stopband attenuation is limited, so several stages may be cascaded.

Where does a CIC filter appear in a radar?

  • It follows the DDC low-pass filter.
  • It performs large-factor decimation, such as 80 MHz to 10 MHz.
  • Subsequent half-band and FIR filters provide finer filtering and smaller rate reductions.

2.4.5 Half-Band Filters

A half-band (HB) filter is a special FIR filter designed specifically for decimation by two.

Properties:

  • The passband edge $$\omega_p$$ and stopband edge $$\omega_s$$ are symmetric about $$\pi/2$$.
  • Passband and stopband ripples are equal.
  • Every even-indexed tap except the centre tap is zero.

Why is a half-band filter efficient?

  • Half the coefficients are zero, halving the computation.
  • It is particularly suitable for decimation by two.
  • Cascading several half-band filters gives any power-of-two decimation factor.

A typical DDC decimation chain:

This three-stage CIC + HB + HB chain lowers the sample rate from 80 MHz to 2.5 MHz, with each stage playing a distinct role:

Stage Filter Decimation Output rate Function
1 CIC ×8 10 MHz Large rate reduction and coarse filtering
2 HB ×2 5 MHz Medium-precision half-band filtering
3 HB ×2 2.5 MHz Medium-precision half-band filtering
4 FIR ×1 2.5 MHz Fine compensation and shaping

2.4.6 Why Is Multistage Decimation More Efficient?

Suppose the sample rate must fall from 80 MHz to 2.5 MHz, a factor of 32.

  • Single-stage method: design one low-pass filter with a 1.25 MHz cutoff and decimate directly by 32.

    • The transition band is extremely narrow, only 1.25 MHz from 1.25 to 2.5 MHz.
    • A filter with hundreds or even thousands of taps is required.
    • The computational cost is enormous.

  • Multistage method: CIC (×8) + HB (×2) + HB (×2) + FIR.

    • Each stage has a much wider relative transition band.
    • Each stage needs only a low-order filter.
    • The total computational cost is only a small fraction of that of the single-stage design.

Core principle: perform decimation in stages; never try to do everything in one step.

2.4.7 A Complete DDC Processing Chain

The digital section of a modern radar receiver usually contains:

The numerically controlled oscillator (NCO) produces digital sine and cosine waves at a precisely controllable frequency.

The complete chain performs:

  1. ADC conversion: analogue IF → digital IF;
  2. NCO mixing: digital IF → digital baseband, moving the spectrum close to 0 Hz;
  3. CIC + HB + FIR filtering: low-pass filtering and decimation, lowering the data rate and the later processing load;
  4. I/Q output: two orthogonal baseband signals containing complete amplitude and phase information, ready for MTI, pulse-Doppler, CFAR, and other processing.

Chapter Summary

Key Points

  1. A discrete signal is a set of “snapshots” of a continuous signal. Computers handle only discrete values, so an ADC must convert a continuous radar echo into a discrete numerical sequence.

  2. The Nyquist sampling theorem is the fundamental constraint. The sample rate must exceed twice the highest signal frequency, or aliasing occurs. Band-pass sampling, however, permits a much lower rate for a narrow-band high-frequency signal and is crucial in radar ADC design.

  3. I/Q sampling retains all information: amplitude and phase. Losing phase means losing Doppler information, so radar requires I/Q sampling.

  4. The FFT reduces DFT complexity from $$N^2$$ to $$\frac{N}{2}\log_2 N$$. Through divide and conquer, it decomposes a time-domain signal into its frequency components. It is one of the most frequently used core algorithms in radar signal processing.

  5. Frequency resolution $$\Delta f = f_s/N$$ determines Doppler resolution. Resolution is inversely proportional to observation time, creating an unavoidable radar design trade-off.

  6. A digital filter is a gate for frequencies. FIR filters are stable and can have linear phase. Radar uses them extensively in pulse compression, MTI filtering, DDC, and other tasks.

  7. The DDC is central to a digital radar receiver. It first downconverts a high-frequency signal to baseband, then lowers the data rate with multistage CIC + HB + FIR decimation, and finally produces I/Q baseband signals for later processing.

(1) By the Nyquist sampling theorem:
$$f_s > 2f_H = 2 \times 10 = 20\ \text{MHz}$$

(2) When $f_s = 22\ \text{MHz}$, the Nyquist interval is $[-11\ \text{MHz}, 11\ \text{MHz}]$, so the highest representable signal frequency is:
$$f_{\max} = f_s/2 = 11\ \text{MHz}$$

Problem 2: Selecting a Band-Pass Sample Rate

A band-pass signal occupies $f_L = 60\ \text{MHz}$ to $f_H = 80\ \text{MHz}$, giving a bandwidth of $B = 20\ \text{MHz}$. Select a band-pass sample rate $f_s$ that prevents spectral aliasing.

Show the solution

The band-pass sampling theorem requires:
$$\frac{2f_H}{m+1} \le f_s \le \frac{2f_L}{m}$$

Here $m$ is an integer satisfying $0 \le m \le \lfloor f_L/B \rfloor$.

Since $f_L/B = 60/20 = 3$, $m = 0, 1, 2, 3$.

  • $m=0$: $f_s \ge 160\ \text{MHz}$, ordinary low-pass sampling and inefficient;
  • $m=1$: $80 \le f_s \le 120\ \text{MHz}$;
  • $m=2$: $53.3 \le f_s \le 60\ \text{MHz}$;
  • $m=3$: $40 \le f_s \le 40\ \text{MHz}$, so $f_s = 40\ \text{MHz}$.

An engineering design will often choose $f_s = 40\ \text{MHz}$ because it minimizes the sample rate and ADC load.

Verification: sampling at $f_s = 40\ \text{MHz}$ folds the 60–80 MHz spectrum into the 0–20 MHz baseband without overlap.

Problem 3: FFT Frequency Resolution

A radar has PRF = 1 kHz and performs an FFT over 64 pulses in one CPI. Find (1) the FFT frequency resolution $\Delta f$, (2) the maximum unambiguous Doppler range covered by the FFT, and (3) the number of pulses required for 10 Hz resolution.

Show the solution

(1) Frequency resolution:
$$\Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{\text{PRF}}{N} = \frac{1000}{64} = 15.625\ \text{Hz}$$

(2) Maximum unambiguous Doppler range:
$$F_{D,\max} = \text{PRF} = 1000\ \text{Hz}$$

(3) For $\Delta f = 10\ \text{Hz}$:
$$N = \frac{f_s}{\Delta f} = \frac{1000}{10} = 100\ \text{个脉冲}$$

In practice, FFT lengths are generally powers of two, so $N = 128$ may be used. Then $\Delta f = 1000/128 = 7.8125\ \text{Hz}$.

Problem 4: Computational Cost of an FIR Filter

An FIR filter has order $M = 127$, or 128 taps, and its input sample rate is $f_s = 10\ \text{MHz}$. (1) How many multiply–accumulate operations are required per second? (2) If FFT fast convolution with N=256 is used instead, how many complex multiplications are required per second?

Show the solution

(1) The direct FIR implementation requires:
$$\text{运算量} = (M+1) \times f_s = 128 \times 10 \times 10^6 = 1.28 \times 10^9\ \text{次/秒}$$

That is 1.28 GOPS, a heavy load for a real-time system.

(2) FFT fast convolution with N=256:

  • each FFT requires $(N/2)\log_2 N = 128 \times 8 = 1024$ complex multiplications;
  • each block contains N samples, so $f_s/N = 10^7/256 \approx 39062$ blocks are processed per second;
  • total cost: $39062 \times (1024 + 512 + 1024) \approx 39062 \times 2560 \approx 10^8$ operations per second.

The FFT method is about thirteen times faster than direct FIR filtering.

3.1 How Pulsed Radar Works

3.1.1 Beginning with Echo Ranging

Everyday example: Imagine shouting “Hello!” in a valley and then listening carefully. A little later, you hear your voice return as an echo. The echo exists because the sound travels to the opposite mountainside and is reflected back. If you know the speed of sound in air, about 340 m/s, and measure the interval between shouting and hearing the echo, you can calculate the distance to the mountain:

$$
\text{距离} = \frac{\text{声速} \times \text{时间差}}{2}
$$

Why divide by two? The sound makes a round trip: from you to the mountain and from the mountain back to you.

Radar works in exactly the same way, except that “sound” is replaced by an electromagnetic wave. A radar transmits a pulse of electromagnetic energy. When the pulse encounters a target, such as an aircraft, ship, or missile, it is reflected, and the radar receives the echo. Electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light, $c = 3 \times 10^8$ m/s. Measuring the delay $\Delta t$ between transmission and reception gives the target range:

$$
R = \frac{c \cdot \Delta t}{2} \tag{3.1}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.1:

  • $R$: target range in metres;
  • $c$: speed of light, $3 \times 10^8$ m/s;
  • $\Delta t$: interval from transmission to reception in seconds.

This is the most basic ranging principle of pulsed radar.

3.1.2 Transmitting a Pulse and Waiting for Its Echo

A pulsed radar does not transmit continuously. It repeatedly transmits a short pulse → waits for echoes → transmits the next pulse. The sequence resembles a camera flash: one flash, a wait for reflected light, and then the next flash.

One complete transmission-and-wait cycle is called the pulse repetition interval (PRI). It is denoted by $T$ and measured in seconds.

The number of pulses transmitted each second is the pulse repetition frequency (PRF) in hertz:

$$
\text{PRF} = \frac{1}{\text{PRI}} = \frac{1}{T} \tag{3.2}
$$

For example:

  • If PRI = 1 ms, PRF = 1,000 Hz, so the radar transmits 1,000 pulses per second.
  • If PRI = 0.2 ms, PRF = 5,000 Hz.

Why not transmit continuously?
A radar must “listen” for echoes. If it transmitted all the time, its outgoing signal would overwhelm the returning echo. A pulsed radar therefore turns off its receiver while transmitting, then turns it on to listen after the pulse. The delay between transmission and reception determines the greatest range it can measure.

3.1.3 Maximum Unambiguous Range

Within one PRI, the radar has only one interval in which to listen for an echo. If the target is so distant that its echo arrives after the next pulse has already been transmitted, the radar will mistake it for an echo from the second pulse and calculate the wrong range.

Everyday example: Suppose you shout “Hello!” in a valley every two seconds. If the mountainside is so far away that the echo returns after two seconds, you cannot tell which shout produced it.

The greatest range that radar can measure without this ambiguity is the maximum unambiguous range:

$$
R_{\text{ua}} = \frac{c \cdot T}{2} = \frac{c}{2 \cdot \text{PRF}} \tag{3.3}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.3:

  • $R_{\text{ua}}$: maximum unambiguous range in metres;
  • $c$: speed of light, $3 \times 10^8$ m/s;
  • $T$: PRI in seconds;
  • PRF: pulse repetition frequency in hertz.

Selecting a PRF involves a trade-off:

  • A high PRF, or short PRI, gives a small maximum unambiguous range but more frequent measurements.
  • A low PRF, or long PRI, gives a large maximum unambiguous range but less frequent measurements.
PRF PRI Maximum unambiguous range Application
1,000 Hz 1 ms 150 km Long-range early-warning radar
5,000 Hz 0.2 ms 30 km Medium-range fire-control radar
20 kHz 50 μs 7.5 km Short-range radar

3.1.4 Mathematical Representation of an Echo

The transmitted signal can be written as:

$$
x(t) = A(t) \cos(2\pi f_c t) \tag{3.4}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.4:

  • $x(t)$: the transmitted signal, a voltage or power varying with time;
  • $A(t)$: the pulse envelope, usually rectangular and dimensionless;
  • $f_c$: the carrier, or radar operating, frequency in hertz;
  • $t$: time in seconds.

If the target is at range $R_0$, the echo delay is $\tau_0 = 2R_0 / c$, and the received echo is:

$$
y(t) = k \cdot A(t - \tau_0) \cos[2\pi f_c (t - \tau_0) + \phi(t)] + n(t) \tag{3.5}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.5:

  • $y(t)$: received signal;
  • $k$: attenuation coefficient, reflecting propagation loss;
  • $\tau_0 = 2R_0/c$: time delay in seconds;
  • $\phi(t)$: phase change in radians introduced by target reflection;
  • $n(t)$: receiver noise.

Core idea: an echo is a delayed and attenuated version of the transmitted signal. Its delay $\tau_0$ reveals the target range $R_0 = c\tau_0/2$.

3.2 Signal-Sampling Strategies

3.2.1 Understanding Two-Dimensional Sampling through Film

Everyday example: How does a film work? A strip of film contains one static frame after another. Played quickly, perhaps at 24 frames per second, they become continuous motion. Each frame is a complete spatial image, while the sequence of frames describes change over time.

Radar sampling is remarkably similar. It divides time into two dimensions:

  1. Fast time: within the echo from one pulse, the receiver samples at a very high rate. These samples resemble the pixels within one film frame and describe the range dimension.
  2. Slow time: from one pulse to the next, the receiver records how the value in the same range gate changes. This resembles the change from one film frame to the next and describes target motion, or Doppler frequency.

3.2.2 Range Gates

The echo from one pulse is sampled at high speed, and each sample corresponds to a range gate, or range cell.

$$
L = \frac{R_{\text{swath}}}{\Delta R} = \frac{R_{\text{swath}}}{c / (2F_s)} \tag{3.6}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.6:

  • $L$: total number of range gates, or samples;
  • $R_{\text{swath}}$: observed range interval, or range window, in metres;
  • $\Delta R$: length represented by one range gate, or range resolution, in metres;
  • $F_s$: sample rate in hertz;
  • $c$: speed of light in metres per second.

Intuitively: suppose you want to observe targets from 0 to 30 km and use $F_s = 10$ MHz, or ten million samples per second. The interval between samples is $1/F_s = 0.1$ μs, corresponding to a range increment of $\Delta R = c \times 0.1\text{μs} / 2 = 15$ m. Each range gate therefore represents a fifteen-metre range segment, and $L = 30000/15 = 2000$ samples cover the full 30 km.

3.2.3 The Coherent Processing Interval

A coherent processing interval (CPI) contains the consecutive pulses used in one complete radar processing operation.

If PRF = 5,000 Hz and a CPI contains $M = 16$ pulses, its duration is:

$$
T_{\text{CPI}} = M \times \text{PRI} = 16 \times 0.2\text{ms} = 3.2\text{ms}
$$

Within a CPI, the radar assumes that the target’s state of motion remains approximately constant and jointly processes the echoes from all $M$ pulses, for example by Doppler filtering.

3.2.4 The Two-Dimensional Fast-Time–Slow-Time Matrix

Echoes from all pulses can be arranged in a two-dimensional matrix:

$$
\text{数据矩阵大小} = L \ (\text{距离门数}) \times M \ (\text{CPI内脉冲数})
$$

Dimension Meaning Sample rate Information
Fast time, columns Samples within one pulse echo $F_s$, on the MHz scale Target range
Slow time, rows Values of one range gate across pulses PRF, on the kHz scale Target velocity, or Doppler

Everyday analogy: fast time is like the pixels in one photograph taken by a high-speed camera, while slow time is like filming motion at 24 frames per second.

3.2.5 Sample Rate and Range Resolution

By the Nyquist sampling theorem, distortion-free reconstruction of a signal with bandwidth $B$ requires:

$$
F_s \geq B \tag{3.7}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.7:

  • $F_s$: sample rate in hertz;
  • $B$: signal bandwidth in hertz.

In radar, sample rate directly determines range resolution:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2F_s} = \frac{c}{2B} \tag{3.8}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.8:

  • $\Delta R$: range resolution in metres;
  • $c$: speed of light in metres per second;
  • $F_s$: sample rate in hertz when $F_s = B$;
  • $B$: signal bandwidth in hertz.

Example: if the radar signal bandwidth is $B = 10$ MHz, its range resolution is $\Delta R = 3\times 10^8 / (2 \times 10^7) = 15$ m. The radar cannot distinguish two targets less than fifteen metres apart.

3.2.6 Doppler Frequency and Slow-Time Sampling

When a target moves relative to the radar, the echo frequency shifts. This is the Doppler effect.

Everyday example: an ambulance siren sounds higher as the vehicle approaches you and lower as it recedes. Electromagnetic waves behave in the same way.

For a target with radial velocity $v$, the Doppler shift is:

$$
f_d = \frac{2v}{c/f_c} = \frac{2v}{\lambda} \tag{3.9}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.9:

  • $f_d$: Doppler frequency in hertz;
  • $v$: target radial velocity in metres per second;
  • $\lambda = c/f_c$: electromagnetic wavelength in metres.

PRF is the slow-time sampling rate. By the Nyquist theorem, PRF must exceed twice the greatest Doppler frequency:

$$
\text{PRF} > 2|f_{d,\max}|
$$

Otherwise, Doppler ambiguity occurs. The effect resembles a wheel appearing to turn backwards in a film because the frame rate is too low to capture its true motion.

Maximum unambiguous Doppler frequency:

$$
F_{D,\text{ua}} = \frac{1}{\text{PRI}} = \text{PRF} \tag{3.10}
$$

The corresponding maximum unambiguous velocity is:

$$
v_{\text{ua}} = \frac{\text{PRF} \cdot \lambda}{2} \tag{3.11}
$$

3.3 I/Q Quadrature Demodulation

3.3.1 Why Use I/Q? Hearing a Voice without Seeing the Speaker

Everyday example: On a telephone, you hear the other person’s voice, an amplitude variation, but cannot see their expression, analogous here to a phase variation. Half the information is lost.

A radar signal contains two essential kinds of information: amplitude, related to target strength, and phase, related to target motion. A conventional magnitude-only method loses phase, even though phase carries vital evidence of whether a target is moving.

I/Q demodulation is designed to retain amplitude and phase simultaneously.

3.3.2 What Are I and Q?

  • I, the in-phase component, is in phase with the reference signal.
  • Q, the quadrature component, is orthogonal to the reference, with a 90-degree phase difference.

A signal carrying phase information can be written as:

$$
x(t) = A(t) \cos[2\pi f_c t + \phi(t)] \tag{3.12}
$$

I/Q demodulation separates it into:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(t) &= A(t) \cos[\phi(t)] \
Q(t) &= A(t) \sin[\phi(t)]
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.13}
$$

Symbols in Equation 3.13:

  • $I(t)$: in-phase component;
  • $Q(t)$: quadrature component;
  • $A(t)$: signal amplitude;
  • $\phi(t)$: signal phase.

The complete signal can then be represented elegantly as a complex number:

$$
s(t) = I(t) + j Q(t) = A(t) e^{j\phi(t)} \tag{3.14}
$$

Why is the complex representation useful? The phase $\phi(t)$ contains information about tiny target movements. For example, movement by $\lambda/4$ changes the phase by $\pi$, or 180 degrees, which is immediately visible in the complex plane.

3.3.3 Hardware Implementation of I/Q Demodulation

The following block diagram shows a hardware I/Q demodulator:

The steps are:

  1. Mixing: multiply the RF signal by signals from a local oscillator (LO). The oscillator supplies:

    • a reference $\cos(2\pi f_{\text{LO}} t)$;
    • a signal shifted by 90 degrees, $-\sin(2\pi f_{\text{LO}} t)$.

  2. Low-pass filtering: the mixed output contains sum-frequency $f_c + f_{\text{LO}}$ and difference-frequency $f_c - f_{\text{LO}}$ components. The low-pass filter removes the sum and retains the difference.

Mathematical derivation:

For the I path, multiply $s(t) = A(t)\cos[2\pi f_c t + \phi(t)]$ by $\cos(2\pi f_{\text{LO}} t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s(t) \cdot \cos(2\pi f_{\text{LO}} t) &= A(t)\cos[2\pi f_c t + \phi(t)] \cdot \cos(2\pi f_{\text{LO}} t) \
&= \frac{A(t)}{2}{\cos[2\pi (f_c+f_{\text{LO}})t + \phi(t)] + \cos[2\pi (f_c-f_{\text{LO}})t + \phi(t)]}
\end{aligned}
$$

This uses the identity $\cos\alpha \cos\beta = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(\alpha+\beta) + \cos(\alpha-\beta)]$.

After low-pass filtering removes the sum-frequency term:

$$
I(t) = \frac{A(t)}{2} \cos[2\pi f_I t + \phi(t)]
$$

where $f_I = f_c - f_{\text{LO}}$ is the intermediate frequency.

The ADC first turns the analogue signal into a digital one, after which mixing and filtering occur in the digital domain. This implementation is precise, stable, and programmable.

Once a signal is represented as $I + jQ$, its amplitude and phase describe it completely:

$$
\text{幅度} = \sqrt{I^2 + Q^2} \quad \text{（对应目标强度）}
$$
$$
\text{相位} = \arctan(Q/I) \quad \text{（对目标运动极其敏感）}

In real hardware, the I and Q channels are difficult to match perfectly:

  • amplitude imbalance: the two paths have different gains;
  • phase imbalance: the separation is not exactly 90 degrees.

The resulting image interference must be compensated through digital calibration.

3.4 Analogue-to-Digital Conversion

3.4.1 Understanding an ADC through a Digital Photograph

Everyday example: A traditional film camera records continuous light and shadow chemically, whereas a digital camera discretizes the image into pixels. More pixels, or greater spatial resolution, produce a clearer photograph. More colour bits per pixel, such as sixteen instead of eight, produce finer colour transitions.

An analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) plays a similar role in radar. It turns a continuous analogue echo into a discrete digital signal that a computer can process.

An ADC has two essential parameters:

  1. Sample rate ($F_s$): the number of samples acquired per second, in hertz.
  2. Quantization depth ($N_{\text{bits}}$): the number of binary bits representing each sample.

3.4.2 Sample Rate: Undersampling and Oversampling

By the Nyquist sampling theorem, distortion-free recovery requires a sample rate at least twice the highest signal frequency:

$$
F_s \geq 2 \times f_{\text{max}} \tag{3.15}
$$

Radar, however, presents a special case.

A radar signal is often narrow-band: its bandwidth $B$ is far smaller than its carrier frequency $f_c$. Such a signal can use band-pass sampling, also called undersampling, at a rate far below the carrier frequency, provided:

$$
F_s \geq 2B
$$

Example: a radar has carrier frequency $f_c = 70$ MHz and bandwidth $B = 10$ MHz. Ordinary low-pass sampling at RF would require $F_s \geq 140$ MHz, while band-pass sampling requires only $F_s \geq 20$ MHz. This greatly reduces the ADC hardware requirement.

3.4.3 Quantization Depth and Dynamic Range

Quantization depth determines the number of signal levels the ADC can distinguish:

$$
\text{等级数} = 2^{N_{\text{bits}}}
$$

Quantization Levels Dynamic range Application
8 bit 256 ~48 dB Simple radar
12 bit 4,096 ~72 dB Mid-range radar
14 bit 16,384 ~84 dB High-end radar
16 bit 65,536 ~96 dB Precision radar

Dynamic range is the ratio between the strongest and weakest signals that an ADC can handle simultaneously. Echoes from a large target such as an airliner and a small target such as a drone may differ by several orders of magnitude. If the converter has too few bits, quantization noise buries the small target.

3.4.4 Relationship between Sample Rate and Range Resolution

The ADC sample rate directly influences range resolution, as explained in Section 3.2.5:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2F_s} \ (\text{当带宽受限时}) \quad \text{或} \quad \Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} \ (\text{一般情况})
$$

Sample rate / bandwidth Range resolution Application
1 MHz 150 m Long-range early-warning radar
10 MHz 15 m Medium-range radar
100 MHz 1.5 m Fire-control radar
1 GHz 0.15 m High-resolution imaging radar

3.4.5 How the ADC Affects Radar Performance

The ADC’s two parameters directly constrain the radar’s “eyesight”:

  1. Sample rate determines how finely the radar can see: range resolution. A higher rate gives narrower gates and finer resolution.
  2. Quantization depth determines how clearly it can see: dynamic range. More bits make weak targets less likely to disappear under noise.

The practical trade-off:

  • High sample rate × many quantization bits = an enormous data volume, challenging storage and transmission.
  • For example, $F_s = 100$ MHz and $N_{\text{bits}} = 14$ bits give $100 \times 10^6 \times 14 = 1.4$ Gbit/s.
  • This is why radar systems commonly process data in real time on an FPGA instead of sending all raw samples back to a computer.

Problem 1: Range Resolution

A radar transmits a signal with bandwidth $B = 5\ \text{MHz}$. Find its range resolution $\Delta R$. If the bandwidth increases to 50 MHz, by what factor does the resolution improve?

Show the solution

$$\Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 5 \times 10^6} = 30\ \text{m}$$

At 50 MHz:
$$\Delta R = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 50 \times 10^6} = 3\ \text{m}$$

The resolution improves by a factor of ten. Conclusion: greater bandwidth gives finer resolution.

Problem 2: Maximum Unambiguous Range versus PRF

A radar must detect a target 300 km away. (1) What is the greatest PRF that avoids range ambiguity? (2) If PRF is 500 Hz, what is the maximum unambiguous range?

Show the solution

(1) The unambiguous condition is $R_{\max} \ge 300\ \text{km}$:

$$\text{PRF}{\max} = \frac{c}{2 \cdot R{\max}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 300 \times 10^3} = 500\ \text{Hz}$$

(2) For PRF = 500 Hz:
$$R_{\max} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 500} = 300\ \text{km}$$

This exactly meets the requirement. Seeing farther requires an even lower PRF, but that also reduces the unambiguous Doppler interval.

Problem 3: Fast-Time Sampling and Range Gates

A signal has bandwidth $B = 10\ \text{MHz}$ and baseband sample rate $F_s = 10\ \text{MHz}$, so $F_s = B$. PRF = 1 kHz and a pulse covers 150 km. Find (1) the number of range gates in one pulse and (2) the interval between range gates.

Show the solution

(1) One PRI lasts $T_{\text{PRI}} = 1/\text{PRF} = 1\ \text{ms}$.

The number of samples, or range gates, required to cover 150 km is:
$$N_{\text{gate}} = \frac{2 \times R}{c} \times F_s = \frac{2 \times 150 \times 10^3}{3 \times 10^8} \times 10^7 = 10000\ \text{个}$$

(2) Range-gate spacing:
$$\Delta R = \frac{c}{2F_s} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{2 \times 10^7} = 15\ \text{m}$$

Problem 4: I/Q Demodulation and Complex Signals

An IF signal at $f_I = 30\ \text{MHz}$ is band-pass sampled at $f_s = 40\ \text{MHz}$. Find its spectral position after sampling and explain how a DDC obtains the I/Q baseband signal.

Show the solution

The position of the $f_I = 30\ \text{MHz}$ signal in the digital domain is:
$$f_{\text{alias}} = |f_I - m \cdot f_s|$$

Taking $m = 1$:
$$f_{\text{alias}} = |30 - 40| = 10\ \text{MHz}$$

The 30 MHz IF has therefore moved to a 10 MHz digital IF.

DDC processing:

  1. Digital mixing: multiply the 10 MHz digital IF by $\cos(2\pi \cdot 10 \times 10^6 \cdot nT_s)$ and $-\sin(2\pi \cdot 10 \times 10^6 \cdot nT_s)$ to form the I and Q paths.
  2. Low-pass filtering: remove the $2f_I$ component produced by mixing.
  3. Decimation: reduce the data rate to a suitable value.

The final output is the complex I/Q baseband signal $s(n) = I(n) + jQ(n)$.

4.1 Representing Radar Signals

4.1.1 Understanding a Signal Model through Basketball

Everyday example: You throw a basketball toward the hoop. It flies outward, hits the rim, bounces back, and you catch it. In this process:

  • the ball you throw is the transmitted signal;
  • the ball bouncing from the hoop is target scattering;
  • the ball you catch is the received signal.

The flight time tells you the distance to the hoop, while the force with which the ball returns reveals the hoop’s “scattering characteristics.” A radar signal model is the mathematical language used to describe this process.

4.1.2 Transmitted-Signal Model

The transmitted signal of a pulsed radar can be modelled as:

$$
s_T(t) = \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{T_p}\right) \cdot \sin(2\pi f_c t) \tag{4.1}
$$

where the rectangular-pulse function is:

$$
\text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{T_p}\right) =
\begin{cases}
1, & |t| \leq T_p/2 \
0, & \text{其他}
\end{cases}
\tag{4.2}
$$

Symbols in Equations 4.1–4.2:

  • $s_T(t)$: transmitted signal in volts;
  • $\text{rect}(\cdot)$: dimensionless rectangular function describing the on–off pulse shape;
  • $T_p$: pulse width, or duration of transmission, in seconds;
  • $f_c$: carrier, or radar centre, frequency in hertz;
  • $t$: time in seconds.

Intuitively: during $T_p$, the radar transmits a sinusoid at $f_c$. Outside $T_p$, the transmitter is off and the radar “listens” for echoes.

4.1.3 Received-Signal Model

The received signal is the transmitted signal after outward propagation, target reflection, and return propagation, plus unavoidable noise:

$$
y(t) = k \cdot A(t - \tau_0) e^{j[2\pi f_c (t - \tau_0) + \phi(t)]} + n(t) \tag{4.3}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.3:

  • $y(t)$: received signal in volts;
  • $k$: dimensionless propagation attenuation coefficient, related to range;
  • $A(t)$: dimensionless pulse envelope;
  • $\tau_0 = 2R_0 / c$: echo delay in seconds;
  • $R_0$: target range in metres;
  • $c$: speed of light, $3\times 10^8$ m/s;
  • $f_c$: carrier frequency in hertz;
  • $\phi(t)$: additional phase introduced by target reflection, in radians;
  • $n(t)$: receiver thermal noise and other interference.

Key concept: received signal = a delayed version of the transmitted signal, where $\tau_0$ gives target range + an attenuated version, where $k$ reflects target size and range + noise, including ever-present thermal noise.

4.1.4 Determining Target Range

Range follows directly from the delay:

$$
R_0 = \frac{c \cdot \tau_0}{2} \tag{4.4}
$$

This is the fundamental radar ranging equation. The delay $\tau_0$ is measured as the interval between the transmitted pulse and the received echo.

4.2 The Radar Equation: The Most Important Formula in This Guide

4.2.1 What Is the Radar Equation?

Everyday example: You search for someone at night with a flashlight. The lamp’s brightness, corresponding to transmitted power, the beam’s concentration, corresponding to antenna gain, and your eyesight, corresponding to receiver sensitivity, together determine how far you can see. If the person is too far away, the reflected light is too weak to detect.

The radar equation describes this process quantitatively. It collects all important radar parameters in one formula and calculates the maximum range at which a target can be detected.

Why the radar equation matters:

  • System design: before building a radar, determine whether it can meet the required detection range.
  • Parameter analysis: identify which parameter influences range most strongly—antenna or power, for example.
  • Performance evaluation: estimate how far an existing radar can see or how small a target it can detect.

4.2.2 Step One: Power Density at the Target

Let the transmitter output power be $P_t$ watts.

With an isotropic antenna, which radiates uniformly in every direction, the power per unit area at range $R$, or power density, is:

$$
S_1’ = \frac{P_t}{4\pi R^2} \tag{4.5}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.5:

  • $S_1’$: power density at range R from an isotropic antenna, in $W/m^2$;
  • $P_t$: transmitter output power in watts;
  • $R$: target range in metres;
  • $4\pi R^2$: surface area of a sphere of radius R, in $m^2$.

Physical meaning: transmitted power spreads uniformly across the surface of a sphere of radius $R$. A more distant sphere is larger and receives less power per unit area: the inverse-square law.

A radar antenna is not isotropic. We want to concentrate energy toward the target. This ability is described by antenna gain $G$:

$$
S_1 = \frac{P_t G}{4\pi R^2} \tag{4.6}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.6:

  • $S_1$: power density at the target with a directional antenna, in $W/m^2$;
  • $G$: dimensionless antenna gain, usually expressed in dBi.

An intuitive picture of gain: an isotropic antenna resembles a light bulb illuminating every direction; a high-gain antenna resembles a flashlight concentrating energy in one direction. A gain of $G = 100$ means that the target-direction power density is one hundred times its isotropic value.

4.2.3 Step Two: Target Interception and Scattering

When an electromagnetic wave illuminates an aircraft, missile, or other target, the target intercepts some energy and scatters it in various directions.

Its scattering ability is described by radar cross section (RCS), denoted by $\sigma$ and measured in $m^2$.

An intuitive picture of RCS: it is the target’s “radar-visible area.” A metal sphere may have an RCS of 1 m², while a stealth fighter may have an RCS of only 0.005 m², comparable to a fist-sized metal ball.

The total intercepted power equals power density at the target $\times$ RCS:

$$
P_{\text{target}} = S_1 \cdot \sigma = \frac{P_t G \sigma}{4\pi R^2} \tag{4.7}
$$

After intercepting this energy, the target scatters it outward. It behaves like a new transmitter with power $P_{\text{target}}$. After propagation over range $R$ back to the receiving antenna, its power density is:

$$
S_2 = \frac{P_{\text{target}}}{4\pi R^2} = \frac{P_t G \sigma}{(4\pi R^2)^2} = \frac{P_t G \sigma}{16\pi^2 R^4} \tag{4.8}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.8:

  • $S_2$: echo power density at the receiving antenna, in $W/m^2$;
  • $\sigma$: target RCS in $m^2$.

4.2.4 Step Three: Echo Power Captured by the Receiving Antenna

The receiving antenna has an effective aperture $A_e$ in $m^2$, representing the effective area over which it captures electromagnetic energy.

The received echo power is:

$$
P_r = S_2 \cdot A_e = \frac{P_t G \sigma}{(4\pi)^2 R^4} \cdot A_e \tag{4.9}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.9:

  • $P_r$: received echo power in watts;
  • $A_e$: effective receiving-antenna aperture in $m^2$.

4.2.5 Step Four: Relating Antenna Gain to Effective Aperture

Antenna gain $G$ and effective aperture $A_e$ have the important relationship:

$$
G = \frac{4\pi A_e}{\lambda^2} \tag{4.10}
$$

or, rearranged:

$$
A_e = \frac{G \lambda^2}{4\pi} \tag{4.11}
$$

Symbols in Equations 4.10–4.11:

  • $\lambda$: wavelength, $\lambda = c/f_c$, in metres;
  • $G$: dimensionless antenna gain;
  • $A_e$: effective aperture in $m^2$.

Physical meaning: a higher-gain, more focused antenna has a larger equivalent aperture. For the same gain, a shorter wavelength permits a smaller aperture.

4.2.6 Step Five: Substitution Gives the Basic Radar Equation

Substitute Equation 4.11 into Equation 4.9:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_r &= \frac{P_t G \sigma}{(4\pi)^2 R^4} \cdot \frac{G \lambda^2}{4\pi} \
&= \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4}
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.12}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.12:

  • $P_r$: received power in watts;
  • $P_t$: transmitted power in watts;
  • $G$: dimensionless antenna gain, the same on transmit and receive when one antenna is shared;
  • $\lambda$: wavelength in metres;
  • $\sigma$: target RCS in $m^2$;
  • $R$: target range in metres;
  • $(4\pi)^3 = 64\pi^3 \approx 1984$: constant factor.

Meaning of the equation: received power $P_r$ is inversely proportional to the fourth power of range $R$. Doubling the range reduces received power to $1/16$ of its former value. This is why a radar needs a dramatic increase in power or antenna gain to see farther.

4.2.7 Step Six: Introducing System Losses

A real radar has many losses, represented together by a loss factor $L$, where $L > 1$:

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L} \tag{4.13}
$$

Symbol in Equation 4.13:

  • $L$: dimensionless total system loss, $L > 1$.

Common sources of loss:

Loss Typical value Source
Atmospheric absorption $L_a$ 1.2 dB Electromagnetic energy absorbed by the atmosphere
Antenna loss $L_{\text{ant}}$ 1.3 dB Non-ideal conductivity and efficiency of the antenna
Feeder loss $L_f$ 1.8 dB Energy loss in transmission lines
Receiver contribution $L_n$ 0.8 dB Noise and imperfections within the receiver

The linear total loss $L$ is the product of its components; their dB values add.

4.2.8 Step Seven: The Signal-to-Noise-Ratio Form

Whether a receiver detects a signal depends not on its absolute power alone but on its ratio to noise power: the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

Receiver noise power is:

$$
N = k T B_n F_n \tag{4.14}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.14:

  • $N$: noise power in watts;
  • $k$: Boltzmann constant, $1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K;
  • $T$: system temperature in kelvin, commonly 290 K, near room temperature;
  • $B_n$: receiver noise bandwidth in hertz, usually close to signal bandwidth;
  • $F_n$: dimensionless receiver noise factor describing additional noise introduced by the receiver.

The received SNR is therefore:

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{P_r}{N} = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 k T B_n F_n L} \tag{4.15}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.15:

  • SNR: dimensionless signal-to-noise ratio, sometimes given in dB as $10\log_{10}(\text{SNR})$;
  • $k$: Boltzmann constant, $1.38\times 10^{-23}$ J/K;
  • $T$: system noise temperature in kelvin;
  • $B_n$: noise bandwidth in hertz;
  • $F_n$: dimensionless noise factor.

Physical meaning of SNR: at SNR = 1, or 0 dB, signal and noise have equal power and detection is difficult. A practical radar commonly needs 10–20 dB for reliable detection.

4.2.9 Step Eight: Maximum Detection Range

Let the minimum SNR required for reliable detection be $SNR_{\min}$. Setting $SNR = SNR_{\min}$ and solving for $R$ gives:

$$
R_{\max} = \left[ \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 SNR_{\min} k T B_n F_n L} \right]^{1/4} \tag{4.16}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.16:

  • $R_{\max}$: maximum detection range in metres;
  • $SNR_{\min}$: dimensionless minimum detectable SNR, determined by the required probability of detection;
  • all other symbols are as defined above.

This equation is so important that it deserves close analysis.

4.2.11 Deeper Analysis of the Radar Equation

(1) Fourth-Power Range Loss

$R_{\max} \propto P_t^{1/4}$: doubling transmitted power increases maximum range by only about 19%, because $2^{1/4} \approx 1.19$.

$R_{\max} \propto G^{1/2}$: doubling antenna gain increases maximum range by about 41%, because $2^{1/2} \approx 1.41$.

Therefore:

  • increasing antenna gain is more effective than increasing transmitted power when the goal is longer range;
  • doubling range requires sixteen times as much power.

(2) Pulse-Integration Gain

A real CPI contains $M$ pulses. Coherently integrating those $M$ pulses can improve SNR by a factor of $M$:

$$
R_{\max} = \left[ \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma M}{(4\pi)^3 SNR_{\min} k T B_n F_n L} \right]^{1/4}
$$

Before integration, SNR = $P_r / N$; afterward, SNR = $M \cdot P_r / N$.

The same expression can use average power $P_{av} = P_t \cdot T_p / PRI$:

$$
R_{\max} = \left[ \frac{P_{av} G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma T_c}{(4\pi)^3 SNR_{\min} k T F_n L} \right]^{1/4}
$$

where $T_c = M \cdot PRI$ is the CPI duration and $T_p$ is the pulse width.

4.2.12 Example Application of the Radar Equation

Problem: A radar has:

  • $P_t = 1$ MW;
  • $G = 40$ dB, or $10^4$ in linear units;
  • $\lambda = 0.03$ m, corresponding to $f_c = 10$ GHz in X band;
  • $B_n = 5$ MHz;
  • $F_n = 3$ dB, or a factor of 2;
  • $L = 5$ dB, or a factor of 3.16;
  • target $\sigma = 1$ m²;
  • $SNR_{\min} = 13$ dB, or a factor of 20.

Find the maximum detection range.

Solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{\max} &= \left[ \frac{(10^6)(10^4)^2(0.03)^2(1)}{(4\pi)^3 (20)(1.38\times 10^{-23})(290)(5\times 10^6)(2)(3.16)} \right]^{1/4} \
&\approx \left[ \frac{9\times 10^{13}}{1984 \times 20 \times 1.38\times 10^{-23} \times 290 \times 5\times 10^6 \times 2 \times 3.16} \right]^{1/4} \
&\approx [2.3 \times 10^{20}]^{1/4} \
&\approx 123 \ \text{km}
\end{aligned}
$$

The radar’s detection range against a 1 m² target is approximately 123 km.

4.3 Target-Fluctuation Models and RCS

4.3.1 What Is RCS?

Everyday example: Shine a flashlight on a basketball and a table-tennis ball in the dark. The larger basketball normally reflects much more light. But what if its surface is matte while the smaller ball is mirrored? A small, smooth object can sometimes reflect more light than a large, rough one.

Radar cross section (RCS) quantitatively describes a target’s ability to reflect electromagnetic energy. It is usually denoted by $\sigma$ and measured in $m^2$.

The strict definition is:

$$
\sigma = \lim_{R\to\infty} 4\pi R^2 \frac{|E_s|^2}{|E_i|^2} \tag{4.17}
$$

Symbols in Equation 4.17:

  • $\sigma$: RCS in $m^2$;
  • $R$: distance from target to observation point in metres;
  • $E_s$: scattered electric-field strength in V/m;
  • $E_i$: incident electric-field strength in V/m.

In plain language: RCS is the apparent area with which a target reflects electromagnetic waves. A metal plate with a physical area of $1$ m² may, when facing the radar, have an RCS of $4\pi A^2/\lambda^2$, much larger than its actual area. Radar can therefore see a target as “larger than life.”

4.3.3 Factors That Affect RCS

  1. Target size: larger targets generally have greater RCS.
  2. Target shape: for the same projected area, a plate generally scatters more strongly than a sphere, and a sphere more strongly than a cone.
  3. Material: metal generally scatters far more strongly than a non-metal; better conductivity gives stronger reflection.
  4. Incidence angle: a plate facing the radar has a very large RCS, but from the side it may have a very small one.
  5. Frequency: RCS may vary greatly with frequency because of resonance.

4.3.4 Three Scattering Regions

According to the relationship between target dimension $a$ and wavelength $\lambda$, represented by $ka = 2\pi a/\lambda$:

Region $ka$ RCS characteristics Example
Rayleigh $ka \ll 1$ $\sigma \propto f^4$, changing steeply with frequency Raindrops and insects
Resonance $ka \approx 1$ Oscillatory and potentially greater than the optical-region value Small drones
Optical $ka \gg 1$ $\sigma \approx \text{投影面积}$ and relatively stable Aircraft and missiles

Intuitively:

  • In the Rayleigh region, wavelength is much larger than the target. Diffraction dominates and scattering is weak, like ocean waves bending around a rock.
  • In the resonance region, wavelength is comparable to target dimensions and resonant effects arise.
  • In the optical region, wavelength is much shorter than the target and scattering resembles optical reflection. This is a common operating region for radar.

4.3.5 Swerling Target-Fluctuation Models

A target’s RCS is not constant. It fluctuates randomly as target aspect changes. Swerling models describe those fluctuations statistically.

Swerling proposed four standard models. They combine two fluctuation rates, slow and fast, with two distributions, exponential and $\chi^2$.

Swerling I: Slow Fluctuation, Exponential Distribution

  • Probability density function: $p(\sigma) = \frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}} e^{-\sigma/\bar{\sigma}}$.
  • RCS remains constant throughout one CPI, giving slow fluctuation.
  • Pulses are correlated from one to the next.
  • Application: complex targets containing many independent scatterers, such as large aircraft.

Swerling II: Fast Fluctuation, Exponential Distribution

  • Same PDF as Swerling I: $p(\sigma) = \frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}} e^{-\sigma/\bar{\sigma}}$.
  • RCS changes independently from pulse to pulse, giving fast fluctuation.
  • Pulses are uncorrelated.
  • Application: complex targets undergoing rapid rotation or attitude changes.

Swerling III: Slow Fluctuation, $\chi^2$ Distribution with Four Degrees of Freedom

  • PDF: $p(\sigma) = \frac{4\sigma}{\bar{\sigma}^2} e^{-2\sigma/\bar{\sigma}}$.
  • The target contains one dominant reflector plus several smaller scatterers.
  • RCS remains constant throughout one CPI.
  • Application: targets with a large specular reflector, such as a wing surface facing the radar.

Swerling IV: Fast Fluctuation, $\chi^2$ Distribution with Four Degrees of Freedom

  • Same PDF as Swerling III.
  • RCS changes independently from pulse to pulse.
  • Application: rapidly manoeuvring targets with a dominant reflector.

4.3.6 RCS Reduction: The Principle of Stealth

Stealth technology essentially reduces RCS. It lowers $\sigma$ in the radar equation and thereby shortens the detection range $R_{\max}$.

The main methods are:

  1. Shaping: redirect reflections away from the radar.
    • Examples include the faceted F-117 and the smooth surfaces of the F-22 and B-2.
  2. Radar-absorbing material: convert electromagnetic energy into heat.
    • Examples include ferrite coatings and carbon-fibre composites.
  3. Passive cancellation: arrange for scattered waves to oppose and cancel the incident wave in phase.
  4. Active cancellation: transmit a signal opposite in phase to the echo.

Estimating the stealth effect:

If RCS falls from 1 m² to 0.001 m², a thousandfold, or 30 dB, reduction, the radar equation gives:

$$
R_{\max,\text{new}} = R_{\max,\text{old}} \cdot (0.001)^{1/4} \approx R_{\max,\text{old}} \cdot 0.178
$$

The detection range falls to 17.8% of its former value. Stealth does not make a target completely invisible; it drastically shortens the distance at which the target becomes visible.

Problem 1: Received Radar Power

A radar has transmitted power $P_t = 100\ \text{kW}$, antenna gain $G = 30\ \text{dB}$, target RCS $\sigma = 1\ \text{m}^2$, range $R = 50\ \text{km}$, wavelength $\lambda = 0.1\ \text{m}$, and system loss $L = 2\ \text{dB}$. Calculate the received echo power $P_r$.

Show the solution

Convert the decibel values to linear units:

  • $G_{\text{lin}} = 10^{30/10} = 1000$;
  • $L_{\text{lin}} = 10^{2/10} \approx 1.585$.

Substitute them into the radar equation:
$$P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L}$$

$$P_r = \frac{100 \times 10^3 \times (1000)^2 \times (0.1)^2 \times 1}{(4\pi)^3 \times (50 \times 10^3)^4 \times 1.585}$$

Step by step:

  • $P_t G^2 = 10^5 \times 10^6 = 10^{11}$;
  • $\lambda^2 \sigma = 0.01 \times 1 = 0.01$;
  • numerator: $10^{11} \times 0.01 = 10^9$;
  • $(4\pi)^3 \approx 1984.4$;
  • $R^4 = (50000)^4 = 6.25 \times 10^{18}$;
  • denominator: $1984.4 \times 6.25 \times 10^{18} \times 1.585 \approx 1.966 \times 10^{22}$.

$$P_r \approx \frac{10^9}{1.966 \times 10^{22}} \approx 5.09 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{W}$$

The result is approximately $5 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{W}$, an extremely weak signal.

Problem 2: Maximum Detection Range

Using the conditions in Problem 1, let the receiver noise figure be $F_n = 3\ \text{dB}$, noise bandwidth $B_n = 1\ \text{MHz}$, system temperature $T = 290\ \text{K}$, and minimum detectable SNR 13 dB. Calculate $R_{\max}$.

Show the solution

Noise power:
$$N = k T B_n F_n$$

With $k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ and $F_{n,\text{lin}} = 10^{3/10} \approx 2$:

$$N = 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 290 \times 10^6 \times 2 = 8.004 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{W}$$

Minimum detectable signal power:
$$\text{SNR}_{\min,\text{lin}} = 10^{13/10} \approx 20$$

$$P_{\min} = N \cdot \text{SNR}_{\min} = 8.004 \times 10^{-15} \times 20 = 1.601 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{W}$$

Maximum detection range:
$$R_{\max} = \left[\frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 P_{\min} L}\right]^{1/4}$$

$$R_{\max} = \left[\frac{10^9}{1984.4 \times 1.601 \times 10^{-13} \times 1.585}\right]^{1/4}$$

$$R_{\max} \approx \left[\frac{10^9}{5.04 \times 10^{-10}}\right]^{1/4} = [1.985 \times 10^{18}]^{1/4}$$

$$R_{\max} \approx 3.76 \times 10^4\ \text{m} \approx 37.6\ \text{km}$$

Problem 3: SNR Improvement from Pulse Integration

Using the same conditions, suppose $M = 16$ pulses are coherently integrated. (1) By how many decibels does SNR improve? (2) What is the maximum detection range with the new SNR?

Show the solution

(1) Coherently integrating M pulses improves SNR by a factor of M:
$$\text{SNR 提升} = 10\log_{10}(16) = 12.04\ \text{dB}$$

(2) The equivalent $P_{\min}$ becomes $1/16$ of its former value:
$$P_{\min}’ = P_{\min}/16 = 1.001 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{W}$$

The new maximum range is:
$$R_{\max}’ = \left[\frac{10^9}{1984.4 \times 1.001 \times 10^{-14} \times 1.585}\right]^{1/4}$$

$$R_{\max}’ \approx \left[\frac{10^9}{3.15 \times 10^{-11}}\right]^{1/4} = [3.175 \times 10^{19}]^{1/4}$$

$$R_{\max}’ \approx 7.5 \times 10^4\ \text{m} = 75\ \text{km}$$

Integrating sixteen pulses doubles the detection range from 37.6 km to 75 km.

Problem 4: RCS and Stealth

A fighter initially has RCS $5\ \text{m}^2$. Stealth design reduces it to $0.1\ \text{m}^2$. (1) By how many decibels does echo power fall? (2) With all other conditions unchanged, what fraction of the original maximum detection range remains?

Show the solution

(1) Echo power is proportional to RCS:
$$\Delta P_r = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{0.1}{5}\right) = 10\log_{10}(0.02) \approx -17\ \text{dB}$$

Echo power falls by about 17 dB, to 1/50 of its original value.

(2) Maximum range is proportional to the fourth root of RCS:
$$\frac{R_{\max}’}{R_{\max}} = \left(\frac{\sigma’}{\sigma}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{0.1}{5}\right)^{1/4} = (0.02)^{1/4} \approx 0.376$$

Stealth design reduces the detection range to about 37.6% of its original value. A target formerly detectable at 200 km would now be detectable only at about 75 km.

5.1 Matched Filters

5.1.1 Understanding a Matched Filter through a Stethoscope

Everyday example: A doctor’s stethoscope is optimized for the frequency range of a heartbeat; it is “matched” to the sound’s characteristics. Similarly, in a noisy room, your brain can match the melody of a familiar song and hear it despite surrounding noise.

A matched filter plays a similar role in radar. Designed specifically for a known transmitted signal, it maximizes the output signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and lets the radar detect an echo as reliably as possible amid noise.

5.1.2 Mathematical Definition

For a transmitted signal $s(t)$, the matched filter’s impulse response is:

$$
h(t) = K \cdot s(t_0 - t) \tag{5.1}
$$

In the frequency domain:

$$
H(f) = K \cdot S^*(f) e^{-j2\pi f t_0} \tag{5.2}
$$

Symbols in Equations 5.1–5.2:

  • $h(t)$: matched-filter impulse response;
  • $H(f)$: matched-filter frequency response;
  • $s(t)$: known transmitted reference signal;
  • $S(f)$: spectrum, or Fourier transform, of the transmitted signal;
  • $S^*(f)$: complex conjugate of $S(f)$;
  • $t_0$: time of maximum output SNR, usually the end of the pulse;
  • $K$: constant gain factor, which does not affect SNR.

Intuitively: the impulse response is a time-reversed, conjugated, and shifted copy of the transmitted signal. The filter “waits” for the signal to arrive, aligns with it, and produces its largest response.

5.1.3 Why Does a Matched Filter Maximize SNR?

Mathematical derivation, optional for readers who want the details:

Let the received signal be $y_{\text{in}}(t) = s(t) + n(t)$, where $n(t)$ is white noise with power spectral density $N_0/2$.

After filtering by $h(t)$:

$$
y_{\text{out}}(t) = y_{\text{in}}(t) * h(t) = s(t) * h(t) + n(t) * h(t) = y_s(t) + n_{\text{out}}(t)
$$

At $t = t_0$, the output SNR is:

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{|y_s(t_0)|^2}{E[|n_{\text{out}}(t)|^2]}
$$

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality proves that $h(t) = K \cdot s(t_0 - t)$ maximizes this ratio:

$$
\text{SNR}_{\max} = \frac{2E}{N_0} \tag{5.3}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.3:

  • $\text{SNR}_{\max}$: dimensionless maximum output SNR;
  • $E = \int |s(t)|^2 dt$: signal energy in joules;
  • $N_0$: input white-noise power spectral density in W/Hz.

The conclusion is simple: maximum matched-filter SNR depends only on signal energy and noise spectral density, not on the particular waveform. Whatever pulse shape is transmitted, use a filter matched to that shape.

5.1.4 Matched-Filter Output

When $s(t)$ passes through its matched filter $h(t)$, the output is its autocorrelation:

$$
y_s(t) = s(t) * h(t) = K \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s(\tau) s^*(\tau - (t - t_0)) d\tau = K \cdot R_{ss}(t - t_0) \tag{5.4}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.4:

  • $y_s(t)$: matched-filter output;
  • $R_{ss}(\cdot)$: signal autocorrelation;
  • $*$: convolution.

Key point: the output is the transmitted signal’s autocorrelation, which is greatest at zero lag ($\tau = 0$). This is why the output peaks at $t_0$.

5.1.5 Example: Matched Filtering a Rectangular Pulse

Suppose the transmitted signal is a simple rectangular pulse:

$$
s(t) =
\begin{cases}
1, & 0 \leq t \leq T_p \
0, & \text{其他}
\end{cases}
$$

The matched filter is:

$$
h(t) = s(T_p - t) =
\begin{cases}
1, & 0 \leq t \leq T_p \
0, & \text{其他}
\end{cases}
$$

Its output is a triangular pulse:

$$
y_s(t) =
\begin{cases}
t, & 0 \leq t \leq T_p \
2T_p - t, & T_p \leq t \leq 2T_p \
0, & \text{其他}
\end{cases}
$$

Physical meaning: the output peaks at $t = T_p$, with peak amplitude $T_p$ and maximum SNR.

5.1.6 Digital Implementation

A practical matched filter has two common implementations.

1. Time-domain convolution, for short pulses:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} x[k] \cdot h[n-k]
$$

Direct computation has complexity $O(N^2)$.

2. Frequency-domain multiplication, for long pulses:

Its complexity is $O(N\log N)$, far lower than time-domain convolution for long signals.

5.1.7 The Effect of Doppler Mismatch

If the target moves, its echo contains a Doppler shift $f_d$ in addition to a time delay:

$$
s’(t) = s(t) e^{j2\pi f_d t}
$$

The matched-filter output becomes:

$$
y(t; f_d) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s(\tau) e^{j2\pi f_d \tau} s^*(\tau - (t - t_0)) d\tau
$$

Conclusion: a large $f_d$ lowers the output peak, so target motion can degrade detection. Slow-time Doppler filtering is therefore required to compensate for it.

5.2 The Ambiguity Function

5.2.1 Understanding Ambiguity through an Eye Chart

Everyday example: During an eye test, a doctor asks you to identify the direction of the gap in a letter C. With mild myopia, you can distinguish the gap clearly—high resolution. With severe myopia, you see only a blurred ring and cannot locate the gap.

Radar faces similar questions: If two targets are close together, can it resolve them as separate objects? If their velocities are close, can it distinguish their Doppler frequencies?

The ambiguity function answers these questions. It describes a radar waveform’s resolution in two dimensions: range, represented by delay, and velocity, represented by Doppler.

5.2.2 Definition of the Ambiguity Function

The ambiguity function is the squared magnitude of a suitably normalized matched-filter output:

$$
|\chi(\tau, f_d)|^2 = \left| \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} s(t) s^*(t + \tau) e^{j2\pi f_d t} dt \right|^2 \tag{5.5}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.5:

  • $\chi(\tau, f_d)$: complex ambiguity function;
  • $\tau$: delay difference, corresponding to target range separation, in seconds;
  • $f_d$: Doppler difference, corresponding to target velocity separation, in hertz;
  • $s(t)$: complex envelope of the transmitted signal;
  • $s^*(t)$: complex conjugate.

In plain language: when a real target lies at $(\tau=0, f_d=0)$, the ambiguity function describes how strongly the radar might also interpret it as lying at another $(\tau, f_d)$.

5.2.3 Properties of the Ambiguity Function

  1. Maximum at the origin: $|\chi(0, 0)|^2 = E^2$, where E is signal energy.
  2. Volume invariance: $\iint |\chi(\tau, f_d)|^2 d\tau df_d = E^2$; the volume under the surface is constant.
  3. Symmetry: $|\chi(-\tau, -f_d)| = |\chi(\tau, f_d)|$.
  4. Zero-Doppler cut: $|\chi(\tau, 0)|$ shows range resolution.
  5. Zero-delay cut: $|\chi(0, f_d)|$ shows velocity, or Doppler, resolution.

5.2.4 The Ideal “Thumbtack” Ambiguity Function

An ideal ambiguity function has a thumbtack shape:

  • a very sharp peak at the origin, giving high resolution;
  • an otherwise flat surface near zero, giving little ambiguity and low side lobes.

Meaning of the thumbtack shape:

Property Requirement Ideal value
Range resolution Main-lobe width of the zero-Doppler cut $\rightarrow 0$
Doppler resolution Main-lobe width of the zero-delay cut $\rightarrow 0$
Range ambiguity Side lobes along the delay axis $\rightarrow 0$
Doppler ambiguity Side lobes along the frequency axis $\rightarrow 0$
Total volume Area under the surface Constant and unchangeable

However, volume invariance prevents all side lobes from becoming zero simultaneously. This is the fundamental constraint of radar waveform design, an uncertainty principle: with fixed total energy, resolution and side-lobe level must be traded against each other.

5.2.5 Ambiguity Function of a Simple Pulse

For a rectangular pulse of width $T_p$:

$$
|\chi(\tau, f_d)| = \left| \left(1 - \frac{|\tau|}{T_p}\right) \frac{\sin[\pi f_d (T_p - |\tau|)]}{\pi f_d (T_p - |\tau|)} \right|, \quad |\tau| \leq T_p
$$

Zero-Doppler cut along the $\tau$ axis, corresponding to range:

$$
|\chi(\tau, 0)| = 1 - \frac{|\tau|}{T_p}, \quad |\tau| \leq T_p
$$

This is a triangle. Its 3 dB, or half-power, width determines range resolution: $\tau_{\text{3dB}} \approx T_p$, so $\Delta R = c \cdot T_p / 2$. A wider pulse gives poorer range resolution.

Zero-delay cut along the $f_d$ axis, corresponding to Doppler or velocity:

$$
|\chi(0, f_d)| = \left| \frac{\sin(\pi f_d T_p)}{\pi f_d T_p} \right|
$$

This is a sinc function. Its 3 dB width gives Doppler resolution: $f_{d,\text{3dB}} \approx 1/T_p$. A wider pulse gives finer Doppler resolution.

The conflict in a simple pulse:

Parameter Wide pulse Narrow pulse
Range resolution Poor, large $\Delta R$ Fine, small $\Delta R$
Doppler resolution Fine, small $\Delta f_d$ Poor, large $\Delta f_d$
Detection range Long, because average power is high Short, because peak power is limited

Here lies the conflict: a wide pulse supports long-range detection and fine Doppler resolution but sacrifices range resolution. A narrow pulse does the opposite. How can radar obtain both long range and fine range resolution? The answer is pulse compression.

5.3 Pulse Compression

5.3.1 Why Is Pulse Compression Needed?

Review of the central conflict:

A simple pulse has two opposing performance measures:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{最大探测距离} &\propto T_p \quad (\text{脉宽越大，能量越大，看得越远}) \
\text{距离分辨率} &\propto \frac{1}{T_p} \quad (\text{脉宽越大，分辨率越差})
\end{aligned}
$$

Everyday example: Suppose you strike a distant bell. A hard, sustained strike—a wide pulse—produces a loud sound that travels far, but if two bells stand close together, their long sounds overlap and you cannot distinguish them. A brief, light strike—a narrow pulse—lets you separate the bells, but the weak sound does not travel far.

Pulse compression resolves this conflict. The radar transmits a wide pulse for long range, but specially modulates it so that its internal structure changes, perhaps in frequency. After reception, a matched filter “compresses” it into a narrow pulse for fine resolution.

Core idea:

  • On transmission: use a wide pulse for high energy and long range.
  • After reception: use a matched filter to compress the pulse width and obtain fine resolution.
  • Essential condition: time–bandwidth product $BT \gg 1$.

5.3.2 Pulse-Compression Ratio

Compression performance is measured by the compression ratio:

$$
\text{压缩比} = \frac{\text{输入脉宽}}{\text{输出脉宽}} = \frac{T_p}{\tau_{\text{out}}} = B \cdot T_p \tag{5.6}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.6:

  • $T_p$: transmitted, or input, pulse width in seconds;
  • $\tau_{\text{out}}$: compressed, or output, pulse width in seconds;
  • $B$: signal bandwidth in hertz;
  • $B \cdot T_p$: dimensionless time–bandwidth product.

Example: let $T_p = 100$ μs and $B = 10$ MHz.

  • Compression ratio $= BT_p = 10^7 \times 10^{-4} = 1000$.
  • Output width $\tau_{\text{out}} = 100$ μs / 1000 = 0.1 μs.
  • Range resolution $\Delta R = c \cdot \tau_{\text{out}} / 2 = 3\times 10^8 \times 10^{-7} / 2 = 15$ m.

The radar transmits a wide 100 μs pulse with high energy and long range, but obtains the fifteen-metre resolution of a 0.1 μs pulse. It has effectively compressed the pulse by a factor of one thousand.

5.3.3 Implementing Compression with a Matched Filter

Pulse compression is implemented by the matched filter introduced in Section 5.1:

  1. Transmit a modulated signal with a large time–bandwidth product, such as an LFM waveform.
  2. Use a receiver filter matched to the transmitted waveform.
  3. The output autocorrelation is a narrow, compressed pulse.

The processing chain:

5.3.4 Why Must the Time–Bandwidth Product Be Large?

Recall a simple rectangular pulse:

  • duration = $T_p$;
  • bandwidth ≈ $1/T_p$;
  • time–bandwidth product $BT_p \approx 1$, so it cannot be compressed.

Compression requires internal variation, or modulation, such that $BT_p \gg 1$.

Waveform Duration $T_p$ Bandwidth $B$ $BT_p$ Compressible?
Simple rectangular pulse 10 μs ~0.1 MHz ~1 No
Linear FM 100 μs 10 MHz 1,000 Yes, ratio 1,000
Phase coding 100 μs 10 MHz 1,000 Yes, ratio 1,000

5.3.5 Two Implementations of Pulse Compression

Method one: time-domain convolution, or matched filtering

$$
y(t) = s_r(t) * h(t) = \int s_r(u) h(t-u) du
$$

  • Suitable for shorter pulses.
  • Computational cost $O(N^2)$.

Method two: frequency-domain multiplication with FFTs

  • Suitable for longer pulses.
  • Computational cost $O(N\log N)$, much faster.

5.3.6 The Side-Lobe Problem

The matched-filter output is an autocorrelation. For an LFM signal, it is approximately a sinc function, whose first side lobe is only about 13 dB below the main lobe. A weak target beside a strong one may therefore be hidden by the strong target’s side lobe.

The solution: windowing, or weighting

Applying a Hamming, Hann, Blackman, or other window before matched filtering suppresses side lobes:

Window First side lobe Main-lobe broadening SNR loss
Rectangular, no window −13 dB 1.0× 0 dB
Hamming −43 dB 1.5× ~1.4 dB
Hann −32 dB 1.6× ~1.8 dB
Blackman −58 dB 1.9× ~2.4 dB

Trade-off: windowing lowers side lobes but broadens the main lobe, worsening resolution, and also costs SNR.

5.4 Linear Frequency Modulation

5.4.1 What Is an LFM Signal?

Everyday example: Imagine a whistle whose pitch rises continuously from low to high, like a siren. Its frequency changes linearly with time, making it a linear frequency-modulated (LFM) signal, also called a chirp.

LFM is the most common pulse-compression waveform. Its central idea is to change frequency linearly within a wide pulse, assigning a different frequency to each part of the pulse and thereby “marking” internal time.

5.4.2 Mathematical Expression

The complex LFM signal is:

$$
s(t) = \text{rect}\left(\frac{t}{T_p}\right) \cdot \exp\left(j\pi K t^2\right) \tag{5.7}
$$

where the chirp rate $K$ is:

$$
K = \frac{B}{T_p} \tag{5.8}
$$

Symbols in Equations 5.7–5.8:

  • $s(t)$: complex envelope of the LFM signal;
  • $\text{rect}(t/T_p)$: rectangular pulse of width $T_p$;
  • $\exp(j\pi K t^2)$: quadratic-phase term producing linear frequency modulation;
  • $K$: chirp rate in Hz/s;
  • $B$: signal bandwidth in hertz;
  • $T_p$: pulse width in seconds.

Instantaneous frequency:

$$
f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{d}{dt}[\pi K t^2] = Kt = \frac{B}{T_p} t \tag{5.9}
$$

Intuitively: at the beginning, $t = -T_p/2$ and the instantaneous frequency is $-B/2$. At the end, $t = T_p/2$ and the frequency is $B/2$. It sweeps linearly from low to high across total bandwidth $B$.

5.4.3 Waveform Characteristics

In the time domain:

  • amplitude is constant inside a rectangular envelope;
  • frequency changes linearly, either upward or downward;
  • phase is quadratic in time, $\phi(t) = \pi K t^2$.

In the frequency domain, for large $BT_p$:

  • the magnitude spectrum is approximately rectangular over $[-B/2, B/2]$;
  • the phase spectrum is approximately quadratic;
  • when $BT_p \geq 100$, the magnitude spectrum is very close to rectangular.

5.4.4 Matched-Filter Output of an LFM Signal

After matched filtering, the output is approximately:

$$
y(t) \approx \sqrt{BT_p} \cdot \frac{\sin(\pi B t)}{\pi B t} = \sqrt{BT_p} \cdot \text{sinc}(Bt) \tag{5.10}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.10:

  • $y(t)$: matched-filter output amplitude;
  • $\sqrt{BT_p}$: compressed peak-amplitude gain relative to the input;
  • $\text{sinc}(Bt)$: sinc-shaped output with main-lobe width about $1/B$.

Key conclusions:

  1. Amplitude gain: peak amplitude increases by $\sqrt{BT_p}$, corresponding to power gain $BT_p$.
  2. Pulse compression: output width is about $1/B$, a reduction by $BT_p$ from $T_p$.
  3. First side lobe: approximately −13 dB relative to the main lobe.

5.4.5 LFM Ambiguity Function

The LFM ambiguity function exhibits range–Doppler coupling:

$$
|\chi(\tau, f_d)| \approx \left| \left(1 - \frac{|\tau|}{T_p}\right) \frac{\sin[\pi(B\tau - f_d T_p)(1 - |\tau|/T_p)]}{\pi(B\tau - f_d T_p)(1 - |\tau|/T_p)} \right|
$$

Its peak lies along the line $\tau = f_d/K$ instead of being symmetric about horizontal and vertical axes through the origin.

Consequently:

  • If a target has Doppler shift $f_d$, the matched-filter peak is displaced in time by $\Delta\tau = f_d/K$.
  • This creates a range error $\Delta R = c \cdot f_d / (2K)$.

Physical interpretation: because LFM frequency changes with time, a Doppler shift changes the received frequency. The matched filter mistakes this frequency shift for the frequency belonging to a different instant in the chirp, creating a delay, and therefore range, displacement.

How can it be corrected?

  • Jointly process up- and down-chirps and average their range estimates.
  • Compensate Doppler before pulse compression.

5.4.6 Example of LFM Parameter Design

Problem: Design an LFM pulse-compression radar with:

  • range resolution $\Delta R \leq 1.5$ m;
  • maximum detection range $R_{\max} \geq 150$ km.

Step 1: Determine bandwidth

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} \quad \Rightarrow \quad B = \frac{c}{2\Delta R} = \frac{3\times 10^8}{2 \times 1.5} = 100 \text{ MHz}
$$

Step 2: Determine pulse width

To see a target at 150 km, pulse energy must be sufficient. Suppose peak power is 1 MW:

$$
E = P_t \cdot T_p = 10^6 \cdot T_p
$$

Given a receiver requirement of $SNR_{\min} = 13$ dB, the radar equation can provide the required pulse energy and hence $T_p$.

Suppose it gives a pulse width of 100 μs.

Step 3: Calculate compression ratio

$$
\text{压缩比} = B \cdot T_p = 100 \times 10^6 \times 100 \times 10^{-6} = 10000
$$

Conclusion: the radar transmits a wide 100 μs pulse for high energy and long range, then compresses it to the equivalent of a narrow 0.01 μs pulse, giving $\Delta R = 1.5$ m. The compression ratio is 10,000.

5.4.8 An LFM Variant: Nonlinear Frequency Modulation

One disadvantage of LFM is its relatively high −13 dB side lobe. A nonlinear frequency-modulated (NLFM) waveform addresses this problem by varying the chirp rate according to a chosen function instead of keeping it constant.

Advantages of NLFM:

  • low side lobes without windowing, avoiding the associated SNR loss;
  • side lobes below −40 dB are possible.

Disadvantages of NLFM:

  • greater sensitivity to Doppler, with more severe degradation for moving targets;
  • somewhat greater generation and processing complexity.

5.5 Range Resolution

5.5.1 What Is Range Resolution?

Everyday example: Draw two parallel lines very close together. At a separation of perhaps 0.1 mm, your unaided eye cannot distinguish them and sees one thick line. Only beyond some minimum separation do they appear as two lines.

Radar range resolution is the minimum separation at which two neighbouring targets can be distinguished. If they are closer than the resolution, radar “sees” one target.

5.5.2 Formula for Range Resolution

For a pulse-compression radar, signal bandwidth determines range resolution:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2B} \tag{5.11}
$$

Symbols in Equation 5.11:

  • $\Delta R$: range resolution in metres;
  • $c$: speed of light, $3\times 10^8$ m/s;
  • $B$: signal bandwidth in hertz.

For a simple pulse without compression:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c T_p}{2} \tag{5.12}
$$

Symbol in Equation 5.12:

  • $T_p$: pulse width in seconds.

5.5.3 Why Does Bandwidth Determine Resolution?

The matched-filter output is $\text{sinc}(Bt)$. Two targets separated by $\Delta\tau$ produce two $\text{sinc}$ peaks along the time axis. The Rayleigh criterion says they are resolvable when the peak of one main lobe lies at the first zero of the other.

The first zero is at $t = \pm 1/B$, giving time resolution $1/B$ and therefore range resolution:

$$
\Delta R = \frac{c}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{B} = \frac{c}{2B}
$$

Intuitively: greater bandwidth covers a wider frequency interval and produces a narrower compressed $\text{sinc}$ main lobe, making two nearby targets easier to separate.

There is only one route: increase signal bandwidth.

5.5.6 Physical Limits on Range Resolution

Increasing bandwidth encounters practical limits:

  1. Spectrum availability: radio spectrum is limited and bands require authorization.
  2. Hardware bandwidth: transmitters, receivers, and ADCs all have finite bandwidth.
  3. Atmospheric absorption: some bands, including 60 GHz and 120 GHz, are absorbed strongly by the atmosphere.
  4. Sample rate: ADC sample rate must be at least as great as the bandwidth under the stated baseband convention.

5.5.7 Why Range Resolution Matters

  1. Target recognition: fine resolution reveals structures such as aircraft wings and engines.
  2. Clutter suppression: a narrow range gate contains less total clutter.
  3. Multiple-target separation: distinct targets remain distinguishable in dense environments.
  4. Imaging: high resolution in range is central to SAR and ISAR imaging.
  5. Precision guidance: accurate target range supports missile guidance.

Learning Objectives

  1. A matched filter is the optimal linear filter for maximizing output SNR; its impulse response is a time-reversed transmitted signal.
  2. The ambiguity function analyses waveform resolution. A thumbtack is ideal, but volume invariance imposes a trade-off.
  3. A simple pulse has a fundamental conflict: a wide pulse sees farther but resolves poorly, while a narrow pulse resolves finely but sees less far.
  4. Pulse compression uses a signal with large time–bandwidth product, $BT_p \gg 1$, to decouple range and resolution.
  5. LFM is the most common pulse-compression waveform. Its frequency changes linearly and its compressed output is a $\text{sinc}$ function.
  6. Range resolution $\Delta R = c/(2B)$ depends only on bandwidth, not pulse width.
  7. Range–Doppler coupling is inherent in LFM and biases a moving target’s measured range.

Chapter 6 Doppler Processing

6.1 Clutter Characteristics

6.1.1 What Is Clutter?

Everyday example: Imagine standing in a noisy market and trying to hear a friend call your name from ten metres away. Food stalls are sizzling, crowds are talking, and children are crying. These “unwanted sounds” make your friend’s voice difficult to distinguish. If your friend claps once instead of calling your name, you may not notice the brief sound at all in that environment.

In the radar world, clutter is the equivalent of those unwanted sounds. It consists of echoes received by the radar from objects other than the target. Clutter is not noise. Clutter is an “echo that should not be there,” whereas noise is a random signal produced by electronic motion inside the receiver.

Formal definition: Clutter is the echo produced when electromagnetic waves transmitted by a radar illuminate non-target objects such as the ground, buildings, trees, sea waves, or raindrops. In form, these echoes are no different from target echoes—both are caused by electromagnetic reflection—but their sources are objects that do not interest us.

Radar example: A radar scans the area around an airport to detect drones in the air. Buildings, trees, and even vehicles on the ground all produce strong echoes that can bury the weak echo from a drone. Without clutter processing, the radar display would be filled with “bright spots” from ground objects, and the drone would be impossible to see.

6.1.2 Three Main Types of Clutter

(1) Ground Clutter

Ground clutter comes from reflections off the ground. Buildings, hills, trees, and utility poles all generate echoes.

Ground clutter has the following characteristics:

  • High intensity: Echoes from nearby terrain may be tens of decibels stronger than target echoes.
  • Fixed position: Ground objects do not move, so their echoes have zero Doppler frequency.
  • Concentrated distribution: It is concentrated in the area near the radar.

Imagine shining a torch at a small distant object while a white wall stands in front of it. The light reflected by the wall is far stronger than that reflected by the small object. Ground clutter is that “white wall.”

(2) Sea Clutter

Sea clutter comes from electromagnetic waves reflected by the sea surface. It differs from ground clutter because the sea surface is always moving.

Sea clutter has the following characteristics:

  • Dynamic variation: Waves keep moving, so sea clutter has a Doppler spread.
  • Dependence on sea state: It is weak when the sea is calm and strong in high winds and heavy waves.
  • Dependence on polarisation: Vertically and horizontally polarised sea clutter have very different characteristics.

The velocity of sea clutter is generally 5–10 m/s under moderate wind conditions. In coastal or harbour environments, its Doppler velocity is usually 5–50 m/s.

(3) Weather Clutter

Rain, snow, hail, and other weather particles produce weather clutter. It has the following characteristics:

  • Volume distribution: Unlike ground clutter, which is distributed over a surface, weather clutter occupies a volume of space.
  • Doppler spread: Raindrops moving in the wind produce Doppler shifts.
  • Relatively weak echoes: They are usually weaker than ground clutter, but they can still mask a target.

The Doppler-velocity range of weather clutter is normally 30–100 m/s in storms, while extreme weather such as tornadoes can produce velocities of more than 150 m/s.

6.1.3 Spectral Distribution of Clutter

Now that we know what clutter is, we can ask a more important question: what does its spectrum look like?

A Gaussian model is commonly used to describe the clutter spectrum. “Gaussian” means a bell-shaped curve: high in the middle and low on both sides.

Clutter power spectral density:

$$
W(f) = W_0 \exp\left(-\frac{f^2}{2\sigma_c^2}\right) = W_0 \exp\left(-\frac{f^2}{8\sigma_v^2/\lambda^2}\right)
$$

where:

  • $W(f)$: clutter power density at frequency $f$;
  • $W_0$: power density at the centre of the spectrum, which is the maximum value;
  • $\sigma_c$: standard deviation of the clutter spectrum, in Hz;
  • $\sigma_v$: standard deviation of clutter velocity, in m/s, which represents the spread of clutter motion;
  • $\lambda$: radar operating wavelength, in metres.

Physical meaning: This formula tells us that clutter energy is concentrated mainly around a central frequency. For stationary clutter such as terrain, the central frequency is 0 Hz because the Doppler shift is zero. The wider the clutter spectrum—that is, the larger $\sigma_c$ is—the more violently the clutter is moving.

6.1.4 How Clutter Affects Radar

Clutter directly affects radar detection performance. More specifically, it:

  1. Reduces detectability: Strong clutter echoes mask weak target echoes.
  2. Produces false alarms: Strong clutter may be mistaken for a target.
  3. Limits operating range: Clutter restricts the radar’s ability to detect slow targets.

Basic solution:

  • If the target is moving while the clutter is stationary, they can be separated by their Doppler shifts.
  • This is the purpose of MTI (Moving Target Indication), introduced in Section 6.3.

6.2 The Doppler Effect

6.2.1 Starting with an Everyday Phenomenon

You may have had this experience:

You stand at a railway crossing and wait for a train to pass. As the distant train approaches while sounding its horn, the horn seems sharp and high-pitched because its frequency is high. Once the train passes and moves away, the horn suddenly sounds lower because its frequency is lower. The horn’s own frequency has not changed, but the frequency you hear has.

This is the Doppler effect: when there is relative motion between a wave source and an observer, the frequency received by the observer changes.

For sound waves: train approaches → waves are “compressed” → frequency rises → pitch becomes higher.
For electromagnetic waves: target approaches radar → waves are “compressed” → echo frequency rises → Doppler shift is positive.

6.2.2 Doppler-Shift Formula

Core formula (you must remember it):

$$
f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}
$$

where:

  • $f_d$: Doppler shift, in Hz;
  • $v$: target radial velocity, in m/s—that is, the projection of the target’s velocity relative to the radar onto the radar line of sight;
  • $\lambda$: wavelength of the electromagnetic wave transmitted by the radar, in metres;
  • factor 2: the electromagnetic wave travels the distance twice, on the outward and return paths.

Determining direction:

  • Target approaches the radar: $v > 0$, $f_d > 0$ (echo frequency increases).
  • Target moves away from the radar: $v < 0$, $f_d < 0$ (echo frequency decreases).
  • Target is stationary or moving tangentially: $v = 0$, $f_d = 0$ (no Doppler shift).

Key insight: echoes from moving targets are shifted in frequency, while echoes from stationary clutter are not.

6.2.3 Numerical Example

Suppose an X-band radar has a wavelength of $\lambda = 0.03\text{ m}$ (3 cm), and a drone approaches it at $v = 50\text{ m/s}$:

$$
f_d = \frac{2 \times 50}{0.03} = \frac{100}{0.03} \approx 3333\text{ Hz}
$$

The echo from the moving target is therefore about 3,333 Hz higher than the transmitted frequency. By contrast, the echo from a building on the ground has the same frequency as the transmitted signal ($f_d = 0$). The radar can use this difference of 3,333 Hz to separate the moving target from stationary clutter.

Now consider the complete derivation.

The radar transmits $s(t) = A\cos(2\pi f_c t)$, where $f_c$ is the carrier frequency.

If a target at range $R_0$ moves with radial velocity $v$, its instantaneous range is:
$$R(t) = R_0 + vt$$

The echo delay is $\tau(t) = 2R(t)/c$, so the echo signal is:
$$s_r(t) = A_r\cos[2\pi f_c(t - \frac{2R(t)}{c})] = A_r\cos[2\pi f_c t - \frac{4\pi f_c R(t)}{c}]$$

Substituting $R(t) = R_0 + vt$ and $\lambda = c/f_c$ gives:
$$s_r(t) = A_r\cos[2\pi f_c t - \frac{4\pi R_0}{\lambda} - \frac{4\pi v}{\lambda}t]$$

The term $-\frac{4\pi v}{\lambda}t$ is equivalent to a frequency change of $f_d = \frac{2v}{\lambda}$.

Summary: A moving target’s echo consists of the transmitted signal, a phase caused by fixed delay, and a changing phase caused by the Doppler shift.

6.2.4 Quadrature Demodulation (I/Q Demodulation)

To extract Doppler-shift information, a radar must perform quadrature demodulation, also called I/Q demodulation. This is a crucial step in radar signal processing.

Why use I/Q demodulation?

Put simply, a single signal channel cannot distinguish positive from negative frequencies. Imagine watching a pendulum swing from side to side: you cannot tell whether it corresponds to clockwise or anticlockwise rotation. I/Q demodulation gives you two orthogonal views, allowing you to distinguish the direction of motion—approaching or receding.

The I- and Q-channel signals combine into the complex signal $I + jQ = Ae^{j\phi}$, whose phase $\phi$ contains information about target range and Doppler.

6.2.5 Doppler Sampling in Pulse Radar

An actual radar transmits not a continuous wave, but a sequence of pulses. The echo from each pulse is sampled at a particular time.

Suppose the pulse repetition interval (PRI) is $T_r$. The sampling instants are then $t = nT_r$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).

At instant $n$, the phase of the echo is:
$$\phi(n) = 2\pi f_d \cdot nT_r + \phi_0$$

The complex signal sequence after I/Q demodulation is therefore:
$$x(n) = Ae^{j(2\pi f_d n T_r + \phi_0)}$$

This is simply a complex sinusoid of frequency $f_d$. Spectral analysis, or an FFT, of $x(n)$ directly extracts the Doppler frequency $f_d$.

Core relationship: The radar transmits a pulse train and takes one sample from each pulse. Along the “slow-time” axis, these samples form a Doppler signal whose frequency is $f_d$.

6.3 Moving Target Indication (MTI)

6.3.1 Basic Principle

Everyday example: Imagine sitting in a room and looking through a window at the view outside. Raindrops on the glass blur your vision. If you blink rapidly, however, the raindrops change position while a distant mountain remains almost unchanged. By comparing the scene before and after the blink, your brain filters out the nearby moving raindrops and sees the stationary distant landscape.

MTI works by the same kind of comparison. It compares the echoes from two adjacent pulses. If an echo at a particular position does not change, it is stationary clutter and is cancelled. If it changes, it is a moving target and is retained.

Formal definition: MTI (Moving Target Indicator) is a technique that uses the Doppler effect to suppress stationary clutter while preserving echoes from moving targets.

6.3.2 Single-Delay Canceller

The simplest MTI is a single-delay canceller, whose mathematical expression is:

$$
y(t) = x(t) - x(t - T)
$$

where:

  • $x(t)$: echo from the current pulse;
  • $x(t - T)$: echo from the previous pulse, where $T$ is the pulse repetition interval;
  • $y(t)$: output after cancellation.

How it works:

  • For stationary clutter: $x(t) \approx x(t - T)$, so $y(t) \approx 0$ and the clutter is cancelled.
  • For a moving target: the phase changes between pulses, so $x(t) \neq x(t - T)$, $y(t) \neq 0$, and the target is preserved.

6.3.3 Frequency-Response Analysis

The transfer function of a single-delay canceller is:
$$H(f) = 1 - e^{-j2\pi f T}$$

Its magnitude-frequency response, the variation of magnitude with frequency, is:
$$|H(f)| = |1 - e^{-j2\pi f T}| = |2\sin(\pi f T)|$$

This is the MTI frequency-response formula. The following sketch shows its form:

Interpreting the graph:

  • The horizontal axis is frequency $f$, and the vertical axis is magnitude response $|H(f)|$.
  • At $f = 0$ (stationary clutter), $|H(0)| = 0$, so clutter is suppressed completely.
  • At $f = n/T = n\cdot PRF$ (integer multiples of the PRF), $|H(f)| = 0$, so these frequencies are also suppressed.
  • At $f = (2n+1)/(2T) = (n+0.5)PRF$, $|H(f)| = 2$, which is the maximum gain.

MTI is therefore essentially a high-pass filter, or a band-stop filter: it passes moving targets at higher frequencies and blocks stationary clutter at low frequencies.

There is, however, a serious problem.

6.3.4 The Blind-Speed Problem

This is an extremely important concept. Look carefully:

When a target’s Doppler frequency is $f_d = n \cdot PRF$, where $n$ is an integer, $|H(f_d)| = 0$ and the target signal is suppressed completely as well.

Why does this happen?

Recall the Doppler-shift formula $f_d = 2v/\lambda$. When $f_d = n \cdot PRF$:
$$v = \frac{n \cdot \lambda \cdot PRF}{2}$$

This velocity is called a blind speed. A radar with MTI cannot see a target travelling at a blind speed.

Physical meaning: Imagine that between consecutive pulses, the target moves by exactly an integer multiple of a wavelength. To the radar, the echo phase looks as though the target has not moved. It perfectly matches the signature of stationary clutter and is cancelled with it.

Blind-speed formula: $v_{blind} = n \cdot \frac{\lambda \cdot PRF}{2}$

Methods for resolving blind speeds:

  1. Staggered PRF: Alternate between different PRFs so that the positions of blind speeds change.
  2. Multiple-PRF techniques: See Section 6.5.

6.3.5 Double-Delay Canceller (Three-Pulse Cancellation)

The notch of a single-delay canceller is not sufficiently deep, so its ability to suppress clutter is limited. A double-delay, or three-pulse, canceller provides better suppression.

Mathematical expression:
$$y(t) = x(t) - 2x(t - T) + x(t - 2T)$$

Transfer function:
$$H(f) = 1 - 2e^{-j2\pi f T} + e^{-j4\pi f T}$$

Magnitude-frequency response:
$$|H(f)| = |4\sin^2(\pi f T)|$$

The double-delay canceller has a deeper, wider notch and therefore suppresses clutter more effectively:

Comparison: Near $f=0$, the double-delay response has a “flatter” notch than the single-delay response, which means stronger suppression of stationary clutter.

6.3.6 FIR-Filter Perspective

An MTI canceller can in fact be regarded as a finite impulse response (FIR) filter.

  • A single-delay canceller has two taps with coefficients $[1, -1]$.
  • A double-delay canceller has three taps with coefficients $[1, -2, 1]$.

More generally, the coefficients of an FIR filter of any order can be designed to achieve a more complex frequency response. The design principle is to place a notch at the clutter frequency and provide gain at target frequencies.

6.4 Moving Target Detection (MTD)

6.4.1 From MTI to MTD

Although MTI can suppress clutter, it has a fundamental limitation: it can distinguish only “moving” from “stationary”; it cannot tell us the target’s exact velocity.

MTD (Moving Target Detector) goes a step beyond MTI. It not only suppresses clutter but also measures target velocity.

Core idea: MTI + FFT filter bank = MTD.

6.4.2 Implementing a Doppler Filter Bank with a DFT

Section 6.2.5 showed that, after I/Q demodulation and pulse sampling, the echoes in each range cell form a complex sinusoid along the slow-time axis: $x(n) = Ae^{j2\pi f_d nT_r}$.

Apply a discrete Fourier transform (DFT) to this sequence:

$$
X(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n) e^{-j2\pi kn/N}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1
$$

where:

  • $N$: number of pulses in one processing window;
  • $k$: Doppler-channel index;
  • $X(k)$: output of the $k$th Doppler channel.

Each value of $k$ corresponds to a Doppler filter.

The centre frequency of each filter is $f_k = k \cdot PRF/N$, and each filter covers a particular velocity interval.

Numerical example: Suppose $PRF = 1000\text{ Hz}$ and $N = 8$:

  • Filter 0: centre frequency 0 Hz, covering stationary clutter;
  • Filter 1: centre frequency 125 Hz;
  • Filter 2: centre frequency 250 Hz;
  • …
  • Filter 7: centre frequency 875 Hz.

The frequency width, or 3-dB bandwidth, of each filter is approximately $PRF/N = 125\text{ Hz}$.

The eight filters therefore cover the entire frequency range from 0 to $PRF$ uniformly. In fact, because of DFT symmetry, they cover $-PRF/2$ to $PRF/2$.

6.4.3 Clutter-Suppression Ability of MTD

MTD suppresses clutter better than MTI for two reasons:

  1. Narrower notch: MTD suppresses only the Doppler channel containing clutter, whereas MTI suppresses the entire low-frequency region.
  2. Velocity measurement: MTD determines target velocity, which aids subsequent tracking and target recognition.

Text diagram of an MTD filter bank:

  • Filter 0 covers the clutter region near 0 Hz; the output of this channel is usually discarded.
  • Filters 1–7 cover targets travelling at different velocities.

6.4.4 Windowing

Using the DFT directly presents a problem: every filter has relatively high sidelobes, with the first sidelobe at approximately -13.2 dB, and clutter can leak through them into other channels.

The solution is windowing. Before the DFT, the data are multiplied by a window function such as a Hanning or Blackman window to reduce the sidelobes.

Window First sidelobe Mainlobe width
Rectangular window (no windowing) -13.2 dB 1× (reference)
Hanning window -31 dB 2×
Blackman window -57 dB 3×

Cost: Windowing broadens the mainlobe and reduces Doppler resolution. This is a genuine trade-off: lowering the sidelobes costs resolution.

6.5 Resolving Velocity Ambiguity

6.5.1 Origin of the Problem

Consider the blind-speed formula again:

$$v_{blind} = \frac{\lambda \cdot PRF}{2}$$

If $PRF = 1000\text{ Hz}$ and $\lambda = 0.03\text{ m}$ (X band):
$$v_{blind} = \frac{0.03 \times 1000}{2} = 15\text{ m/s}$$

A target travelling at 15 m/s is therefore at a blind speed, and targets within the $\pm 15\text{ m/s}$ interval can become mixed with stationary clutter.

6.5.2 Multiple-PRF Technique

The basic idea for resolving ambiguity is to measure the same target with two different PRFs and then use the Chinese remainder theorem to remove the ambiguity.

The procedure is as follows:

  1. Transmit pulses at $PRF_1$ and measure an ambiguous Doppler frequency $f_{d1}$ in $[0, PRF_1)$.
  2. Transmit pulses at $PRF_2$ and measure another ambiguous Doppler frequency $f_{d2}$ in $[0, PRF_2)$.
  3. The target’s true Doppler frequency must satisfy both conditions:
    $$f_d = n_1 \cdot PRF_1 + f_{d1}$$
    $$f_d = n_2 \cdot PRF_2 + f_{d2}$$
  4. Find integers $n_1, n_2$ that satisfy both equations, and solve for the true $f_d$.

6.5.3 Numerical Example from the Course Exercises

Suppose the target gives the following measurements under two PRFs:

  • $PRF_1 = 1200\text{ Hz}$, with measured $f_{d1} = 100\text{ Hz}$;
  • $PRF_2 = 1000\text{ Hz}$, with measured $f_{d2} = 500\text{ Hz}$.

Solution:

Step 1: write the equations
$$f_d = 1200n_1 + 100$$
$$f_d = 1000n_2 + 500$$

Step 2: combine them
$$1200n_1 + 100 = 1000n_2 + 500$$
$$1200n_1 = 1000n_2 + 400$$
$$6n_1 = 5n_2 + 2$$

Step 3: find an integer solution

  • Try $n_1 = 2$: $6 \times 2 = 12 = 5n_2 + 2$, giving $n_2 = 2$.

Verification:
$$f_d = 1200 \times 2 + 100 = 2500\text{ Hz}$$
$$f_d = 1000 \times 2 + 500 = 2500\text{ Hz}$$

Thus, the actual Doppler frequency is $f_d = 2500\text{ Hz}$.

Other possible higher-order solutions are:

  • when $n_1 = 7$, $f_d = 1200 \times 7 + 100 = 8500\text{ Hz}$;
  • when $n_1 = 12$, $f_d = 1200 \times 12 + 100 = 14500\text{ Hz}$.

The lowest-frequency solution, $f_d = 2500\text{ Hz}$, is normally selected.

Converting to velocity: If $\lambda = 0.03\text{ m}$:
$$v = \frac{f_d \cdot \lambda}{2} = \frac{2500 \times 0.03}{2} = 37.5\text{ m/s}$$

6.5.4 Engineering Considerations in PRF Selection

Selecting a PRF requires several factors to be balanced:

  1. Avoiding blind speeds: If the PRF is too low, the blind speed is low and many target velocities fall into blind regions.
  2. Avoiding range ambiguity: If the PRF is too high, echoes from distant targets may arrive only after the next pulse has been transmitted, producing range ambiguity.
  3. Ambiguity-resolution ability: The ratio of two PRFs should be chosen as a pair of relatively prime integers, such as 4:3 or 5:4.

PRF selection is therefore a crucial engineering problem in radar design.

Chapter 7 Detection Theory and Methods

7.1 Basic Detection Concepts

7.1.1 A Story First

You are a goalkeeper standing on the goal line for a penalty kick. Your task is to judge the direction of the shot and make the save.

Four outcomes are possible:

What actually happens Your decision Result
Ball goes left Dive left Saved! Correct detection
Ball goes right Dive right Saved! Correct detection
Ball goes left Dive right Goal conceded! Miss
No shot, only a feint Dive left An unnecessary dive but no goal conceded: false alarm

Detecting a radar target is fundamentally the same as saving a penalty: both require a binary decision under uncertainty.

7.1.2 Two Hypotheses

Radar detection always involves only two hypotheses:

  • $H_0$ (null hypothesis): no target; the received signal contains noise alone.
    • This is like the goalkeeper facing a feint with no actual shot.
  • $H_1$ (alternative hypothesis): a target is present; the received signal contains a target echo plus noise.
    • This is like the ball actually being kicked.

7.1.3 Three Probabilities

False-alarm probability $P_{FA}$:

The true condition is $H_0$ (no target), but the detector declares a target.

$$P_{FA} = \int_{T}^{\infty} p(y|H_0) dy$$

where:

  • $T$: detection threshold;
  • $p(y|H_0)$: probability density function (PDF) of the received signal when no target is present;
  • $P_{FA}$: probability that noise exceeds the threshold.

Physical meaning: If a radar reports one false target, on average, in every 100 scans, then $P_{FA} = 0.01$.

Detection probability $P_D$:

The true condition is $H_1$ (target present), and the detector correctly declares a target.

$$P_D = \int_{T}^{\infty} p(y|H_1) dy$$

Here $p(y|H_1)$ is the PDF of the received signal when a target is present.

Miss probability $P_M$:

The true condition is $H_1$, but the detector declares no target. $$P_M = 1 - P_D$$

Relationship among the three:

$$P_{FA} + P_D + P_M \neq 1 \quad \text{（不要混为一谈！）}$$

The correct relationship is $P_D + P_M = 1$ under the same hypothesis $H_1$. $P_{FA}$ is a separate quantity based on $H_0$.

7.1.4 Text Diagram

Key points:

  • Move threshold $T$ to the left: $P_D$ rises, but $P_{FA}$ also rises.
  • Move $T$ to the right: $P_{FA}$ falls, but $P_D$ also falls; this is conservative but may miss targets.
  • The detector must find a balance between the two.

7.1.5 Decision Criteria

(1) Bayes Criterion

The central idea of the Bayes criterion is to assign a “cost” to each kind of error and choose the decision rule that minimises average cost.

$$
\frac{p(y|H_1)}{p(y|H_0)} \mathrel{\mathop{\gtrless}\limits_{H_0}^{H_1}} \frac{P(H_0)(c_{10} - c_{00})}{P(H_1)(c_{01} - c_{11})}
$$

where:

  • $P(H_0), P(H_1)$: prior probabilities of no target and a target;
  • $c_{10}$: cost of a false alarm;
  • $c_{01}$: cost of a miss;
  • $c_{00}, c_{11}$: costs of correct decisions, usually set to zero.

Everyday example: A fire alarm must balance two errors:

  • false alarm: a fire engine makes an unnecessary trip, with cost $c_{10}$ = 100 yuan;
  • miss: a house burns down, with cost $c_{01}$ = one million yuan.

The cost of a miss is clearly far greater, so the threshold should be lowered appropriately.

(2) Neyman–Pearson Criterion—the Most Common Choice

In radar, we generally do not know $P(H_0)$ and $P(H_1)$, and costs are difficult to quantify. The NP criterion instead says:

For a given acceptable false-alarm probability $P_{FA}$, maximise detection probability $P_D$.

The procedure is:

  1. Set the maximum permitted $P_{FA}$, such as $10^{-6}$.
  2. Calculate threshold $T$ from $P_{FA}$.
  3. Detect with that threshold and obtain the corresponding $P_D$.

Likelihood-ratio test:

$$
\Lambda(y) = \frac{p(y|H_1)}{p(y|H_0)} \mathrel{\mathop{\gtrless}\limits_{H_0}^{H_1}} \eta
$$

  • $\Lambda(y)$ is the likelihood ratio, measuring whether observation $y$ is more likely under $H_1$ or $H_0$.
  • $\eta$ is the threshold, determined by $P_{FA}$.
  • If $\Lambda(y) > \eta$, decide $H_1$ (target present).

7.2 Statistical Models of Noise and Clutter

7.2.1 Why Statistical Models Matter

Noise and clutter in a radar receiver are random, not deterministic. We cannot predict the noise value at a particular instant, but a statistical model can describe its pattern of behaviour.

Purpose of a statistical model: Only when we understand the “temperament” of noise can we design a detector for it.

7.2.2 Common Distribution Models

(1) Gaussian Distribution

Application: Receiver thermal noise, the most common noise source.

The Gaussian probability density function is:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

where $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma^2$ is the variance.

Noise after I/Q demodulation: Noise in the I and Q channels independently follows $N(0, \sigma^2)$, a zero-mean Gaussian distribution.

(2) Rayleigh Distribution

Application: Amplitude distribution of noise after square-law detection.

$$p(x) = \frac{2x}{\sigma^2} e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}, \quad x \geq 0$$

Here $\sigma$ is a scale parameter.

Why does a Rayleigh distribution arise? If I and Q are independent, identically distributed Gaussian noise, the envelope $\sqrt{I^2 + Q^2}$ follows a Rayleigh distribution. It resembles a drunkard’s walk: movement in the x and y directions is independently random, and the final distance from the origin follows a Rayleigh distribution.

Mean and variance:

  • Mean: $E[X] = \sigma\sqrt{\pi/2}$
  • Variance: $Var[X] = (2 - \pi/2)\sigma^2$

Relationship between threshold and false-alarm probability (very important):

For Rayleigh noise, false-alarm probability has a simple relationship with threshold $T$:

$$P_{FA} = \int_T^\infty p(x)dx = e^{-T^2/\sigma^2}$$

Conversely, for a given $P_{FA}$:

$$T = \sigma\sqrt{-\ln P_{FA}}$$

Example: If $P_{FA} = 10^{-6}$ and $\sigma = 1$:
$$T = 1 \times \sqrt{-\ln(10^{-6})} = \sqrt{13.82} \approx 3.72$$

(3) Weibull Distribution

Application: Modelling ground and sea clutter; it is more flexible than the Rayleigh distribution.

$$p(x) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\left(\frac{x}{\beta}\right)^{\alpha-1} e^{-(x/\beta)^\alpha}, \quad x \geq 0$$

Here $\alpha > 0$ is a shape parameter and $\beta > 0$ is a scale parameter.

  • At $\alpha = 2$, the Weibull distribution reduces to the Rayleigh distribution.
  • $\alpha < 2$: heavier tail and stronger clutter, suitable for high-sea-state clutter.
  • $\alpha > 2$: lighter tail, suitable for low sea states or some ground clutter.

(4) Log-Normal Distribution

Application: Ground clutter for high-resolution radar, especially in urban environments.

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma x} \exp\left[-\frac{(\ln x - \ln x_m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right], \quad x > 0$$

It has a very long tail, meaning that extremely strong clutter spikes occasionally occur. A larger $\sigma$ produces a longer tail.

(5) K Distribution

Application: Sea-clutter modelling; it is currently recognised as one of the most accurate sea-clutter models.

$$p(A) = \frac{2}{a\Gamma(v)}\left(\frac{A}{2a}\right)^v K_{v-1}\left(\frac{A}{a}\right)$$

Here $K_{v-1}(\cdot)$ is a modified Bessel function, and $v$ is a shape parameter; a smaller $v$ gives a heavier tail.

(6) Rice Distribution

Application: Clutter environments containing a strong scatterer.

$$p(x) = \frac{2x}{\sigma^2} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2 + A^2}{\sigma^2}\right) I_0\left(\frac{2Ax}{\sigma^2}\right)$$

Here $I_0$ is the zeroth-order modified Bessel function and $A$ is the amplitude of the dominant scatterer.

High-sea-state sea clutter Weibull/K Heavy tail and spikes

7.3 Detector Design

7.3.1 Square-Law Detector

The most common detector in radar is the square-law detector. Its output is the sum of squares of the I and Q signals:

$$z = \sum_{n=1}^{N} (I_n^2 + Q_n^2) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} |x(n)|^2$$

where $N$ is the number of coherently integrated pulses.

Distribution under $H_0$ (noise only):

$z’ = z/\sigma^2$ follows a chi-squared distribution with $2N$ degrees of freedom:

$$p(z’|H_0) = \frac{(z’)^{N-1} e^{-z’}}{(N-1)!}$$

Distribution under $H_1$ (signal + noise):

$$p(z’|H_1) = \left(\frac{z’}{N}\right)^{\frac{N-1}{2}} e^{-z’-N} I_{N-1}(2\sqrt{N z’})$$

Here $I_{N-1}$ is a modified Bessel function of order $N-1$, and $N \cdot SNR$ is the signal-to-noise ratio after integration.

7.3.2 Albersheim’s Formula

In engineering practice, a complicated integral is not always necessary. An empirical formula can estimate the required SNR.

Albersheim’s formula gives the SNR required for specified $P_D$, $P_{FA}$, and $N$:

$$\text{SNR}{\text{dB}} = -5\log{10}N + \left[6.2 + \frac{4.54}{\sqrt{N+0.44}}\right] \log_{10}(A + 0.12AB + 1.7B)$$

where:
$$A = \ln\left(\frac{0.62}{P_{FA}}\right)$$
$$B = \ln\left(\frac{P_D}{1-P_D}\right)$$

This is a quick and practical engineering tool: it is fast, sufficiently accurate, and avoids complicated integration.

7.3.3 Swerling Target Models

Real target echoes are not constant. As the target moves, changes in attitude cause its radar cross-section (RCS) to fluctuate. Swerling models describe these fluctuations:

Model Fluctuation Inter-pulse correlation Application
Swerling 0 None Fully correlated Ideal case, rarely encountered
Swerling 1 Slow Independent between scans, correlated between pulses Jet aircraft (large targets)
Swerling 2 Fast Independent between pulses Propeller aircraft
Swerling 3 Slow Independent between scans, correlated between pulses Missiles and helicopters
Swerling 4 Fast Independent between pulses Small drones

Difference between Swerling 1/3 and Swerling 2/4:

  • Slow fluctuations (Swerling 1/3): target RCS is constant within a coherent processing interval (CPI) and changes between CPIs.
  • Fast fluctuations (Swerling 2/4): RCS changes from pulse to pulse.

Effect on detection: A rapidly fluctuating target is equivalent to a larger number of independent samples during integration. Swerling 2/4 therefore gives higher integration efficiency when calculating $P_D$.

7.4 M/N Detection

7.4.1 Basic Idea

Everyday example: You watch eight matches and bet on the same team each time. If it wins at least six, you post “The great team is back” to your social feed. That is 6/8 detection: six successes in eight attempts produce a positive decision.

Formal definition: In M/N detection, a target is declared if at least $M$ out of $N$ independent observations exceed the threshold.

Its mathematical basis is the binomial distribution:

$$P_B = \sum_{r=M}^{N} \binom{N}{r} p^r (1-p)^{N-r}$$

where:

  • $P_B$: cumulative probability, either false alarm or detection;
  • $N$: total number of observations;
  • $M$: required number exceeding the threshold;
  • $p$: probability that a single observation exceeds the threshold, either $P_{FA}$ or $P_D$;
  • $\binom{N}{r} = \frac{N!}{r!(N-r)!}$: binomial coefficient.

7.4.2 Numerical Example

Use 2/4 detection ($N=4, M=2$), with single-look $P_D = 0.5$ and $P_{FA} = 10^{-3}$.

Detection probability:

$$P_D = \sum_{r=2}^{4} \binom{4}{r} (0.5)^r (0.5)^{4-r} = 6 \times 0.5^4 + 4 \times 0.5^4 + 1 \times 0.5^4 = 11 \times 0.0625 = 0.688$$

False-alarm probability:

$$P_{FA} = \sum_{r=2}^{4} \binom{4}{r} (10^{-3})^r (1-10^{-3})^{4-r} \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}$$

Results:

Single-look $P_D$ 2/4 $P_D$ Single-look $P_{FA}$ 2/4 $P_{FA}$
0.5 0.688 $10^{-3}$ $6 \times 10^{-6}$
0.8 0.973 $10^{-6}$ $6 \times 10^{-12}$
0.9 0.996 — —

Key conclusion: M/N detection both raises detection probability ($0.5 \to 0.688$) and dramatically lowers false-alarm probability ($10^{-3} \to 6\times 10^{-6}$).

7.4.3 Engineering Applications of M/N Detection

M/N detection is commonly used in radar for:

  1. Multiple-scan confirmation: A target must appear in at least $M$ of $N$ consecutive scans before confirmation.
  2. Multiple-pulse detection: At least $M$ of $N$ pulses must exceed the threshold.
  3. CFAR post-processing: Apply M/N filtering after CFAR detection to remove false alarms.

7.5 CFAR: Constant-False-Alarm-Rate Detection

7.5.1 Why Is CFAR Necessary?

A story to begin:

Imagine that you are a security guard monitoring a radio. You set its volume just high enough to hear faint sounds—a “threshold.”

Everything works during the day. At night, however, the wind rises and the radio fills with static as noise power increases. With the daytime threshold unchanged, every gust makes the radio crackle continuously: one false alarm after another.

The next day the wind stops, and you adjust the threshold again. Someone in the distance then speaks quietly, but because the threshold is too high, you hear nothing: a missed detection.

Core problem: Noise and clutter power change with time and environment. A fixed threshold cannot both control false alarms and preserve detection performance.

What you need is an adaptive threshold that rises when noise is strong and falls when noise is weak, keeping the false-alarm probability constant. This is CFAR (Constant False Alarm Rate) detection.

7.5.2 CA-CFAR (Cell-Averaging CFAR)

CA-CFAR is the most basic and widely used CFAR method.

Core idea: Average the “neighbours” around the cell under test to estimate current noise or clutter power, then multiply that average by a factor to obtain the threshold.

Text diagram of the window:

CA-CFAR procedure:

  1. Select $N/2$ reference cells on each side of the cell under test (CUT).
  2. Calculate the average reference-cell power:
    $$\hat{P}n = \frac{2}{N}\sum{i=1}^{N/2} (x_i + x_{N/2+i})$$
    or simply $\hat{P}n = \frac{1}{N}\sum{i=1}^{N} x_i$, where $x_i$ is reference-cell power.
  3. Calculate threshold $T = \alpha \cdot \hat{P}_n$, where $\alpha$ is the threshold factor.
  4. Compare: if CUT power is $> T$, declare a target; otherwise, declare no target.

Relationship between threshold factor $\alpha$ and $P_{FA}$:

$$P_{FA} = (1 + \alpha)^{-N}$$

Conversely, for given $P_{FA}$ and $N$:
$$\alpha = P_{FA}^{-1/N} - 1$$

Example: For $P_{FA} = 10^{-6}$ and $N = 16$:
$$\alpha = (10^{-6})^{-1/16} - 1 = 10^{6/16} - 1 = 10^{0.375} - 1 \approx 2.37 - 1 = 1.37$$

The threshold is $1.37 \times \hat{P}_n$, or 1.37 times the average noise power.

Detection-probability formula:

$$P_D = (1 + \frac{\alpha}{1+SNR})^{-N}$$

where $SNR$ is the signal-to-noise ratio.

7.5.3 Advantages and Disadvantages of CA-CFAR

Advantages:

  • Simple algorithm and low computational cost.
  • Optimal performance in a homogeneous noise environment.

Disadvantages (crucial):

  1. Clutter-edge effect: If the reference window crosses a clutter boundary—half strong clutter and half weak noise—the average rises and a weak target is buried.
  2. Multiple-target interference: Other targets in the reference window raise the average and can bury a weak target in the CUT.
  3. Reduced performance in non-homogeneous environments.

Text diagram: clutter-edge problem

These problems led to improved versions of CA-CFAR.

7.5.4 GO-CFAR and SO-CFAR

GO-CFAR (Greatest-Of CFAR)—take the larger value

Idea: Calculate the averages of the leading and trailing windows separately and use the larger as the noise estimate.

$$
P_{\text{GO}} = \max(P_{\text{lead}}, P_{\text{trail}})
$$

Application: Clutter-edge environments.

Why it works: At a clutter edge, the trailing window may contain strong clutter while the leading window contains weak noise. Taking the strong-clutter value prevents their average from being “diluted,” which would make the threshold too low and cause many false alarms.

Text example:

SO-CFAR (Smallest-Of CFAR)—take the smaller value

Idea: Calculate the averages of the leading and trailing windows separately and use the smaller as the noise estimate.

$$
P_{\text{SO}} = \min(P_{\text{lead}}, P_{\text{trail}})
$$

Application: Multiple-target environments in which other targets enter the reference window.

Why it works: If the leading window contains a target and the trailing window also contains one, CA-CFAR raises the threshold because of both. SO-CFAR selects the smaller side; there is at least a chance that it selects the side without a target, preventing excessive threshold inflation.

7.5.5 OS-CFAR (Ordered-Statistic CFAR)

OS-CFAR is one of the CFAR methods most resistant to multiple-target interference.

Idea: Sort the powers of the reference cells from smallest to largest, then use the $k$th ordered value as the noise estimate.

Procedure:

  1. Sort the powers of the $N$ reference cells: $x_{(1)} \leq x_{(2)} \leq \dots \leq x_{(N)}$.
  2. Use the $k$th value $x_{(k)}$ as the noise estimate.
  3. Set threshold $T = \alpha_{\text{OS}} \cdot x_{(k)}$.

Why it works:

Suppose $N = 16$ and $k = 12$, selecting the 12th ordered, relatively high value.

  • If three targets in the reference window produce abnormally high values, sorting places them at positions 14, 15, and 16.
  • The value at $k=12$ is likely to come from a noise-only cell.
  • OS-CFAR can therefore tolerate up to $N-k$ interfering targets.

Tolerance: If the reference window contains $M$ targets, they do not significantly affect the noise estimate as long as $M < N - k$.

Engineering rule of thumb: Usually choose $k = 3N/4$, the 75th percentile of the ordered samples.

7.5.8 Recursive CFAR

CFAR processing of a video signal, continuously cell by cell in range, can be implemented recursively:

$$y(n) = (1-K) \cdot s(n) + K \cdot y(n-1)$$

where:

  • $y(n)$: current noise-power estimate;
  • $s(n)$: signal power in the current range cell;
  • $K$: forgetting factor (0 < K < 1), usually between 0.5 and 1;
  • $y(n-1)$: previous noise-power estimate.

Physical meaning: Current estimate = (1-K) × current measurement + K × past estimate. Older samples have smaller weights and are gradually “forgotten.”

System function (Z-transform):
$$H(z) = \frac{a}{1 - (1-a)z^{-1}}$$

where $a = 1-K$ is the update factor.

This is essentially a first-order IIR low-pass filter that computes an exponentially weighted moving average of the input.

Advantages and disadvantages:

  • Advantage: extremely low computation, requiring only one multiplication and one addition.
  • Disadvantage: slow response; it needs time to follow a sudden change in clutter.

Chapter Summary

Core Points

  1. Detection is a binary problem: $H_0$ (no target) versus $H_1$ (target present), involving false-alarm probability $P_{FA}$, detection probability $P_D$, and miss probability $P_M$.

  2. Neyman–Pearson criterion: Maximise $P_D$ for a fixed $P_{FA}$. This is the most common criterion in radar detection.

  3. Statistical noise models: Rayleigh after thermal-noise detection, and Weibull, log-normal, or K distributions for clutter. Select a model for the environment.

  4. M/N detection: Declare a target when $M$ observations out of several exceed the threshold, effectively suppressing false alarms.

  5. CFAR is central to radar detection:

    • CA-CFAR: averages the reference window; optimal in homogeneous environments but poor at clutter edges and with multiple targets;
    • GO-CFAR: takes the larger window average and resists clutter edges;
    • SO-CFAR: takes the smaller window average and resists multiple-target interference;
    • OS-CFAR: selects the $k$th ordered sample; strongest against multiple targets but computationally expensive.

  6. Two-dimensional CFAR detects over the range-Doppler plane. It performs well but is computationally complex.

Exercise 1: False-Alarm Probability and Threshold

Gaussian white noise has power $\sigma^2 = 1$. After detection, its amplitude follows a Rayleigh distribution. If the detection threshold is $T = 3$, find $P_{FA}$.

Click to view the solution

For a Rayleigh distribution:
$$P_{FA} = e^{-T^2/(2\sigma^2)} = e^{-9/2} = e^{-4.5} \approx 0.0111 = 1.11%$$

About one false alarm occurs in every 100 detections. To make $P_{FA} < 10^{-6}$:
$$e^{-T^2/(2\sigma^2)} < 10^{-6}$$
$$-\frac{T^2}{2\sigma^2} < \ln(10^{-6}) = -13.82$$
$$T > \sigma \sqrt{2 \times 13.82} \approx 5.26\sigma$$

The threshold must exceed 5.26 times the noise root-mean-square value.

Exercise 2: CA-CFAR Threshold Factor

CA-CFAR uses $N = 24$ reference cells and requires $P_{FA} = 10^{-6}$. Find threshold factor $\alpha$.

Click to view the solution

For CA-CFAR:
$$P_{FA} = (1 + \alpha)^{-N}$$

Solving for $\alpha$:
$$\alpha = P_{FA}^{-1/N} - 1 = (10^{-6})^{-1/24} - 1$$

$$10^{-6} = 0.000001$$
$$P_{FA}^{-1/N} = (10^{-6})^{-1/24} = 10^{6/24} = 10^{0.25} \approx 1.778$$

$$\alpha \approx 1.778 - 1 = 0.778$$

Set detection threshold to $T = \alpha \cdot \mu = 0.778 \times \text{（参考单元平均功率）}$.

Exercise 3: CA-CFAR Normalisation Factor

For the same CA-CFAR setting as Exercise 2 ($N = 24$, $P_{FA} = 10^{-6}$), suppose an interfering target in the reference cells raises the average power by 3 dB. How does the actual $P_{FA}$ change?

Click to view the solution

The CA-CFAR threshold is $T = \alpha \cdot \mu$.

An interfering target raises $\mu$. If $\mu$ rises by 3 dB, or doubles, $\alpha$ remains unchanged and $T$ also doubles.

The true noise power $\sigma^2$ has not changed, so the actual threshold-to-noise ratio rises.

For the CUT’s actual $P_{FA}$:
$$P_{FA,\text{实际}} = e^{-T^2/(2\sigma^2)}$$

When $T$ doubles, $T^2$ becomes four times as large, and $P_{FA}$ falls exponentially. That appears beneficial, but if a target occupies the CUT, its signal must also overcome the higher threshold, so $P_D$ falls as well.

This is the “target-masking” problem of CA-CFAR in a multiple-target environment: an interfering target raises the threshold and causes a real target to be missed.

Exercise 4: M/N Detection

A radar observes the same target $N = 10$ times. It uses M/N detection with single-observation $P_{FA} = 0.01$ and $P_D = 0.9$. For $M = 3$, find the system false-alarm and detection probabilities.

Click to view the solution

The system declares a target if at least M of N observations exceed the threshold.

System false-alarm probability:
$$P_{FA,\text{sys}} = \sum_{k=M}^{N} \binom{N}{k} P_{FA}^k (1 - P_{FA})^{N-k}$$

$$P_{FA,\text{sys}} = \sum_{k=3}^{10} \binom{10}{k} (0.01)^k (0.99)^{10-k}$$

Calculation:

  • $k=3$: $\binom{10}{3}(0.01)^3(0.99)^7 = 120 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.932 \approx 1.118 \times 10^{-4}$;
  • terms for $k=4$ and above are negligible.

$$P_{FA,\text{sys}} \approx 1.12 \times 10^{-4}$$

System detection probability:
$$P_{D,\text{sys}} = \sum_{k=3}^{10} \binom{10}{k} (0.9)^k (0.1)^{10-k}$$

Using the complement, $P_{D,\text{sys}} = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{2} \binom{10}{k} (0.9)^k (0.1)^{10-k}$:

  • $k=0$: $1 \times 1 \times 10^{-10} \approx 0$;
  • $k=1$: $10 \times 0.9 \times 10^{-9} \approx 9 \times 10^{-9}$;
  • $k=2$: $45 \times 0.81 \times 10^{-8} \approx 3.65 \times 10^{-7}$.

$$P_{D,\text{sys}} \approx 1.0$$

M/N detection greatly reduces false-alarm probability from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$ while maintaining nearly 100% detection probability.

8.1 Angle Measurement

8.1.1 Why Measure Angle?

Everyday example: You are in a dark room, and someone claps. You hear the sound, but can you tell which direction the person is in? Yes! The sound reaches your two ears at slightly different times—it reaches the nearer ear first. Your brain uses this tiny time difference to determine direction.

Radar works the same way. A radar must tell us not only “how far away the target is” (range), but also “which direction the target is in” (angle). Only by combining these two pieces of information can we locate the target’s position.

Radar example: A search radar rotates its antenna horizontally. When it “sees” a target, the direction in which the antenna is pointing is the target’s direction. The problem is that the antenna beam has a finite width, so the target may be anywhere within that span. We need a more precise method.

8.1.2 Phase-Comparison Angle Measurement

This is one of the most accurate methods of measuring angle.

Basic Idea

Imagine that you have two antennas—or two elements of an antenna array—separated by a distance $d$. A target signal arrives from far away. Because the path lengths to the two antennas are different, the signal also arrives at different times, producing a phase difference.

The path from the target to Antenna 1 is longer than the path to Antenna 2 by: $\Delta R = d \cdot \sin\theta$

Phase-Difference Formula

This path difference produces the following phase difference:

$$
\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \cdot d \cdot \sin\theta
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\phi$ Phase difference between the signals received by the two antennas radians (rad)
$\lambda$ Wavelength of the radar signal metres (m)
$d$ Separation between the two antennas metres (m)
$\theta$ Angle between the target direction and the antenna normal radians (rad)

Angle-Measurement Formula

Conversely, if we measure the phase difference $\phi$, we can calculate the angle:

$$
\theta = \arcsin\left(\frac{\lambda \cdot \phi}{2\pi d}\right)
$$

Key Issue: Unambiguous Angular Range

Because $-1 \leq \sin\theta \leq 1$, $\frac{\lambda \cdot \phi}{2\pi d}$ must lie within $[-1, 1]$.

When $\phi$ exceeds $\pm\pi$, phase ambiguity occurs: you cannot tell whether the phase difference is $30^\circ$ or $390^\circ$, because $390^\circ = 30^\circ + 360^\circ$.

To avoid ambiguity, we need $d \leq \frac{\lambda}{2}$. In other words, the antenna spacing cannot exceed half a wavelength.

Intuitive explanation: Think of the hands of a clock. If you look only at the hour hand, you cannot tell whether it is 3:00 or 15:00; the two times differ by 12 hours. Phase is likewise “periodic,” so values separated by more than one cycle become confused.

Radar example: The element spacing in a phased-array radar is usually designed to be $\lambda/2$ precisely to avoid angular ambiguity.

8.1.3 Amplitude-Comparison Angle Measurement

Basic Idea

Amplitude comparison is much simpler: use two antenna beams pointed in slightly different directions, then compare the amplitudes of the signals they receive.

  • If the target is exactly in the middle, the two beams receive signals of equal strength.
  • If the target is to the left, the left beam receives the stronger signal.
  • If the target is to the right, the right beam receives the stronger signal.

Angle-Measurement Formula

Let the patterns of the two beams—that is, the antenna gains for signals arriving from different directions—be $F_1(\theta)$ and $F_2(\theta)$. The ratio of the received signal amplitudes is:

$$
\frac{A_1}{A_2} = \frac{F_1(\theta)}{F_2(\theta)}
$$

The angle is then recovered from the amplitude ratio by using a lookup table or a fitted curve.

Intuitive explanation: Imagine that you have two flashlights, one aimed slightly left and the other slightly right. If an object is illuminated more brightly by the flashlight on your left, it must be located toward the left.

Phase Comparison vs Amplitude Comparison

Method Accuracy Advantage Disadvantage
Phase comparison High Insensitive to amplitude Suffers from phase ambiguity
Amplitude comparison Lower Simple and unambiguous Strongly affected by amplitude fluctuations

8.2 The α-β Filter

8.2.1 Why Track a Target?

Everyday example: You are playing badminton, and your opponent hits the shuttlecock toward you. While your eyes follow its trajectory, you are actually doing two things:

  1. Prediction: the shuttlecock is here now, so at its current velocity it will be there one second later.
  2. Update: when it actually reaches a certain position, you correct your judgment.

This “predict-update” process is the essence of tracking.

Tracking in radar: Every $T$ seconds—one scan period—the radar measures the target’s position. However, each measurement contains error (noise), and the target’s position between two measurements is unknown. We need a method that can both smooth measurement noise and predict where the target will be at the next instant.

8.2.2 Principle of the α-β Filter

The α-β filter is the simplest tracking filter. It is suitable for tracking targets moving in a straight line at constant velocity.

Two Main Stages: Prediction + Update

Stage 1: Prediction

At time $k$—the $k$th scan—we use the previous information to predict the target’s current position:

$$
\hat{x}(k|k-1) = \hat{x}(k-1) + \hat{v}(k-1) \cdot T
$$

$$
\hat{v}(k|k-1) = \hat{v}(k-1)
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\hat{x}(k|k-1)$ Position at time $k$ predicted from the first $k-1$ data points metres (m)
$\hat{x}(k-1)$ Updated position estimate at time $k-1$ metres (m)
$\hat{v}(k-1)$ Velocity estimate at time $k-1$ metres per second (m/s)
$\hat{v}(k|k-1)$ Predicted velocity at time $k$ metres per second (m/s)
$T$ Radar scan period seconds (s)

Logic of the prediction: If you were at 10 metres one second ago and were moving at 10 metres per second, then you should now be at $10 + 10 \times 1 = 20$ metres. The velocity remains unchanged under the constant-velocity assumption.

Stage 2: Measurement

At time $k$, the radar actually measures the target position as $z(k)$. This measurement contains error.

Stage 3: Update

Use the measurement to correct the prediction:

$$
\hat{x}(k) = \hat{x}(k|k-1) + \alpha \cdot [z(k) - \hat{x}(k|k-1)]
$$

$$
\hat{v}(k) = \hat{v}(k|k-1) + \frac{\beta}{T} \cdot [z(k) - \hat{x}(k|k-1)]
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Range
$\alpha$ Position-correction coefficient $0 < \alpha < 1$
$\beta$ Velocity-correction coefficient $0 < \beta < 1$
$z(k) - \hat{x}(k|k-1)$ Innovation—the difference between the measurement and the prediction metres (m)

Intuitive explanation:

Innovation = measurement - prediction = “the discrepancy I did not expect”

  • If the innovation is +2 metres—the measurement is 2 metres farther than the prediction—the target is farther away than expected, so both the position and velocity estimates need to be increased.
  • The larger $\alpha$ is, the more we trust the measurement; the smaller $\alpha$ is, the more we trust the prediction.

8.2.3 Choosing α and β

The choice of α and β directly affects filter performance:

  1. Relationship between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (critical damping):

$$
\beta = \frac{\alpha^2}{2 - \alpha}
$$

  1. Typical values:

    • $\alpha = 0.3\text{}0.5$, with the corresponding $\beta = 0.05\text{}0.4$.
    • The larger $\alpha$ is, the faster the filter tracks—that is, the faster its response—but the worse it suppresses noise, so the output is less smooth.
    • The smaller $\alpha$ is, the slower the filter tracks, but the better it suppresses noise, so the output is smoother.

  2. Physical interpretation:

    • $\alpha = 0.5$: the measurement and the prediction each receive half the weight.
    • $\alpha = 0.3$: the prediction is trusted more, providing good noise suppression but a slower response.

Intuitive explanation: Think of following navigation instructions while driving. If navigation updates very quickly—large $\alpha$—you will adjust direction frequently, but GPS noise may make you weave from side to side. If it updates slowly—small $\alpha$—your route will be smooth, but you may react too late to a sharp turn.

8.2.4 The α-β-γ Filter

If the target has acceleration—it is not moving at constant velocity—we need to introduce a third parameter, $\gamma$, to estimate that acceleration:

$$
\hat{a}(k|k-1) = \hat{a}(k-1)
$$

$$
\hat{a}(k) = \hat{a}(k|k-1) + \frac{\gamma}{T^2} \cdot [z(k) - \hat{x}(k|k-1)]
$$

A simple way to remember it:

  • α-β filter: tracks position + velocity for a constant-velocity target.
  • α-β-γ filter: tracks position + velocity + acceleration for a constant-acceleration target.

8.3 The Kalman Filter (Core Topic!)

8.3.1 Why Do We Need the Kalman Filter?

The α-β filter has two limitations:

  1. α and β are fixed—they cannot adjust dynamically as circumstances change.
  2. It does not account for changes in measurement noise—even if a particular measurement is especially inaccurate, the filter still uses the same α.

The Kalman filter’s breakthrough: It can adjust its gain dynamically. When the measurement is more reliable, it trusts the measurement more; when the prediction is more reliable, it trusts the prediction more. It can also provide the uncertainty of the estimate in the form of a covariance.

Everyday example: You are using mobile navigation in an unfamiliar place. When the GPS signal is good and the error is small, you trust the navigation. When you enter a tunnel and the GPS signal becomes poor, you rely more on extrapolating from your own velocity. The Kalman filter automatically decides “which one should I trust?”

8.3.2 Mathematical Model of the Kalman Filter

The Kalman filter assumes that the system is linear and that the noise follows a Gaussian distribution.

State Equation (Describing How the Target Moves)

$$
\mathbf{x}(k) = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{x}(k-1) + \mathbf{w}(k-1)
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Description
$\mathbf{x}(k)$ State vector at time $k$ Contains position, velocity, and so on
$\mathbf{F}$ State-transition matrix Describes how the state evolves over time
$\mathbf{w}(k-1)$ Process noise Error caused by an imperfect model (Gaussian)

Observation Equation (Describing How the Radar Measures)

$$
\mathbf{z}(k) = \mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{x}(k) + \mathbf{v}(k)
$$

Symbol Meaning Description
$\mathbf{z}(k)$ Measurement vector at time $k$ Values actually measured by the radar
$\mathbf{H}$ Observation matrix Maps the state into measurement space
$\mathbf{v}(k)$ Measurement noise Radar measurement error (Gaussian)

Statistical Properties of the Noise

  • Process noise $\mathbf{w} \sim N(0, \mathbf{Q})$—zero mean and covariance matrix $\mathbf{Q}$.
  • Measurement noise $\mathbf{v} \sim N(0, \mathbf{R})$—zero mean and covariance matrix $\mathbf{R}$.

8.3.3 A Concrete Tracking Example

Let us work through a one-dimensional constant-velocity motion example step by step.

Problem Setup

A car travels along a straight line. We measure its position once every $T = 1$ second.

State vector: $\mathbf{x}(k) = \begin{bmatrix} x(k) \ v(k) \end{bmatrix}$ (position + velocity)

State-transition matrix: $\mathbf{F} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & T \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Why? Because:

  • $x(k) = x(k-1) + v(k-1) \cdot T$ (new position = old position + velocity × time)
  • $v(k) = v(k-1)$ (constant-velocity assumption)

Observation matrix: $\mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Why? Because we measure only position, not velocity directly. Therefore, $\mathbf{z}(k) = 1 \cdot x(k) + 0 \cdot v(k) = x(k) + \text{噪声}$.

The Five Steps of Kalman Filtering

The Kalman filter performs five operations at every iteration. We give each one a short name to make them easier to remember:

Step 1: State Prediction

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}(k|k-1) = \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{x}}(k-1)
$$

Meaning: use the result from the previous step to predict the current state.

Expanded form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}(k|k-1) \ \hat{v}(k|k-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & T \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}(k-1) \ \hat{v}(k-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{x}(k-1) + T \cdot \hat{v}(k-1) \ \hat{v}(k-1) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Step 2: Covariance Prediction

$$
\mathbf{P}(k|k-1) = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{P}(k-1) \cdot \mathbf{F}^T + \mathbf{Q}
$$

Meaning: Predict the uncertainty. $\mathbf{P}$ is the covariance matrix. Its diagonal elements represent the variances of position and velocity—the squares of their uncertainties.

Intuitive explanation: The farther ahead we predict, the greater the uncertainty becomes. We add the process noise $\mathbf{Q}$ because the model itself also contains error.

Here, $\mathbf{P}$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix:

$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} P_{xx} & P_{xv} \ P_{vx} & P_{vv} \end{bmatrix}
$$

  • $P_{xx}$: variance of the position estimate (uncertainty)
  • $P_{vv}$: variance of the velocity estimate
  • $P_{xv} = P_{vx}$: covariance between position and velocity (are they correlated?)
Step 3: Calculate the Kalman Gain

$$
\mathbf{K}(k) = \mathbf{P}(k|k-1) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T \cdot [\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(k|k-1) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R}]^{-1}
$$

Meaning: Calculate the weight that determines “whether to trust the measurement or the prediction.”

Intuitive explanation:

  • If the measurement noise $\mathbf{R}$ is large—the measurement is inaccurate—then the gain $\mathbf{K}$ is small, so the filter trusts the prediction more.
  • If the predicted covariance $\mathbf{P}$ is large—the prediction is inaccurate—then the gain $\mathbf{K}$ is large, so the filter trusts the measurement more.
Step 4: State Update

$$
\hat{\mathbf{x}}(k) = \hat{\mathbf{x}}(k|k-1) + \mathbf{K}(k) \cdot [\mathbf{z}(k) - \mathbf{H} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{x}}(k|k-1)]
$$

Meaning: Use the measurement to correct the prediction.

Compared with the α-β filter:

  • $\mathbf{K}(k)$ plays the role of $\alpha$ (the position gain).
  • But $\mathbf{K}(k)$ changes dynamically; it is not a fixed $\alpha$!
Step 5: Covariance Update

$$
\mathbf{P}(k) = [\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K}(k) \cdot \mathbf{H}] \cdot \mathbf{P}(k|k-1)
$$

Meaning: Update the uncertainty. After incorporating a measurement, the uncertainty should decrease.

8.3.4 Complete Numerical Example

This is where the essence of Kalman filtering becomes clear. Let us work through a complete numerical example by hand.

Scenario

A car starts at the origin and travels at a constant velocity of $v_0 = 10\text{ m/s}$. The radar measures its position once every $T = 1$ second, for a total of four measurements.

Measurements: $z = [12\text{ m}, 19\text{ m}, 34\text{ m}, 41\text{ m}]$

Parameter Settings

Parameter Value Meaning
$T$ 1 second Sampling interval
$\mathbf{Q}$ $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ Process noise (ideal constant velocity, no noise)
$\mathbf{R}$ $[2]$ Measurement-noise variance (standard deviation about 1.4 metres)
$\mathbf{P}(0)$ $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ Initial uncertainty

Initial State

$$
\hat{x}(0) = 0 \text{ m}, \quad \hat{v}(0) = 10 \text{ m/s}, \quad \mathbf{P}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

First Iteration (k=1)

(1) State prediction:

$$
\hat{x}(1|0) = \hat{x}(0) + T \cdot \hat{v}(0) = 0 + 1 \times 10 = 10 \text{ m}
$$

$$
\hat{v}(1|0) = \hat{v}(0) = 10 \text{ m/s}
$$

(2) Covariance prediction:

$$
\mathbf{P}(1|0) = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{P}(0) \cdot \mathbf{F}^T + \mathbf{Q}
$$

First calculate $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{P}(0)$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then multiply by $\mathbf{F}^T$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \times 1 + 1 \times 1 & 1 \times 0 + 1 \times 1 \ 0 \times 1 + 1 \times 1 & 0 \times 0 + 1 \times 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Adding $\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0}$ leaves the result unchanged: $\mathbf{P}(1|0) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

(3) Calculate the gain:

$$
\mathbf{K}(1) = \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T \cdot [\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R}]^{-1}
$$

First calculate $\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T$:

$$
\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 2
$$

Add $\mathbf{R} = 2$:

$$
\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R} = 2 + 2 = 4
$$

Calculate the gain:

$$
\mathbf{K}(1) = \mathbf{P}(1|0) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \ 0 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \ 0.25 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore, $K_x = 0.5$ (position gain) and $K_v = 0.25$ (velocity gain).

(4) State update:

Innovation = $z(1) - \hat{x}(1|0) = 12 - 10 = 2$ m

$$
\hat{x}(1) = \hat{x}(1|0) + K_x \cdot \text{新息} = 10 + 0.5 \times 2 = 11 \text{ m}
$$

$$
\hat{v}(1) = \hat{v}(1|0) + K_v \cdot \text{新息} = 10 + 0.25 \times 2 = 10.5 \text{ m/s}
$$

(5) Covariance update:

$$
\mathbf{P}(1) = [\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{K} \cdot \mathbf{H}] \cdot \mathbf{P}(1|0) = \left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \ 0.25 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \right) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
= \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \ -0.25 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \ 0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Second Iteration (k=2)

(1) State prediction:

$$
\hat{x}(2|1) = 11 + 1 \times 10.5 = 21.5 \text{ m}
$$

$$
\hat{v}(2|1) = 10.5 \text{ m/s}
$$

(2) Covariance prediction:

$$
\mathbf{P}(2|1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \ 0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

First calculate the first product:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \ 0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & 1.25 \ 0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then multiply by $\mathbf{F}^T$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1.5 & 1.25 \ 0.5 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \times 1 + 1.25 \times 1 & 1.5 \times 0 + 1.25 \times 1 \ 0.5 \times 1 + 0.75 \times 1 & 0.5 \times 0 + 0.75 \times 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.75 & 1.25 \ 1.25 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(3) Calculate the gain:

$$
\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{P}(2|1) \cdot \mathbf{H}^T + \mathbf{R} = 2.75 + 2 = 4.75
$$

$$
\mathbf{K}(2) = \begin{bmatrix} 2.75 \ 1.25 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{4.75} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.579 \ 0.263 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(4) State update:

Innovation = $19 - 21.5 = -2.5$ m

$$
\hat{x}(2) = 21.5 + 0.579 \times (-2.5) = 21.5 - 1.448 = 20.05 \text{ m}

Intuitive explanation:

  • The α-β filter is like a “fixed formula”: no matter how circumstances change, the strength of the correction remains the same.
  • The Kalman filter is like an “intelligent system”: it dynamically adjusts the correction according to the current uncertainty.

8.3.6 The Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)

Real-world motion is often nonlinear:

  • A satellite moves in a circular orbit around Earth.
  • A missile performs manoeuvres.
  • A car turns on a bend.

For nonlinear systems, the standard Kalman filter is no longer applicable, so we need the extended Kalman filter (EKF).

Core idea of the EKF: Approximate a nonlinear problem with a linear one.

For example, suppose a target follows circular motion: $\mathbf{x}(k) = \begin{bmatrix} r \cdot \cos(\omega k) \ r \cdot \sin(\omega k) \end{bmatrix}$

This is not a linear equation, but we can use a Taylor expansion and retain a first-order approximation:

$$
f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f’(x_0)(x - x_0)
$$

Expand the nonlinear function about the current estimate and discard the higher-order terms; the problem then becomes linear.

More advanced variants:

Name Principle Characteristic
EKF First-order Taylor expansion Simplest, but inaccurate under strong nonlinearity
UKF Unscented transform Uses sampling points for approximation and offers higher accuracy
CKF Cubature-point transform More stable in high-dimensional systems
Particle filter Monte Carlo sampling Works with non-Gaussian noise, but is computationally expensive

Problem 1: Filtering with an α-β Filter

An α-β filter tracks a constant-velocity target with $\alpha = 0.6$ and $\beta = 0.3$. At time $k-1$, the filtered values are $\hat{x}{k-1} = 100\ \text{m}$ and $\hat{v}{k-1} = 10\ \text{m/s}$. The time interval is $T = 1\ \text{s}$. At time $k$, the measurement is $z_k = 115\ \text{m}$. Find the filtered position and velocity.

Show the solution

The α-β filter proceeds as follows:

Prediction:
$$\hat{x}{k|k-1} = \hat{x}{k-1} + \hat{v}{k-1} \cdot T = 100 + 10 \times 1 = 110\ \text{m}$$
$$\hat{v}{k|k-1} = \hat{v}_{k-1} = 10\ \text{m/s}$$

Innovation (measurement - prediction):
$$\Delta = z_k - \hat{x}_{k|k-1} = 115 - 110 = 5\ \text{m}$$

Position update:
$$\hat{x}k = \hat{x}{k|k-1} + \alpha \cdot \Delta = 110 + 0.6 \times 5 = 113\ \text{m}$$

Velocity update:
$$\hat{v}k = \hat{v}{k|k-1} + \frac{\beta}{T} \cdot \Delta = 10 + \frac{0.3}{1} \times 5 = 11.5\ \text{m/s}$$

The filtered position is 113 m, and the filtered velocity is 11.5 m/s.

Problem 2: One Iteration of a Kalman Filter

Consider a one-dimensional constant-velocity Kalman filter with state $x = [\text{位置}, \text{速度}]^T$. The following are known:

  • $F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & T \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, where $T = 1\ \text{s}$
  • $H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0.01 & 0 \ 0 & 0.01 \end{bmatrix}$ and $R = 1$
  • $\hat{x}(k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 100 \ 10 \end{bmatrix}$ and $P(k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Measurement $z(k) = 115$

Perform one iteration of Kalman filtering.

Show the solution

Step 1: State prediction
$$\hat{x}(k|k-1) = F \cdot \hat{x}(k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 100 \ 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 110 \ 10 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 2: Covariance prediction
$$P(k|k-1) = F \cdot P(k-1) \cdot F^T + Q$$

$$P(k|k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.01 & 0 \ 0 & 0.01 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$P(k|k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.01 & 0 \ 0 & 0.01 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 & 1 \ 1 & 1.01 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3: Kalman gain
$$K(k) = P(k|k-1) H^T [H P(k|k-1) H^T + R]^{-1}$$

$$K(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} (2.01 + 1)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} \times \frac{1}{3.01}$$

$$K(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.668 \ 0.332 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 4: State update
$$\hat{x}(k) = \hat{x}(k|k-1) + K(k) [z(k) - H \hat{x}(k|k-1)]$$

$$= \begin{bmatrix} 110 \ 10 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.668 \ 0.332 \end{bmatrix} \times (115 - 110)$$

$$= \begin{bmatrix} 110 \ 10 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3.34 \ 1.66 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 113.34 \ 11.66 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 5: Covariance update
$$P(k) = [I - K(k) H] P(k|k-1)$$

$$P(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 1-0.668 & 0 \ -0.332 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 & 1 \ 1 & 1.01 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.667 & 0.333 \ 0.333 & 0.676 \end{bmatrix}$$

The filtered position is 113.34 m, and the filtered velocity is 11.66 m/s. Compared with the α-β result (113 m, 11.5 m/s), the Kalman filter places greater trust in the measurement.

Problem 3: Meaning of the Kalman Gain

In Problem 2, suppose the measurement noise increases to $R = 100$ while all other conditions remain unchanged. Find the Kalman gain and explain the trend.

Show the solution

Recalculate the gain. The predicted covariance is the same as in Problem 2: $P(k|k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 & 1 \ 1 & 1.01 \end{bmatrix}$.

$$K(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} (2.01 + 100)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.01 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} \times \frac{1}{102.01}$$

$$K(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0197 \ 0.0098 \end{bmatrix}$$

Compared with $K = [0.668, 0.332]^T$ when $R = 1$, the gain is dramatically smaller when $R = 100$.

Problem 4: Angle Measurement

In amplitude-comparison angle measurement, the patterns of the two beams are $F_1(\theta) = \cos^2(\theta - 15^\circ)$ and $F_2(\theta) = \cos^2(\theta + 15^\circ)$. The measured two-channel amplitude ratio is $A_1/A_2 = 1.5$. Find the target angle $\theta$.

Show the solution

The amplitude ratio is:
$$\frac{A_1}{A_2} = \frac{\cos^2(\theta - 15^\circ)}{\cos^2(\theta + 15^\circ)} = 1.5$$

$$\frac{\cos(\theta - 15^\circ)}{\cos(\theta + 15^\circ)} = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.225$$

Expand using trigonometric identities:
$$\frac{\cos\theta \cos 15^\circ + \sin\theta \sin 15^\circ}{\cos\theta \cos 15^\circ - \sin\theta \sin 15^\circ} = 1.225$$

Substitute $\cos 15^\circ \approx 0.966$ and $\sin 15^\circ \approx 0.259$:

$$\frac{0.966\cos\theta + 0.259\sin\theta}{0.966\cos\theta - 0.259\sin\theta} = 1.225$$

$$0.966\cos\theta + 0.259\sin\theta = 1.183\cos\theta - 0.317\sin\theta$$

$$0.576\sin\theta = 0.217\cos\theta$$

$$\tan\theta = \frac{0.217}{0.576} \approx 0.377$$

$$\theta \approx \tan^{-1}(0.377) \approx 20.7^\circ$$

The target angle is approximately 20.7°.

9.1 Digital Beamforming (DBF)

9.1.1 From Ears to Antenna Arrays

Everyday example: At a noisy party, somebody calls your name. Your brain does something remarkable: it “focuses” on the direction of that voice and ignores noise from other directions. This is natural spatial filtering.

With two ears, you can roughly judge direction. If you had ten ears distributed around the room, you could locate a sound with great precision and even “hear clearly” a whisper from one direction.

An antenna array is the radar’s collection of “ears.” Beamforming is the technique that lets the radar “listen” in a particular direction.

Radar example: The PAVE PAWS early-warning radar has 2,677 antenna elements. It can monitor multiple directions simultaneously and track hundreds of targets. That is the power of beamforming.

9.1.2 Uniform Linear Array (ULA)

A uniform linear array is the simplest antenna-array geometry:

  • $N$: number of elements;
  • $d$: spacing between adjacent elements;
  • the signal arrives from direction $\theta$, relative to broadside.

What Is a Radiation Pattern?

A radiation pattern is the response of an antenna array to signals from different directions. It is like a sensitivity map showing the directions in which the antenna is most sensitive.

Intuition: A torch beam is brightest straight ahead, where gain is greatest, and dimmer at the sides, where gain is lower. The antenna pattern is the shape of the radar’s “light beam.”

9.1.3 Array Factor

The array factor is the key expression describing an antenna array’s pattern.

Basic Derivation

Because a signal reaches each element at a different time, the received signals have different phases.

Relative to element 1, the phase difference at element $n$ is:

$$
\phi_n = (n-1) \cdot \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \cdot \sin\theta
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$d$ Element spacing m
$\lambda$ Signal wavelength m
$\theta$ Signal arrival angle rad
$n$ Element index Dimensionless
$\phi_n$ Phase difference at element $n$ rad

Array-Factor Formula

Adding the signals from all elements gives:

$$
AF(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} a_n \cdot e^{j \cdot n \cdot \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta}
$$

where $a_n$ is the weight applied to element $n$.

Array Factor with Uniform Amplitude

If every $a_n = 1$, the expression becomes:

$$
AF(\theta) = \frac{\sin\left(N \cdot \frac{\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta\right)}
$$

The normalised pattern is:

$$
F(\theta) = \left|\frac{AF(\theta)}{N}\right| = \left|\frac{\sin\left(N \cdot \frac{\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta\right)}{N \cdot \sin\left(\frac{\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta\right)}\right|
$$

Intuition: Although the formula looks complicated, it is simply multi-wave interference. The N sources reinforce one another in some directions, producing the mainlobe, and cancel in others, producing nulls.

9.1.4 Steering the Beam

To point the beam towards $\theta_0$, apply phase compensation to each element:

$$
w_n = e^{-j \cdot n \cdot \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \sin\theta_0}
$$

The array factor becomes:

$$
AF(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{j \cdot n \cdot \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)} = \frac{\sin\left[N \cdot \frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}{\sin\left[\frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}
$$

Key point: When $\theta = \theta_0$, all exponential terms equal 1 and all signals add in phase, giving the maximum output. This is the principle of beam steering.

Intuition: To see a star on the left, you turn a telescope to the left. Likewise, to make a radar “look” towards $\theta_0$, adjust the phase of every element so that signals add most strongly in that direction.

Radar example: A phased-array radar scans its beam by changing the phase of each element. No antenna rotation is required, and electronic scanning is thousands of times faster than mechanical scanning.

9.1.5 Digital Beamforming (DBF)

What Is DBF?

Conventional analogue beamforming uses hardware phase shifters. Digital beamforming (DBF) operates in the digital domain: after every element signal has been digitised by an ADC, software in a DSP performs the weighted sum.

Each channel requires its own ADC:

Spatial Snapshot

At an instant $t_0$, the signals received by all elements form a vector:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \hat{A} \cdot [1, e^{j\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}, e^{j2\cdot\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}, \ldots, e^{j(N-1)\cdot\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}]^T
$$

$$
\mathbf{y} = \hat{A} \cdot \mathbf{a}_s(\theta)
$$

Here $\mathbf{a}_s(\theta)$ is the spatial steering vector, describing the phase pattern across the array for a signal arriving from $\theta$.

Basic Beamforming Operation

Beamforming is a weighted sum:

$$
y = \mathbf{w}^H \cdot \mathbf{y}
$$

Here $\mathbf{w}$ is the weight vector and $\mathbf{w}^H$ is its conjugate transpose.

Intuition: Multiply every element signal by a complex weight that adjusts amplitude and phase, then add the results. With suitable weights, signals from one direction add in phase and become large, while signals from other directions cancel and become small.

Conventional Beamforming

Choose the steering vector as the weight vector:

$$
\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0)
$$

The resulting pattern is:

$$
F(\theta) = \left|\frac{\sin\left[N \cdot \frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}{N \cdot \sin\left[\frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}\right|
$$

This is the array factor above, with maximum response in direction $\theta_0$.

Adaptive Beamforming

Problem: Conventional beamforming cannot suppress an interfering signal from another direction.

Solution: Adaptively adjust the weights to place a null, or minimum gain, in the interference direction.

The classic adaptive-beamforming algorithm is MVDR (minimum-variance distortionless response):

$$
\min_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbf{w}^H \mathbf{R} \mathbf{w}
$$

$$
\text{subject to } \mathbf{w}^H \mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0) = 1
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning
$\mathbf{R}$ Covariance matrix of the received signal
$\mathbf{w}$ Weight vector
$\mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0)$ Steering vector in the target direction

Meaning of the constraint: Pass the target-direction signal without distortion ($\mathbf{w}^H \mathbf{a}_s = 1$) while minimising total output power, including interference and noise.

The MVDR solution is:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{MVDR}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0)}{\mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0)^H \cdot \mathbf{R}^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{a}_s(\theta_0)}
$$

Intuition: MVDR is a “directional stethoscope.” It finds the optimal weight combination that preserves a weak sound in the desired direction while pressing nulls into other directions to block noise.

Effect of Adaptive Beamforming

Comparison:

Scenario Conventional beamforming Adaptive beamforming
Target only Detects target Detects target
Target + strong interference Interference buries target Null suppresses interference
Multiple interferers Performance degrades severely Forms multiple nulls flexibly

Radar example: In electronic warfare, an adversary may transmit strong jamming. Adaptive beamforming automatically places a null in the jammer direction, allowing the radar to keep operating. This makes DBF indispensable in modern radar.

9.1.6 Advantages of DBF

  1. Simultaneous multiple beams: One antenna system can produce several beams pointing in different directions.
  2. Adaptive anti-jamming: It automatically forms nulls towards interferers.
  3. Ultra-low sidelobes: Digital weighting can produce extremely low sidelobes.
  4. Flexible beam shape: Shaped beams, such as cosecant-squared beams, are possible.
  5. Self-calibration and correction: Digital processing compensates for channel mismatch.

9.2 Space-Time Adaptive Processing (STAP)

9.2.1 Why Is STAP Necessary?

Everyday example: Imagine recording with a microphone on a windy day. Wind noise, analogous to ground clutter, is low-frequency and can be removed with a high-pass filter. But if wind strength and direction continually change, frequency filtering alone is insufficient.

The radar problem:

Conventional MTI uses a one-dimensional filter in time or Doppler and can remove only stationary clutter. In reality:

  1. Ground clutter moves—trees sway and waves move—so clutter also has a Doppler shift.
  2. Clutter is non-uniform: its strength differs by direction.
  3. Interference is spatially varying: a jammer arrives from a particular direction.

Conclusion: One-dimensional temporal or Doppler filtering is insufficient. We need joint two-dimensional spatial and temporal processing: STAP.

9.2.2 Space-Time Two-Dimensional Spectrum

What Is a Space-Time Spectrum?

Each clutter patch has two attributes:

  • angle in the spatial domain: where does it come from?
  • Doppler frequency in the temporal domain: how fast is it moving?

Plot clutter on an angle-Doppler plane:

The clutter ridge is the diagonal line along which clutter lies in the angle-Doppler plane. Different clutter angles have different radial velocities and hence different Doppler frequencies.

Relationship Between Doppler and Angle

For an airborne radar on a platform travelling at velocity $v$, the Doppler frequency of a clutter patch is:

$$
f_D = \frac{2v}{\lambda} \cdot \sin\theta
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$f_D$ Doppler frequency Hz
$v$ Aircraft velocity m/s
$\lambda$ Radar wavelength m
$\theta$ Angle between clutter direction and flight path rad

Intuition: As an aircraft flies over the ground, terrain ahead approaches and has positive Doppler; terrain behind recedes and has negative Doppler; terrain to the side has almost no radial velocity and thus near-zero Doppler. Clutter therefore appears as a “ridge” in an angle-Doppler plot.

Why Is One-Dimensional Filtering Insufficient?

  • Temporal-frequency filtering (MTI): It suppresses only clutter near zero Doppler, while moving clutter such as wind-blown trees has non-zero Doppler and passes through.
  • Spatial filtering (DBF): It suppresses interference only in particular directions, while clutter arrives from all directions.

Two-dimensional space-time filtering uses both angle and Doppler information to “excavate” the target’s position on the two-dimensional plane.

9.2.3 STAP Data Structure

Space-Time Data Cube

STAP processes a three-dimensional data block:

Its dimensions are:

  • $N$: number of elements, the spatial dimension;
  • $M$: number of pulses in one CPI, the temporal/Doppler dimension;
  • $L$: number of range gates.

Space-Time Snapshot

For one range gate, collect data from all N elements and M pulses into an $NM \times 1$ vector:

$$
\mathbf{x} = [x(0,0), x(0,1), \ldots, x(N-1,M-1)]^T
$$

This space-time snapshot contains all spatial and temporal information for that range gate.

Space-Time Steering Vector

The target’s space-time steering vector is the Kronecker product of two steering vectors:

$$
\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{s}_t(f_d) \otimes \mathbf{s}_s(\theta)
$$

  • $\mathbf{s}_s(\theta) = [1, e^{j\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}, \ldots, e^{j(N-1)\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}]^T$ — spatial steering vector;
  • $\mathbf{s}_t(f_d) = [1, e^{j2\pi f_d T}, \ldots, e^{j(M-1)2\pi f_d T}]^T$ — temporal steering vector;
  • $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product.

Intuition: The space-time steering vector describes the response across all elements and pulses of a target arriving from $\theta$ with Doppler frequency $f_d$.

9.2.4 Optimal STAP

Basic Idea

STAP is essentially a two-dimensional adaptive filter:

$$
y = \mathbf{w}^H \cdot \mathbf{x}
$$

where $\mathbf{w}$ is an $NM \times 1$ weight vector.

Calculating the Weight Vector

The optimal STAP weight is derived like MVDR:

$$
\mathbf{w}_{\text{opt}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{s}}{\mathbf{s}^H \cdot \mathbf{R}^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{s}}
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning
$\mathbf{R}$ Space-time covariance matrix ($NM \times NM$)
$\mathbf{s}$ Space-time steering vector
$\mathbf{w}_{\text{opt}}$ Optimal weight vector

Components of the Covariance Matrix

The space-time covariance matrix contains three components:

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_n + \mathbf{R}_j + \mathbf{R}_c
$$

Component Meaning Source
$\mathbf{R}_n$ Noise covariance Thermal noise, etc.
$\mathbf{R}_j$ Interference covariance Deliberate jamming
$\mathbf{R}_c$ Clutter covariance Ground, sea, or weather clutter

Performance Metric: SINR Loss

$$
\text{SINR}{\text{loss}} = \frac{\text{SINR}{\text{out}}}{\text{SNR}_{\text{in}}}
$$

  • SINR loss = 0 dB: perfect processing with no loss;
  • SINR loss = -3 dB: half the power is lost;
  • SINR loss = -10 dB: 90% is lost and detection is nearly impossible.

Central Difficulty of STAP

Problem: $\mathbf{R}$ is an $NM \times NM$ matrix. If $N = 20$ and $M = 20$, $\mathbf{R}$ has $400 \times 400 = 160,000$ elements. Accurately estimating $\mathbf{R}$ requires about $2NM \approx 800$ independent, identically distributed samples.

This is STAP’s greatest challenge: enormous sample requirements and computational cost.

9.2.7 Reduced-Dimension STAP

Because optimal STAP is too expensive, practical systems use reduced-dimension STAP.

Reduction Strategy

Not all NM dimensions are needed. We care mainly about the neighbourhood of the target’s angle-Doppler cell, so we can:

  1. Perform conventional beamforming first to reduce the spatial dimension.
  2. Perform Doppler filtering next to reduce the temporal dimension.
  3. Adapt over only a few channels to reduce the matrix dimension.

Intuition: Optimal STAP performs an exhaustive search over the entire NM-dimensional space, while reduced-dimension STAP searches only a smaller high-priority region.

Common Reduction Methods

Method Principle Characteristic
Localised STAP Adapt only over adjacent Doppler channels and beams Simple and practical
ΣΔ-STAP Use only sum and difference beams Used in tracking mode
Post-Doppler STAP Doppler-filter first, then adapt spatially Most common
Eigen-cancellation Suppress only clutter components associated with the largest eigenvalues Suitable for strong clutter

Effect of Dimension Reduction

For example, reducing $N=20, M=20$ to $N_{red}=3, M_{red}=3$ gives:

  • matrix dimension: $400 \rightarrow 9$, 45 times smaller;
  • computation: $6400万 \rightarrow 729$, about 88,000 times smaller;
  • sample demand: $800 \rightarrow 18$, 45 times smaller.

Dimension reduction inevitably loses some performance, but this is a necessary engineering trade-off in practical systems.

9.3 Relationship Between DBF and STAP

Differences

Dimension DBF STAP
Processing domain Space only Space + time
Weight-vector size $N \times 1$ $NM \times 1$
Problem solved Directional reception/transmission Joint clutter and interference suppression
Computational complexity $O(N^3)$ $O(N^3M^3)$

Connections

  • DBF is a special case of STAP: when $M=1$, using only one pulse, STAP reduces to DBF.
  • STAP generalises DBF: it adds the temporal or Doppler dimension to spatial filtering.
  • DBF often precedes STAP: form several beams with DBF first to reduce STAP’s spatial dimension.

In One Sentence

DBF provides directionality in space; STAP locates targets precisely and suppresses clutter over the two-dimensional space-time plane.

  2. Beam steering: phase weights point the beam towards a particular direction.
  3. Conventional beamforming: the weight vector equals the steering vector.
  4. Adaptive beamforming (MVDR): automatically places nulls towards interference.
  5. Core formulas:
    • steering vector: $\mathbf{a}_s(\theta) = [1, e^{j\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}, \ldots, e^{j(N-1)\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\theta}]^T$;
    • beamformer output: $y = \mathbf{w}^H \cdot \mathbf{x}$;
    • MVDR weight: $\mathbf{w} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{a}_s}{\mathbf{a}_s^H \mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{a}_s}$.

Space-Time Adaptive Processing (STAP)

  1. Why it is needed: one-dimensional filtering cannot handle spatially varying clutter and interference.
  2. Clutter ridge: clutter lies along a diagonal in the angle-Doppler plane.
  3. Optimal STAP: an $NM$-dimensional weight vector, $R^{-1}s/(s^H R^{-1}s)$.
  4. Reduced-dimension STAP: an engineering trade-off that reduces computation and sample demand.
  5. Core formulas:
    • space-time steering vector: $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{s}_t(f_d) \otimes \mathbf{s}_s(\theta)$;
    • optimal weight: $\mathbf{w}_{\text{opt}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{s}}{\mathbf{s}^H \mathbf{R}^{-1} \mathbf{s}}$.

Typical Applications

Scenario Recommended method Reason
Communications base station DBF Spatial interference only; no clutter
Ground-based radar DBF + MTI Stationary clutter; one-dimensional filtering is sufficient
Airborne early-warning radar STAP Strong clutter with space-time coupling
Spaceborne radar Reduced-dimension STAP Limited computing resources
Passive radar DBF No transmitted signal and no self-generated clutter

Points to Remember

  • DBF = spatial filtering = “where to listen”;
  • MTI/MTD = temporal filtering = “how fast to listen”;
  • STAP = joint space-time processing = “where + how fast” at once.

10.1.2 Principles of Electronic Scanning

The Basic Equation for Phase Control

Recall from Chapter 9 that a phased array’s beam direction is determined by the phase difference between adjacent elements:

$$
\phi = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \cdot \sin\theta_0
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\phi$ Phase difference between adjacent elements rad
$d$ Element spacing m
$\lambda$ Signal wavelength m
$\theta_0$ Desired beam-pointing angle rad

The phase that must be compensated at each element is:

$$
\Phi_n = n \cdot \phi = n \cdot \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \cdot \sin\theta_0
$$

Intuitive interpretation: To direct the beam towards $\theta_0$, add a phase delay of $\Phi_n$ to the $n$th element. It is rather like turning a queue towards a chosen direction by having each person delay their step in sequence: the farther back someone stands, the greater the delay.

Array Factor

The normalized pattern of an array with N elements is:

$$
F(\theta) = \frac{\sin\left[N \cdot \frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}{N \cdot \sin\left[\frac{\pi d}{\lambda} (\sin\theta - \sin\theta_0)\right]}
$$

Mainlobe width (3 dB beamwidth):

$$
\theta_{3\text{dB}} \approx \frac{0.886\lambda}{Nd \cdot \cos\theta_0}
$$

It follows that:

  • the more elements there are (the larger N is), the narrower the beam and the higher the resolution;
  • the larger the scan angle (the larger $\theta_0$ is), the wider the beam and the poorer the performance.

Pattern-Multiplication Theorem

The complete pattern of a phased array is the element pattern multiplied by the array factor:

$$
F_{\text{total}}(\theta) = F_e(\theta) \cdot F_a(\theta)
$$

Term Meaning Characteristic
$F_e(\theta)$ Pattern of one element Broad, covering a wide angular range
$F_a(\theta)$ Array factor (interference among N elements) Narrow, determining the beam shape
$F_{\text{total}}$ Final pattern A narrow beam modulated by the element pattern

Intuitive interpretation: Each element is like a small flashlight with a broad beam. When N flashlights are arranged together, coherent interference forms a very narrow, laser-like beam. The final beam, however, cannot extend beyond the area illuminated by an individual flashlight.

The Grating-Lobe Problem

When $d > \lambda/2$, grating lobes appear: strong beams form in unwanted directions as well.

Condition for grating lobes:

$$
\frac{d}{\lambda} > \frac{1}{1 + |\sin\theta_{\max}|}
$$

Here, $\theta_{\max}$ is the maximum scan angle.

Intuitive interpretation: Grating lobes are like looking through gaps in a fence. When the gaps are too large, you see several “ghost images.” In radar, grating lobes create directional ambiguity and may cause targets in different directions to be confused.

Practical design: Most phased arrays use an element spacing of $d = \lambda/2$, maximizing resolution while ensuring that no grating lobes occur.

Beam Broadening

When a beam is scanned towards angle $\theta$, its width broadens:

$$
\theta_{3\text{dB}}(\theta) = \frac{\theta_{3\text{dB}}(0)}{\cos\theta}
$$

Intuitive interpretation: The beam is narrowest, and its resolution highest, when it points straight ahead. It becomes wider, and its resolution falls, when it points to the side. For this reason, the useful scan range of a phased array is usually limited to within $\pm 60^\circ$.

10.2 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)

10.2.1 From “A Large Antenna” to “Flying a Small Antenna”

An everyday example: Suppose you want to take a clear panoramic photograph. If you take it with your phone, the people in it look tiny. If you attach the phone to a drone, fly it across the whole scene, and stitch together all the photographs it takes, however, you can obtain an ultra-high-definition panorama.

That is the principle of SAR: motion is used to synthesize a large “virtual aperture.”

The central conflict:

  • High resolution requires a large antenna, or large aperture.
  • An aircraft cannot carry an enormous antenna.

SAR’s solution: Place a small antenna on an aircraft, record signals continuously along its flight path, and then synthesize a large aperture during ground processing.

The Relationship between Antenna Aperture and Resolution

Resolution of a real-aperture radar:

$$
\rho_a = \frac{\lambda R}{D}
$$

where:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$\rho_a$ Azimuth resolution m
$\lambda$ Wavelength m
$R$ Range m
$D$ Physical antenna aperture m

Example: For $\lambda = 0.03$ m in the X band, $R = 10$ km, and $D = 1$ m:
$$
\rho_a = \frac{0.03 \times 10000}{1} = 300 \text{ m}
$$

That is a resolution of 300 metres. Two tanks separated by 200 metres would appear as a single point in the radar image and could not be distinguished at all.

Azimuth resolution of SAR:

$$
\rho_a^{\text{SAR}} = \frac{D}{2}
$$

This is a remarkable result. SAR resolution is independent of range and depends only on the physical size of the antenna.

Intuitive interpretation: In SAR, the length of the synthesized “virtual aperture” is $L_s = \frac{\lambda R}{D}$. This gives an angular resolution of $\theta \approx \frac{\lambda}{2L_s} = \frac{D}{2R}$. Multiplying it by range $R$ yields $\rho_a = D/2$.

Although the derivation can feel somewhat circuitous, the conclusion is simple: the smaller the antenna, the finer the SAR resolution, because a small antenna forms a broader beam and therefore a longer synthetic aperture.

10.2.2 How SAR Works

Data Acquisition

An aircraft flies along a straight line while the radar transmits pulses sideways at its pulse-repetition frequency (PRF):

Key concept: synthetic-aperture time

The time for which a target remains within the beam is:

$$
T_s = \frac{L_s}{v} = \frac{\lambda R}{D \cdot v}
$$

Here, $v$ is the speed of the aircraft.

Throughout this interval, the radar continuously receives echoes from the target. Each pulse records information about the target from a different position.

Doppler History

As the aircraft flies past a target, the target’s Doppler frequency changes approximately linearly with time:

As the aircraft approaches the target, $f_d$ is positive and the frequency rises.
When the aircraft passes abeam of the target, $f_d = 0$.
As the aircraft moves away from the target, $f_d$ is negative and the frequency falls.

This frequency variation, which has a linear-frequency-modulation characteristic, is the key to SAR’s high resolution. Matched filtering compresses the signal into a high-resolution azimuth image.

Range Resolution

SAR range resolution is the same as that of an ordinary radar:

$$
\rho_r = \frac{c}{2B}
$$

Here, $B$ is the bandwidth of the transmitted signal.

Improving range resolution requires a larger bandwidth. Modern SAR systems use linear-frequency-modulated (LFM) signals with bandwidths of hundreds of megahertz, enabling sub-metre range resolution.

10.2.3 SAR Imaging Algorithms

SAR imaging turns raw echo data into an image. There are two principal classical algorithms.

Range-Doppler Algorithm (R-D Algorithm)

This is the classic SAR imaging algorithm and consists of two processing stages:

  1. Range compression: matched-filter each pulse to compress it to high resolution.
  2. Azimuth compression: perform matched filtering in the range-Doppler domain.

Range-cell migration (RCM): During the synthetic-aperture interval, the slant range between the target and radar changes, causing the target echo to migrate across several range gates. The R-D algorithm corrects this effect through interpolation.

10.3.2 How Bistatic Radar Works

Basic configuration:

  • Transmitting station: transmits a signal from location A.
  • Receiving station: receives the signal at location B and operates independently of the target.
  • Baseline: the line joining the transmitting and receiving stations.

Positioning principle:

Bistatic radar locates a target by combining time of arrival (TOA) and direction of arrival (DOA).

The total propagation time from the transmitter to the target and then to the receiver determines the range sum ($R_T + R_R$):

$$
R_T + R_R = c \cdot \tau
$$

Here, $\tau$ is the delay between transmission and reception, and $c$ is the speed of light.

10.3.4 The Bistatic Radar Equation

The bistatic radar equation is:

$$
P_R = \frac{P_T G_T G_R \lambda^2 \sigma_b}{(4\pi)^3 R_T^2 R_R^2 L}
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning
$P_R$ Received power
$P_T$ Transmitted power
$G_T, G_R$ Transmit and receive antenna gains
$\lambda$ Wavelength
$\sigma_b$ Bistatic radar cross section (RCS)
$R_T, R_R$ Transmitter-to-target and receiver-to-target ranges
$L$ System loss

Difference from the monostatic equation: The denominator contains $R_T^2 \cdot R_R^2$ rather than $R^4$. When $R_T = R_R$, the two forms are the same.

10.3.5 Advantages of Bistatic and Multistatic Radar

  1. Strong resistance to jamming

    • A receiving station can remain silent, making it difficult for an adversary to detect.
    • Even if the transmitting station is jammed, a receiving station may still receive the signal.

  2. Counter-stealth capability

    • A stealth aircraft is optimized mainly for a small frontal RCS, the monostatic direction.
    • For signals arriving from the side or above, its RCS may be hundreds of times larger.

  3. Defence against anti-radiation missiles

    • A receiving station emits no signal and therefore cannot be targeted by an anti-radiation missile.
    • The transmitting station can be placed far from the battlefield.

  4. Detection of low-altitude targets

    • Multiple viewpoints can overcome masking caused by the curvature of the Earth.

  5. Use of illuminators of opportunity

    • Broadcast, television, and communications signals can serve as transmitters.
    • This gives rise to the concept of passive radar.

10.4.1 From Military to Civilian Use

Radar is not confined to military applications; it has also entered daily life. Automotive radar, in particular, is one of the core sensors used in autonomous driving.

Why use millimetre waves?

  • High frequency → short wavelength → small antenna → easy integration
  • Large bandwidth → high resolution
  • Strong penetration compared with lidar, with little effect from rain or fog

10.4.2 How Automotive Radar Works

Mainstream architecture: FMCW (frequency-modulated continuous wave)

An FMCW radar does not transmit pulses. Instead, it transmits a signal whose frequency changes continuously:

Transmitted signal: The frequency increases linearly from $f_0$ to $f_0 + W$ over a period $T$.

Ranging principle:

Mixing the transmitted and received signals produces the beat frequency $f_b$:

$$
f_b = \frac{W}{T} \cdot \frac{2R}{c}
$$

The range is therefore:

$$
R = \frac{c \cdot T \cdot f_b}{2W}
$$

Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Unit
$f_b$ Beat frequency, the frequency difference between transmitted and received signals Hz
$W$ Modulation bandwidth Hz
$T$ Modulation period s
$R$ Target range m
$c$ Speed of light m/s

Velocity-measurement principle:
Velocity is measured from the phase differences across multiple periods, just as Doppler velocity is measured in an ordinary pulse radar.

Because $d = 1.5\ \text{cm} = \lambda/2$, the condition $d \le \lambda/2$ is satisfied and no grating lobes will appear.

Exercise 2: SAR Azimuth Resolution

A spaceborne SAR has an antenna length of $D = 10\ \text{m}$, an orbital altitude of $H = 600\ \text{km}$, and an operating frequency of $f_c = 5\ \text{GHz}$. Find (1) the SAR azimuth resolution $\rho_a$ and (2) the antenna length a real-aperture radar would need to achieve the same resolution.

Click to view the solution

(1) The SAR azimuth resolution, which is independent of range, is:
$$\rho_a = \frac{D}{2} = \frac{10}{2} = 5\ \text{m}$$

(2) The azimuth resolution of a real-aperture radar is:
$$\rho_{\text{RAR}} = \frac{\lambda R}{D}$$

The wavelength is $\lambda = \frac{c}{f_c} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{5 \times 10^9} = 0.06\ \text{m}$.

The range is $R = H = 600\ \text{km} = 6 \times 10^5\ \text{m}$.

To achieve $\rho_{\text{RAR}} = 5\ \text{m}$:
$$D = \frac{\lambda R}{\rho_{\text{RAR}}} = \frac{0.06 \times 6 \times 10^5}{5} = 7200\ \text{m}$$

The antenna would need to be 7.2 km long, which is entirely impractical. This is what makes SAR so valuable: it uses a small antenna to synthesize a large virtual aperture.

Exercise 3: SAR Synthetic-Aperture Length

The SAR satellite in Exercise 2 travels at $v_s = 7500\ \text{m/s}$. What are its synthetic-aperture time and synthetic-aperture length?

Click to view the solution

The SAR synthetic-aperture length equals the length of the real beam’s footprint on the ground. The real beamwidth is:
$$\theta_{\text{beam}} \approx \frac{\lambda}{D} = \frac{0.06}{10} = 0.006\ \text{rad}$$

The ground-coverage width in azimuth is:
$$L_s = \theta_{\text{beam}} \cdot R = 0.006 \times 6 \times 10^5 = 3600\ \text{m}$$

The synthetic-aperture time is:
$$T_s = \frac{L_s}{v_s} = \frac{3600}{7500} = 0.48\ \text{s}$$

During 0.48 seconds, echo data are collected along a 3.6 km path, synthesizing an equivalent aperture of $2 \times 3.6 = 7.2\ \text{km}$.

Exercise 4: Deriving the Bistatic Radar Equation

In a bistatic radar, the range from the transmitting station to the target is $R_T$, and the range from the target to the receiving station is $R_R$. The transmitted power is $P_t$, the transmit gain is $G_t$, the receive gain is $G_r$, the target RCS is $\sigma_b$, and the system loss is $L$. Derive the equation for received power in a bistatic radar.

Click to view the solution

Step 1: Power density at the target
$$S_1 = \frac{P_t G_t}{4\pi R_T^2}$$

Step 2: Power scattered by the target
$$P_{\text{scat}} = S_1 \cdot \sigma_b = \frac{P_t G_t \sigma_b}{4\pi R_T^2}$$

Step 3: Power density at the receiving antenna
$$S_2 = \frac{P_{\text{scat}}}{4\pi R_R^2} = \frac{P_t G_t \sigma_b}{(4\pi)^2 R_T^2 R_R^2}$$

Step 4: Power received by the receiving antenna ($A_e = \frac{G_r \lambda^2}{4\pi}$)
$$P_r = S_2 A_e = \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^2 \sigma_b}{(4\pi)^3 R_T^2 R_R^2 L}$$

Compare this with the monostatic radar equation, where $R_T = R_R = R$ and $R_T^2 R_R^2 = R^4$:
$$P_r = \frac{P_t G^2 \lambda^2 \sigma}{(4\pi)^3 R^4 L}$$

The detection range of a bistatic radar is determined by the product $R_T \cdot R_R$.
