This course is a core foundation course for majors such as electronic information engineering, communications engineering, and automation.   1. Decomposition and superposition: a complex signal can be decomposed into a combination of simple elementary signals.

Chapter 1: Fundamentals of Signals and Systems

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the different ways in which signals are classified
  • Master elementary signal operations: shifting, reversal, and scaling
  • Become familiar with the mathematical forms and properties of typical signals: step, impulse, ramp, and complex exponential
  • Understand system classifications and the characteristics of each class
  • Master the properties of LTI systems
  • Learn to decompose a signal into its even and odd parts

1.1 What Are Signals and Systems?

1.1.1 Definition of a Signal

A signal is a physical quantity that varies with time or space and carries information.

In plain language: a signal is simply “something that changes.” The sound wave produced when you speak is a signal; the electromagnetic wave received by a phone is a signal; and the curve on an electrocardiogram is also a signal.

Mathematical representation: a signal can be described as a function of one or more variables.

$$
x(t) \quad \text{（连续时间信号，t为连续时间变量）}
$$

$$
x[n] \quad \text{（离散时间信号，n为整数序号）}
$$

1.1.2 Definition of a System

A system is an entity that transforms or processes a signal.

In plain language: a system is a black box defined by “what goes in and what comes out.”

$$
x(t) \longrightarrow \boxed{\text{系统}} \longrightarrow y(t)
$$

The system maps the input signal $x(t)$ to the output signal $y(t)$, written as

$$
y(t) = T{x(t)}
$$

where $T{\cdot}$ denotes the transformation performed by the system.

1.2 Classification of Signals

1.2.1 Continuous-Time Signals vs. Discrete-Time Signals

Continuous-Time Signals

Definition: a signal whose independent variable $t$ ranges over continuous time. It is written as $x(t)$.

In plain language: the signal is defined at every instant, like an uninterrupted stream of water.

Examples:

  • Speech signals, because sound waves are continuous
  • Analog television signals
  • Temperature varying continuously with time

Mathematical representation: $x(t)$, where $t \in \mathbb{R}$, the set of real numbers.

Discrete-Time Signals

Definition: a signal whose independent variable takes values only at discrete instants. It is written as $x[n]$, where $n$ is an integer.

In plain language: it is like taking a photograph at regular intervals instead of recording a continuous video.

Examples:

  • Digital audio on a CD, sampled 44,100 times per second
  • Daily stock prices
  • Digital photographs, represented as pixel arrays

Mathematical representation: $x[n]$, where $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the set of integers.

Important: discrete-time signals normally use square brackets $[\cdot]$, whereas continuous-time signals use parentheses $(\cdot)$.

Sampling: From Continuous to Discrete

Sampling is the process of converting a continuous signal into a discrete one:

$$
x[n] = x(nT_s)
$$

where $T_s$ is the sampling interval, or sampling period.

In plain language: sampling is like using a “shutter” to record a signal’s value at fixed time intervals.

1.2.2 Periodic Signals vs. Aperiodic Signals

Periodic Signals

Definition: there is a positive number $T$, or a positive integer $N$, such that for every $t$, or $n$,

$$
\text{连续：} \quad x(t + T) = x(t), \quad \forall t
$$

$$
\text{离散：} \quad x[n + N] = x[n], \quad \forall n
$$

The smallest positive $T$, or $N$, is called the fundamental period.

In plain language: a periodic signal repeats according to a fixed pattern, just as a heartbeat follows a rhythm.

Examples: a sinusoid $\sin(\omega_0 t)$, a square wave, and a sawtooth wave.

Aperiodic Signals

Definition: signals that do not satisfy the condition for periodicity.

In plain language: such a signal has no fixed repeating pattern, much like random noise.

Examples: a speech signal, which is irregular, and a single pulse.

Common Question

Question: Is $x(t) = \sin(2t) + \cos(3t)$ a periodic signal?

Answer: Yes. The period of $\sin(2t)$ is $T_1 = \pi$, and that of $\cos(3t)$ is $T_2 = 2\pi/3$. Find the least common multiple of the two periods: $T_1 = \pi = 3\pi/3$ and $T_2 = 2\pi/3$, whose least common multiple is $2\pi$. Therefore, $T = 2\pi$.

1.2.3 Energy Signals vs. Power Signals

Signal Energy

For a continuous signal $x(t)$, energy is defined as

$$
E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt
$$

For a discrete signal $x[n]$,

$$
E = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |x[n]|^2
$$

Average Signal Power

For a continuous signal $x(t)$, average power is defined as

$$
P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} |x(t)|^2 dt
$$

For a discrete signal $x[n]$,

$$
P = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{2N+1} \sum_{n=-N}^{N} |x[n]|^2
$$

Energy Signals

Definition: signals with finite energy, $E < \infty$, and zero average power.

In plain language: these are “one-off” signals, such as a single pulse. Their energy is finite, but when averaged over an infinitely long interval, their power is zero.

Examples: a rectangular pulse and $e^{-t}u(t)$, a decaying exponential signal.

Power Signals

Definition: signals with finite power, $0 < P < \infty$, and infinite energy.

In plain language: these signals continue indefinitely, as a sinusoid does. Their total energy over an infinitely long interval is infinite, but their average power is finite.

Examples: a sinusoidal signal $\sin(\omega_0 t)$, a periodic square wave, and the DC signal $x(t) = 1$.

Important Notes

  • A periodic signal is necessarily a power signal: periodic repetition makes its energy infinite while leaving its power finite.
  • A signal of finite duration is generally an energy signal.
  • A signal cannot be both an energy signal and a power signal; the two classes are mutually exclusive.

1.2.4 Deterministic Signals vs. Random Signals

Deterministic Signals

Definition: the value of the signal can be predicted and described exactly.

In plain language: you know what value the signal will take one second from now.

Example: for $x(t) = \sin(2\pi t)$, the value at any instant can be calculated exactly.

Random Signals

Definition: the value of the signal is uncertain and can be described only through statistical properties.

In plain language: you cannot predict its next value exactly; you can only estimate what it will “roughly” be.

Examples: thermal noise, speech signals, which are partly random, and interference in a communication channel.

Note: this course mainly studies deterministic signals. Random signals are covered in a course on random-signal analysis.

1.3 Elementary Signal Operations

1.3.1 Time Shift

Definition:

$$
x(t - t_0) \quad \text{将信号向右平移 } t_0
$$

$$
x(t + t_0) \quad \text{将信号向左平移 } t_0
$$

In plain language:

  • If the position of a car is described by $x(t)$, then $x(t-2)$ describes a car that starts two seconds later. It travels the same route, but the entire motion is delayed.
  • Mnemonic: “minus shifts right, plus shifts left.” Subtracting a positive number shifts a signal right; adding one shifts it left.

Graphical interpretation:

Suppose $x(t)$ has a pulse peak at $t=0$.

  • For $x(t-3)$, the peak occurs at $t=3$: it is three seconds later and has shifted right.
  • For $x(t+3)$, the peak occurs at $t=-3$: it is three seconds earlier and has shifted left.

Why does “minus shift right and plus shift left”?

The value of $x(t)$ at $t=0$ appears in $x(t-t_0)$ at $t=t_0$, because when $t = t_0$, $x(t_0 - t_0) = x(0)$. The entire waveform therefore moves right by $t_0$.

1.3.2 Time Reversal

Definition:

$$
y(t) = x(-t)
$$

In plain language: reflect the signal about the vertical axis, as if viewing it in a mirror.

Graphical interpretation:

  • If $x(t)$ rises like a mountain from 0 to 10 and then falls, $x(-t)$ reverses the process: what originally happened last now happens first.
  • Imagine playing a video backward. That is the effect of time reversal.

Analogy: play an audio file from the end to the beginning. This is time reversal.

1.3.3 Time Scaling

Definition:

$$
y(t) = x(at), \quad a > 0
$$

  • If $a > 1$, the signal is compressed, becoming narrower along the time axis.
  • If $0 < a < 1$, the signal is expanded, becoming wider along the time axis.

In plain language:

  • When $a > 1$, compression resembles playing a video at twice the normal speed: every event happens faster.
  • When $0 < a < 1$, expansion resembles playing a video at half speed: every event happens more slowly.

Concrete example:

Let $x(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le t \le 2 \ 0, & \text{其他} \end{cases}$, a rectangular pulse of width 2.

  • For $x(2t)$, the rectangle becomes $0 \le 2t \le 2 \Rightarrow 0 \le t \le 1$, so its width becomes 1: compression.
  • For $x(t/2)$, the rectangle becomes $0 \le t/2 \le 2 \Rightarrow 0 \le t \le 4$, so its width becomes 4: expansion.

Important reminder: in time scaling, if $a < 0$, the operation includes both reversal and scaling. For example, $x(-2t)$ is first reversed and then compressed.

1.3.4 Comprehensive Example

Example 1: Given the waveform of $x(t)$, sketch the waveform of $x(-2t+1)$.

Solution procedure:

For a composite transformation, follow the rule “scale or reverse first, then shift.”

Method 1: Transform step by step

  1. Reverse first: $x(t) \to x(-t)$
  2. Then scale: $x(-t) \to x(-2t)$, compressing by a factor of 1/2
  3. Then shift: $x(-2t) \to x(-2(t-1/2)) = x(-2t+1)$, shifting right by 1/2

Method 2: Factor the transformation

In the original expression $x(at+b)$, $a=-2, b=1$.
Rewrite it as $x(a(t + b/a)) = x(-2(t - 1/2))$.

  • First scale: $x(-2t)$, reversing and compressing by a factor of 1/2.
  • Then shift right by $\frac{1}{2}$.

1.4 Typical Signals

1.4.1 Unit-Step Signal $u(t)$

Definition:

$$
u(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & t > 0 \ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases}
$$

It is usually left undefined at $t=0$, or defined as $u(0) = 1/2$.

In plain language: it is like a switch. Before $t=0$, the switch is off, at 0; afterward it is on, at 1.

Graph:

Important properties:

  • $u(t)$ has a jump, or discontinuity, at $t=0$.
  • The derivative of $u(t)$ is $\delta(t)$, the unit-impulse signal introduced below.
  • $u(t) + u(-t) = 1$, except at $t=0$.
  • $u(t) - u(t-T)$ represents a rectangular pulse from 0 to T.

Discrete-Time Unit Step $u[n]$

$$
u[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n \ge 0 \ 0, & n < 0 \end{cases}
$$

Note that in the discrete case, $u[0] = 1$, unlike the continuous-time version.

1.4.2 Unit-Impulse Signal $\delta(t)$

Definition, through a limiting process:

$\delta(t)$ is a generalized function satisfying

$$
\delta(t) = 0, \quad t \neq 0
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t) dt = 1
$$

In plain language: an impulse is an “infinitely high, infinitely narrow pulse with area one.” It resembles striking an object instantaneously with a hammer: the force is applied in an instant.

Important properties:

  1. Sifting property, or sampling property:

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\delta(t-t_0) dt = x(t_0)}
$$

In plain language: the impulse acts like a “sampler” that extracts a function’s value at one point.

  1. Product with an ordinary function:

$$
x(t)\delta(t-t_0) = x(t_0)\delta(t-t_0)
$$

  1. Even symmetry: $\delta(-t) = \delta(t)$

  2. Scaling:

$$
\delta(at) = \frac{1}{|a|}\delta(t)
$$

  1. Derivative of the step function:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u(t) = \delta(t)
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{t} \delta(\tau) d\tau = u(t)
$$

Discrete-Time Unit Impulse $\delta[n]$

$$
\delta[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n = 0 \ 0, & n \neq 0 \end{cases}
$$

A discrete impulse is simply a single “1,” so it is much easier to handle than its continuous-time counterpart.

Discrete sifting property:

$$
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\delta[n-n_0] = x[n_0]
$$

1.4.3 Ramp Signal $r(t)$

Definition:

$$
r(t) = \begin{cases} t, & t \ge 0 \ 0, & t < 0 \end{cases} = t \cdot u(t)
$$

In plain language: the signal grows linearly with slope 1 from $t=0$. It is like the accumulated volume of water after a tap is opened.

Relationship with the step and impulse:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}r(t) = u(t), \quad \frac{d^2}{dt^2}r(t) = \delta(t)
$$

$$
r(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} u(\tau) d\tau = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \int_{-\infty}^{\tau} \delta(\sigma) d\sigma d\tau
$$

1.4.4 Complex-Exponential Signal $e^{st}$

Definition:

$$
x(t) = e^{st}, \quad s = \sigma + j\omega \text{（复数）}
$$

This is the most versatile of all signals because it contains many special cases:

Value of $s$ Signal form Physical meaning
$\sigma=0, \omega=0$ $1$ DC signal
$\sigma<0, \omega=0$ $e^{-\alpha t}$ Real exponential decay
$\sigma>0, \omega=0$ $e^{\alpha t}$ Real exponential growth
$\sigma=0, \omega>0$ $e^{j\omega t}$ Constant-amplitude sinusoidal oscillation
$\sigma<0, \omega>0$ $e^{-\alpha t}e^{j\omega t}$ Damped oscillation
$\sigma>0, \omega>0$ $e^{\alpha t}e^{j\omega t}$ Growing oscillation

Relationship Between Complex Exponentials and Sines and Cosines — Euler’s Formula

$$
\boxed{e^{j\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + j\sin(\omega t)}
$$

Inverse forms of Euler’s formula:

$$
\cos(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} + e^{-j\omega t}}{2}
$$

$$
\sin(\omega t) = \frac{e^{j\omega t} - e^{-j\omega t}}{2j}
$$

In plain language: complex exponentials are the “atoms” of signals. Just as matter is made of atoms, an arbitrary signal can be decomposed into a combination of complex-exponential signals. This is the central idea behind both the Fourier and Laplace transforms.

Why are complex-exponential signals so important?

A complex-exponential signal is an eigenfunction of an LTI system. When the input is a complex exponential, the output remains a complex exponential of the same frequency, although its amplitude and phase may change. This fact will be used repeatedly in later chapters.

1.5 Classification of Systems

1.5.1 Linear Systems vs. Nonlinear Systems

Linear Systems

Definition: a system that satisfies both homogeneity and additivity.

  • Homogeneity, or scaling: if $x(t) \to y(t)$, then $ax(t) \to ay(t)$.
  • Additivity, or superposition: if $x_1(t) \to y_1(t)$ and $x_2(t) \to y_2(t)$, then $x_1(t) + x_2(t) \to y_1(t) + y_2(t)$.

Together, these two properties give linearity:

$$
\boxed{ax_1(t) + bx_2(t) \to ay_1(t) + by_2(t)}
$$

In plain language: a linear system is one that “scales proportionally and processes components separately.” Double the input, and the output doubles; superpose several inputs, and the corresponding outputs superpose as well.

Test: check whether the system satisfies $T{ax_1 + bx_2} = aT{x_1} + bT{x_2}$.

Nonlinear Systems

Definition: systems that do not satisfy linearity.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = x^2(t)$, a squaring operation whose output is not proportional to its input
  • $y(t) = \sin(x(t))$, a nonlinear function
  • $y(t) = |x(t)|$, an absolute-value operation that violates homogeneity: $|-2| = 2$, whereas $|-1 \times 2| \neq -1 \times |2| = -2$

1.5.2 Time-Varying Systems vs. Time-Invariant Systems

Time-Invariant Systems

Definition: the system’s properties do not change over time. If the input is delayed by $t_0$, the output is delayed by the same $t_0$.

If $x(t) \to y(t)$, then

$$
\boxed{x(t-t_0) \to y(t-t_0)}
$$

In plain language: if you apply the same signal today and tomorrow, you obtain the same output. The system itself does not “age.”

Test: check whether any system parameter depends explicitly on time $t$ as an independent variable rather than merely as a function argument.

Time-Varying Systems

Definition: systems whose properties change with time.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = \sin(t) \cdot x(t)$: because $\sin(t)$ is a time-varying coefficient, the system’s properties vary with time.
  • By contrast, $y(t) = \sin(x(t))$ is time-invariant. The function $\sin(\cdot)$ does not itself vary with time; it merely acts on $x(t)$.

Quick test:

In the output expression, if $t$ appears anywhere other than as the argument of $x$, the system is generally time-varying.

  • $y(t) = t \cdot x(t)$ — time-varying, because $t$ appears independently
  • $y(t) = x(2t)$ — time-varying, because time is scaled
  • $y(t) = \cos(x(t))$ — time-invariant, because $t$ appears only inside $x$

1.5.3 Causal Systems vs. Noncausal Systems

Causal Systems

Definition: the output depends only on the present and past input, not on future input.

$$
\boxed{y(t_0) \text{ 只取决于 } x(t) \text{ 在 } t \le t_0 \text{ 时的值}}
$$

In plain language: a causal system cannot “foresee the future.” It can respond only to events that have already occurred.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = x(t) + x(t-1)$ is causal because it uses only the present and past values.
  • $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau$ is causal because the integration ends at the present time.

Noncausal Systems

Definition: systems whose output depends on future input.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = x(t+1)$, which requires a future value
  • $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) d\tau$, which requires values at all times

Practical meaning: every physically realizable system is causal because we cannot predict the future. Noncausal systems are normally used in retrospective signal processing; when processing already recorded data, we can “see” future samples.

1.5.4 Stable Systems vs. Unstable Systems

BIBO Stability

Definition: every bounded input produces a bounded output. BIBO stands for Bounded-Input, Bounded-Output.

If $|x(t)| \le B_x < \infty$ for all $t$, then there is a $B_y$ such that $|y(t)| \le B_y < \infty$.

In plain language: the system does not “blow up.” A finite input leads to a finite output.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = e^{-t}x(t)$ is stable: even if $x(t)$ is large, multiplication by $e^{-t}$ makes it decay.
  • $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau$ is unstable: if the bounded input is $x(t)=1$, the integral grows without bound.

1.5.5 Systems with Memory vs. Memoryless Systems

Memoryless Systems

Definition: the present output depends only on the present input.

$$
y(t_0) \text{ 只取决于 } x(t_0)
$$

Examples:

  • $y(t) = 2x(t)$, as in a resistor obeying $v = iR$
  • $y(t) = x^2(t)$

Systems with Memory

Definition: the present output depends on past or future inputs.

Examples:

  • $y(t) = x(t) + x(t-1)$, which must retain the preceding input value
  • $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau$, which must retain all past values
  • A capacitor: $i(t) = C\frac{dv(t)}{dt}$, where current depends on the rate of change of voltage

1.6 Properties of LTI Systems

1.6.1 What Is an LTI System?

LTI = Linear Time-Invariant

This is the most important class of systems in a Signals and Systems course because

  1. They are mathematically tractable: they can be analyzed with tools such as convolution, Fourier transforms, and Laplace transforms.
  2. They are common in the physical world: many practical systems can be approximated as LTI systems, including RLC circuits and mechanical vibration systems.
  3. Their analysis can be decomposed: the response to an arbitrary input can be decomposed into responses to elementary signals.

1.6.2 Approach to Studying LTI Systems

The idea is analogous to the following process:

  1. Break a complex problem into simple problems.
  2. Solve the simple problems separately.
  3. Combine their solutions.

1.7 Signal Decomposition

1.7.1 Even-Odd Decomposition

Every signal can be decomposed into an even part and an odd part.

Even signal: $x_e(t) = x_e(-t)$, symmetric about the vertical axis

Odd signal: $x_o(t) = -x_o(-t)$, symmetric about the origin

Decomposition Formulas

$$
\boxed{x(t) = x_e(t) + x_o(t)}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{x_e(t) = \frac{x(t) + x(-t)}{2}}
$$

$$
\boxed{x_o(t) = \frac{x(t) - x(-t)}{2}}
$$

Proof — Verifying the Decomposition Formulas

$$
x_e(t) + x_o(t) = \frac{x(t) + x(-t)}{2} + \frac{x(t) - x(-t)}{2} = \frac{2x(t)}{2} = x(t)
$$

Verify the symmetry of the even part:

$$
x_e(-t) = \frac{x(-t) + x(t)}{2} = x_e(t) \quad \checkmark
$$

Verify the symmetry of the odd part:

$$
x_o(-t) = \frac{x(-t) - x(t)}{2} = -\frac{x(t) - x(-t)}{2} = -x_o(t) \quad \checkmark
$$

Intuitive Interpretation

  • The even part is the component symmetric about the vertical axis, or “mirror-symmetric.”
  • The odd part is the component antisymmetric about the origin, or “mirror-reversed.”

Example

Example 2: Find the even and odd parts of $x(t) = e^{-at}u(t)$.

Solution:

Even part:

$$
x_e(t) = \frac{e^{-at}u(t) + e^{at}u(-t)}{2}
$$

Odd part:

$$
x_o(t) = \frac{e^{-at}u(t) - e^{at}u(-t)}{2}
$$

Verification: when $t>0$, $x_e(t) = e^{-at}/2$ and $x_o(t) = e^{-at}/2$; when $t<0$, $x_e(t) = e^{at}/2$ and $x_o(t) = -e^{at}/2$. Their sum is indeed $x(t)$.

1.7.2 Other Forms of Decomposition

In addition to even-odd decomposition, a signal can be decomposed in the following ways:

  1. Impulse decomposition: decompose the signal into a superposition of impulse signals.

$$
x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\delta(t-\tau) d\tau
$$

This is the foundation of the convolution integral and will be discussed in detail in Chapter 2.

  1. Sinusoidal decomposition: decompose the signal into a superposition of sinusoids at different frequencies.

This is the foundation of Fourier series and the Fourier transform, discussed in detail in Chapter 3.

  1. Complex-exponential decomposition: decompose the signal into a superposition of complex-exponential signals.

This is the foundation of the Laplace transform, discussed in detail in Chapter 4.

Example 3: Classifying a Signal

Determine whether each of the following is an energy signal or a power signal.

(1) $x(t) = \sin(2t)$

Solution: a sinusoid is periodic, and every periodic signal is a power signal.

To verify this, calculate the average power:

$$
P = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} \sin^2(2t) dt = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{1}{2T} \int_{-T}^{T} \frac{1-\cos(4t)}{2} dt = \frac{1}{2}
$$

The power is the finite value $1/2$, so this is a power signal.

(2) $x(t) = e^{-|t|}$

Solution:

Calculate the energy:

$$
E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2|t|} dt = 2\int_0^{\infty} e^{-2t} dt = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1 < \infty
$$

The energy is finite, so this is an energy signal.

Example 4: Determining System Properties

Determine the properties of the system $y(t) = x(t) + x(t-1)$.

Solution:

  1. Linear: $T{ax_1 + bx_2} = ax_1(t) + ax_1(t-1) + bx_2(t) + bx_2(t-1) = aT{x_1} + bT{x_2}$. ✓ Linear

  2. Time-invariant:

    • Shift before applying the system: the input $x(t-t_0)$ produces $x(t-t_0) + x(t-t_0-1)$.
    • Apply the system before shifting: $y(t-t_0) = x(t-t_0) + x(t-t_0-1)$.
    • The two results are equal. ✓ Time-invariant

  3. Causal: the output uses only $x(t)$, the present value, and $x(t-1)$, a past value. ✓ Causal

  4. Stable: if $|x(t)| \le B$, then $|y(t)| \le |x(t)| + |x(t-1)| \le 2B$. ✓ BIBO-stable

  5. Has memory: the output depends on a past input. ✓ Has memory

Find the even and odd parts of $x(t) = u(t)$.

Solution:

Even part:

$$

$$

Note that there is a jump at $t=0$, but for $t \neq 0$, $u(t)+u(-t) = 1$.

Odd part:

This is one half of the sign function: $x_o(t) = \frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(t)$.

1.9 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What exactly is a signal? It feels too abstract.

A: Do not overcomplicate it. A signal is simply a mathematical representation of “something that changes.” Temperature over time is a signal, expressed as a temperature–time function; sound over time is a signal, expressed as a sound-pressure–time function; and an image is a signal, expressed as a brightness–space function. In this course, a signal is just $x(t)$ or $x[n]$—nothing more.

Q2: Why do discrete signals use square brackets while continuous signals use parentheses?

A: It is a conventional notation that helps distinguish discrete from continuous signals. Parentheses in $(t)$ indicate that $t$ is a continuous real number; square brackets in $[n]$ indicate that $n$ is an integer. Using the wrong brackets on an exam may cost points, so build the correct habit.

Q3: Is the impulse function $\delta(t)$ actually a function?

A: Strictly speaking, $\delta(t)$ is not an ordinary function; it is a “generalized function,” or “distribution.” An ordinary function must have a finite value at $t=0$, whereas $\delta(0)$ is “infinite.” For practical work, it is enough to picture it as “an infinitely narrow, infinitely high pulse with area one.” You need not wrestle with the rigorous definition when solving problems; knowing how to use the sifting property is sufficient.

Q4: I find time invariance and time variance difficult to determine.

A: Remember the key question: when the input is delayed, is the output delayed by the same amount?

A simple test is to inspect the system expression. Apart from the argument of $x$, does $t$ appear independently, for example as a coefficient of $x$? If so, the system is generally time-varying.

However, note that

  • $y(t) = x(-t)$ is time-varying because time reversal changes the order of events.
  • $y(t) = x(2t)$ is time-varying because time is compressed.

Q5: How do I determine whether a system is causal?

A: Ask one question: to calculate the output at time $t_0$, do I need any input value after $t_0$, that is, from the future?

  • If $y(t_0)$ depends only on $x(t)$ for $t \le t_0$, the system is causal.
  • If $y(t_0)$ depends on $x(t)$ for any $t > t_0$, the system is noncausal.

Typical examples:

  • $y(t) = x(t-1)$: causal, because it uses only the past
  • $y(t) = x(t+1)$: noncausal, because it requires the future
  • $y(t) = x(2t)$: when $t>0$, $y(1) = x(2)$ requires a future value, so the system is noncausal

Q6: What is the “dimension” of a signal?

A: This course discusses one-dimensional signals $x(t)$ with a single independent variable $t$. An image is a two-dimensional signal $f(x,y)$ with two spatial independent variables. A video is a three-dimensional signal $f(x,y,t)$. Although the course covers only one dimension, its basic concepts extend to multiple dimensions.

Q7: Why is classifying signals useful?

A: Classification helps us choose the appropriate analytical tool:

Signal type Appropriate tool
Periodic signal Fourier series
Aperiodic energy signal Fourier transform
Aperiodic power signal May require power-spectrum analysis
Continuous signal Differential equations, convolution integrals
Discrete signal Difference equations, convolution sums

2.1 Describing a System with Differential Equations

2.1.1 Why Use Differential Equations?

In the physical world, the behavior of many systems can be described by differential equations. For example:

  • RC circuit: $RC\frac{dv_c(t)}{dt} + v_c(t) = v_s(t)$
  • Spring–mass–damper system: $m\frac{d^2x(t)}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx(t)}{dt} + kx(t) = f(t)$

In plain language: a differential equation describes the rule governing how a system’s present state changes over time. Weather forecasts, for example, use differential equations to describe how the atmosphere evolves.

2.1.2 General Form of a Differential Equation

The differential equation of an $N$th-order LTI system generally takes the form

$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \frac{d^k x(t)}{dt^k}}
$$

where

  • $y(t)$ is the output signal,
  • $x(t)$ is the input signal,
  • $a_k, b_k$ are constant coefficients because the system is time-invariant,
  • the equation is linear because the system is linear, and
  • $N$ is the order of the system, equal to the highest derivative order.

Expanded form:

$$
a_N\frac{d^N y}{dt^N} + a_{N-1}\frac{d^{N-1} y}{dt^{N-1}} + \cdots + a_1\frac{dy}{dt} + a_0 y(t) = b_M\frac{d^M x}{dt^M} + \cdots + b_1\frac{dx}{dt} + b_0 x(t)
$$

2.1.3 Initial Conditions

Solving a differential equation requires initial conditions, which describe the system’s state at $t=0$.

An $N$th-order system requires $N$ initial conditions:

$$
y(0^-), \quad y’(0^-), \quad y’’(0^-), \quad \cdots, \quad y^{(N-1)}(0^-)
$$

Here, $0^-$ means “immediately before time zero,” allowing any initially stored energy to be included.

2.2 Zero-Input and Zero-State Responses

2.2.1 Decomposing the System Response

For an LTI system, the total response can be decomposed as

$$
\boxed{y(t) = y_{zi}(t) + y_{zs}(t)}
$$

where

  • the zero-input response $y_{zi}(t)$ is caused solely by the initial state, or initially stored energy, when the input is zero; and
  • the zero-state response $y_{zs}(t)$ is caused solely by the input signal when the initial state is zero.

In plain language:

  • A zero-input response resembles water already in a cup, representing stored energy, slowly evaporating while no new water is added.
  • A zero-state response resembles pouring water, the input signal, into an initially empty cup and watching its level rise.

2.2.2 Zero-Input Response $y_{zi}(t)$

Solution procedure:

  1. Set the input $x(t) = 0$ to obtain the homogeneous differential equation:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k} = 0
$$

  1. Write the characteristic equation:

$$
a_N \lambda^N + a_{N-1} \lambda^{N-1} + \cdots + a_1 \lambda + a_0 = 0
$$

  1. Solve the characteristic equation for the characteristic roots $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \cdots, \lambda_N$.

  2. Write the homogeneous solution according to the form of the roots:

    • Single real root $\lambda$: $Ce^{\lambda t}$
    • Real root $\lambda$ repeated $r$ times: $(C_0 + C_1 t + \cdots + C_{r-1} t^{r-1})e^{\lambda t}$
    • Complex-conjugate roots $\lambda = \alpha \pm j\beta$: $e^{\alpha t}[A\cos(\beta t) + B\sin(\beta t)]$

  3. Use the initial conditions to determine the constants.

Example 1: Finding the Zero-Input Response

A system is described by $\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dt} + 2y(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} + 2x(t)$. Its initial conditions are $y(0^-)=1$ and $y’(0^-)=0$. Find the zero-input response.

Solution:

Set $x(t)=0$ to obtain the homogeneous equation

$$
\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dt} + 2y(t) = 0
$$

The characteristic equation is

$$
\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = 0
$$

Its roots are

$$
\lambda_1 = -1, \quad \lambda_2 = -2
$$

Because the roots are distinct and real, the homogeneous solution has the form

$$
y_{zi}(t) = C_1 e^{-t} + C_2 e^{-2t}, \quad t \ge 0
$$

Apply the initial conditions:

$$
y_{zi}(0) = C_1 + C_2 = 1
$$

$$
y_{zi}’(0) = -C_1 - 2C_2 = 0
$$

Solving gives $C_1 = 2$ and $C_2 = -1$. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{y_{zi}(t) = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}, \quad t \ge 0}
$$

2.2.3 Zero-State Response $y_{zs}(t)$

Solution approaches:

The zero-state response can be found in two ways:

  1. Direct method: solve the nonhomogeneous differential equation as the sum of a homogeneous and a particular solution, then use zero initial conditions to determine the constants.
  2. Convolution method: $y_{zs}(t) = x(t) * h(t)$, discussed in detail below.

Solving a Nonhomogeneous Differential Equation

For a differential equation in the general form

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \frac{d^k x(t)}{dt^k}
$$

find the zero-state response as follows:

  1. Find the homogeneous solution, whose form is the same as that of the zero-input response but whose coefficients are different.
  2. Find a particular solution by assuming a form based on the input $x(t)$.
Form of input signal Form of particular solution
Constant $A$ Constant $C$
$e^{at}$ $Ce^{at}$, provided $a$ is not a characteristic root
$t^m$ $C_0 + C_1 t + \cdots + C_m t^m$
$\sin(\omega t)$ or $\cos(\omega t)$ $A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t)$
  1. Complete solution = homogeneous solution + particular solution.
  2. Use the zero initial conditions $y(0^-)=0, y’(0^-)=0, \cdots$ to determine the coefficients of the homogeneous solution.

Example 2: Finding the Zero-State Response

For the same system as in the preceding example, let the input be $x(t) = u(t)$. Find the zero-state response.

Solution:

The homogeneous solution has the form $y_h(t) = C_1 e^{-t} + C_2 e^{-2t}$.

The input $x(t)=u(t)$ is the constant 1 for $t>0$, so assume a constant particular solution $y_p(t) = K$.

Substitute it into the differential equation:

$$
0 + 0 + 2K = 0 + 2 \cdot 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2K = 2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad K = 1
$$

Thus, $y_p(t) = 1$ and the complete solution is

$$
y_{zs}(t) = C_1 e^{-t} + C_2 e^{-2t} + 1, \quad t \ge 0
$$

The zero initial conditions are $y_{zs}(0)=0$ and $y_{zs}’(0)=0$:

$$
y_{zs}(0) = C_1 + C_2 + 1 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad C_1 + C_2 = -1
$$

$$
y_{zs}’(0) = -C_1 - 2C_2 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad C_1 + 2C_2 = 0
$$

Solving gives $C_1 = -2$ and $C_2 = 1$. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{y_{zs}(t) = -2e^{-t} + e^{-2t} + 1, \quad t \ge 0}
$$

Complete response:

$$
y(t) = y_{zi}(t) + y_{zs}(t) = (2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}) + (-2e^{-t} + e^{-2t} + 1) = 1, \quad t \ge 0
$$

This is an interesting result: the total response is the constant 1 because the system’s natural modes are “cancelled” by the input.

2.3 Impulse Response $h(t)$

2.3.1 Definition of the Impulse Response

The impulse response is a system’s response to the unit impulse $\delta(t)$ under zero initial conditions:

$$
\boxed{\delta(t) \longrightarrow \boxed{\text{系统}} \longrightarrow h(t)}
$$

In plain language:

  • The impulse response is the system’s “fingerprint”: every system has its own distinctive impulse response.
  • It is like striking a system with a hammer, the impulse input, and observing how it vibrates, the response.

2.3.2 How to Find the Impulse Response

For an $N$th-order system,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y}{dt^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \frac{d^k x}{dt^k}
$$

find $h(t)$ as follows:

  1. First find $h_0(t)$, the impulse response of the system $\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y}{dt^k} = x(t)$.
  2. Use linearity: $h(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \frac{d^k h_0(t)}{dt^k}$.

Finding $h_0(t)$:

$h_0(t)$ satisfies the homogeneous equation and certain initial conditions. For $t > 0$, $\delta(t)=0$, so $h_0(t)$ takes the form of a homogeneous solution.

The key is to use the jump caused by the impulse at $t=0$ to determine the initial conditions.

For an $N$th-order system, $h_0(t)$ has the following initial conditions at $t=0$:

$$
h_0^{(N-1)}(0^+) = \frac{1}{a_N}, \quad h_0^{(k)}(0^+) = 0 \text{ for } k < N-1
$$

Example 3: Finding an Impulse Response

For the system $\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dt} + 2y(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} + 2x(t)$, find the impulse response $h(t)$.

Solution:

Step 1: First find $h_0(t)$, the impulse response of $\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dt} + 2y(t) = x(t)$.

The characteristic roots are $\lambda_1 = -1, \lambda_2 = -2$.

The homogeneous solution has the form $h_0(t) = (C_1 e^{-t} + C_2 e^{-2t})u(t)$.

The initial conditions, noting that $a_2 = 1$, are

$$
h_0’(0^+) = \frac{1}{a_2} = 1, \quad h_0(0^+) = 0
$$

From $h_0(0^+) = C_1 + C_2 = 0$ and $h_0’(0^+) = -C_1 - 2C_2 = 1$, we obtain $C_1 = 1$ and $C_2 = -1$. Thus,

$$
h_0(t) = (e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t)
$$

Step 2: Use linearity.

The right-hand side of the original equation is $\frac{dx}{dt} + 2x(t)$, so

$$
h(t) = \frac{dh_0(t)}{dt} + 2h_0(t)
$$

Calculate

$$
\frac{dh_0}{dt} = (-e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t})u(t) + (e^{-t} - e^{-2t})\delta(t)
$$

At $t=0$, $(e^{-t} - e^{-2t}) = 0$, so the $\delta(t)$ term vanishes. Hence,

$$
\frac{dh_0}{dt} = (-e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t})u(t)
$$

Therefore,

$$
h(t) = (-e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t})u(t) + 2(e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t) = (e^{-t})u(t)
$$

Final answer:

$$
\boxed{h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)}
$$

2.3.3 Physical Meaning of the Impulse Response

The impulse response completely describes an LTI system. Once $h(t)$ is known, the system’s response to any input can be found. This is precisely the idea of convolution.

2.4 Convolution Integral

2.4.1 Definition of Convolution

The convolution of two functions $x(t)$ and $h(t)$ is defined as

$$
\boxed{y(t) = x(t) * h(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau}
$$

In plain language:

  • Convolution is an operation that “blends” two signals.
  • The output produced when $x(t)$ passes through the LTI system $h(t)$ is their convolution.

2.4.2 Physical Meaning of Convolution: Impulse Decomposition

Why can convolution find the system response? Because any signal can be decomposed into a superposition of impulses:

$$
x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)\delta(t-\tau)d\tau
$$

Using the linearity and time invariance of an LTI system,

  • the response to $\delta(t-\tau)$ is $h(t-\tau)$,
  • the response to $x(\tau)\delta(t-\tau)$ is $x(\tau)h(t-\tau)$, and
  • summing, or integrating, over all $\tau$ gives $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau$.

This is where convolution comes from.

2.4.3 Graphical Interpretation of Convolution — Essential

The calculation of convolution can be divided into four steps:

$$
\boxed{\text{翻转} \to \text{平移} \to \text{相乘} \to \text{积分}}
$$

For $y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau$:

  1. Folding: reverse $h(\tau)$ to obtain $h(-\tau)$.
  2. Shifting: shift $h(-\tau)$ by $t$ to obtain $h(t-\tau)$.
  3. Multiplication: multiply $x(\tau)$ by $h(t-\tau)$.
  4. Integration: calculate the area under the product curve to obtain $y(t)$.

Step-by-step interpretation, using visual reasoning:

Suppose $x(t)$ is a rectangular pulse and $h(t)$ is an exponentially decaying function.

Step 1: Draw $x(\tau)$ and $h(\tau)$, using $\tau$ as the variable.

Step 2: Reverse $h(\tau)$ to obtain $h(-\tau)$.

Step 3: Shift by $t$ to obtain $h(t-\tau)$. As $t$ changes, $h(t-\tau)$ moves left or right.

Step 4: For each $t$, calculate the integral of the product of $x(\tau)$ and $h(t-\tau)$.

Example 4: Graphical Calculation of Convolution

Given $x(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le t \le 1 \ 0, & \text{其他} \end{cases}$ and $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$, find $y(t) = x(t) * h(t)$.

Solution:

Method 1: Direct integration

$$
y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau = \int_0^1 1 \cdot e^{-(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)d\tau
$$

$u(t-\tau)$ requires $t-\tau > 0$, or $\tau < t$.

The upper limit of integration is therefore $\min(1, t)$.

Case 1: if $t < 0$, $u(t-\tau)=0$ and $y(t)=0$.

Case 2: if $0 \le t < 1$,

$$
y(t) = \int_0^t e^{-(t-\tau)}d\tau = e^{-t}\int_0^t e^{\tau}d\tau = e^{-t}[e^{\tau}]_0^t = e^{-t}(e^t - 1) = 1 - e^{-t}
$$

Case 3: if $t \ge 1$,

$$
y(t) = \int_0^1 e^{-(t-\tau)}d\tau = e^{-t}\int_0^1 e^{\tau}d\tau = e^{-t}(e - 1)
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{y(t) = \begin{cases} 0, & t < 0 \ 1 - e^{-t}, & 0 \le t < 1 \ (e-1)e^{-t}, & t \ge 1 \end{cases}}
$$

Method 2: Graphical method — fold, shift, multiply, and integrate

This is the best way to understand what convolution really means. You should sketch the process yourself at least once.

For different values of $t$:

When $t < 0$: $h(t-\tau)$ lies on the negative half of the $\tau$-axis and does not overlap $x(\tau)$ → the product is zero → $y(t)=0$.

When $0 \le t < 1$: the signals partially overlap on $[0, t]$.

When $t \ge 1$: the signals overlap completely on $[0, 1]$.

2.4.4 Properties of Convolution

1. Commutative Property

$$
\boxed{x(t) * h(t) = h(t) * x(t)}
$$

Meaning: the roles of the system input and impulse response can be interchanged. For calculation, you may choose the function that is easier to reverse.

2. Distributive Property

$$
\boxed{x(t) * [h_1(t) + h_2(t)] = x(t) * h_1(t) + x(t) * h_2(t)}
$$

Meaning: the total impulse response of parallel systems equals the sum of the impulse responses of the subsystems.

$h(t) = h_1(t) + h_2(t)$.

3. Associative Property

$$
\boxed{[x(t) * h_1(t)] * h_2(t) = x(t) * [h_1(t) * h_2(t)]}
$$

Meaning: the total impulse response of cascaded systems equals the convolution of the subsystem impulse responses.

is equivalent to

4. Differentiation Property of Convolution

$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}[x(t) * h(t)] = \frac{dx(t)}{dt} * h(t) = x(t) * \frac{dh(t)}{dt}}
$$

Meaning: differentiation may be performed before convolution, which can sometimes simplify the calculation.

5. Integration Property of Convolution

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{t} [x(\tau) * h(\tau)] d\tau = \left[\int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau)d\tau\right] * h(t) = x(t) * \left[\int_{-\infty}^{t} h(\tau)d\tau\right]}
$$

6. Convolution of a Signal with an Impulse

$$
\boxed{x(t) * \delta(t) = x(t)}
$$

$$
\boxed{x(t) * \delta(t-t_0) = x(t-t_0)}
$$

Meaning: the impulse is the identity element of convolution.

7. Convolution of a Signal with a Step

$$
x(t) * u(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau
$$

Example 5: Simplifying a Calculation with Convolution Properties

Given $x(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$ and $h(t) = u(t)$, find $y(t) = x(t) * h(t)$.

Solution:

Method 1: Direct integration

$$
y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\tau}u(\tau)u(t-\tau)d\tau = \int_0^t e^{-\tau}d\tau = (1 - e^{-t})u(t)
$$

Method 2: Use a convolution property

Because $h(t) = u(t)$ and $x(t) * u(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau$,

$$
y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} e^{-\tau}u(\tau)d\tau = \int_0^{t} e^{-\tau}d\tau \cdot u(t) = (1-e^{-t})u(t)
$$

2.5 Finding the System Response by Convolution

2.5.1 Basic Idea

For an LTI system,

$$
\boxed{y(t) = x(t) * h(t)}
$$

This is the system’s zero-state response to an arbitrary input $x(t)$.

2.5.2 Complete Solution Procedure

To find the response of an LTI system to an arbitrary input:

  1. Write the system’s differential equation.
  2. Find the zero-input response $y_{zi}(t)$ by solving the homogeneous equation under the initial conditions.
  3. Find the impulse response $h(t)$.
  4. Find the zero-state response $y_{zs}(t) = x(t) * h(t)$.
  5. Add them to obtain the total response $y(t) = y_{zi}(t) + y_{zs}(t)$.

Example 6: Complete System Solution

Consider the system $\frac{d^2 y}{dt^2} + 3\frac{dy}{dt} + 2y(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} + 2x(t)$ with initial conditions $y(0^-)=1, y’(0^-)=0$, and input $x(t) = e^{-3t}u(t)$.

Solution:

(1) Zero-input response, found above:

$$
y_{zi}(t) = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}, \quad t \ge 0
$$

(2) Impulse response, found above:

$$
h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)
$$

(3) Zero-state response:

$$
y_{zs}(t) = x(t) * h(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-3\tau}u(\tau) \cdot e^{-(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)d\tau
$$

$u(\tau)$ requires $\tau > 0$, while $u(t-\tau)$ requires $\tau < t$.

Thus, when $t > 0$,

$$
y_{zs}(t) = \int_0^t e^{-3\tau} e^{-(t-\tau)} d\tau = e^{-t} \int_0^t e^{-2\tau} d\tau = e^{-t} \cdot \left[-\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\tau}\right]_0^t
$$

$$
= e^{-t} \cdot \frac{1}{2}(1 - e^{-2t}) = \frac{1}{2}e^{-t}(1 - e^{-2t}) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{-t} - e^{-3t}), \quad t \ge 0
$$

When $t < 0$, $y_{zs}(t) = 0$.

Therefore, $y_{zs}(t) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{-t} - e^{-3t})u(t)$.

(4) Complete response:

$$
y(t) = y_{zi}(t) + y_{zs}(t) = (2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}) + \frac{1}{2}(e^{-t} - e^{-3t})
$$

$$
\boxed{y(t) = \frac{5}{2}e^{-t} - e^{-2t} - \frac{1}{2}e^{-3t}, \quad t \ge 0}
$$

2.6 Time-Domain Criteria for System Causality and Stability

2.6.1 Time-Domain Criterion for Causality

Theorem: an LTI system is causal if and only if its impulse response satisfies

$$
\boxed{h(t) = 0, \quad t < 0}
$$

In plain language: a causal system cannot respond before the input arrives. If an impulse is applied at $t=0$, the system should show no response for $t<0$.

Verification:

If a system is causal, its output $y(t)$ depends only on the input at $t$ and before $t$:

$$
y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau = \int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau
$$

This is possible only if $h(t-\tau) = 0$ when $t-\tau < 0$, or $\tau > t$. Equivalently, $h(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$.

Example 7: Determining Causality

Determine whether each impulse response corresponds to a causal system:
(1) $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$
(2) $h(t) = e^{-|t|}$
(3) $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t+1)$

Solution:

(1) $h(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$ because $u(t)=0$, so the system is causal.

(2) For $h(t) = e^{-|t|}$, when $t < 0$, $h(t) = e^{t} \neq 0$, so the system is noncausal.

(3) For $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t+1)$, $h(t)=0$ when $t < -1$, but $h(t) = e^{-t} \neq 0$ when $t \in (-1, 0)$. Because $h(t)$ is not identically zero for $t<0$, the system is noncausal.

2.6.2 Time-Domain Criterion for Stability

Theorem: an LTI system is BIBO-stable if and only if its impulse response is absolutely integrable:

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(t)| dt < \infty}
$$

In plain language: the total area under the magnitude of the impulse-response curve must be finite.

Outline of the Proof

Suppose the input is bounded: $|x(t)| \le B$.

Then the output satisfies

$$
|y(t)| = \left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau)h(t-\tau)d\tau\right| \le \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(\tau)||h(t-\tau)|d\tau \le B\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(\tau)|d\tau
$$

Therefore, if $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |h(\tau)|d\tau$ is finite, then $|y(t)|$ is bounded.

Example 8: Determining Stability

Determine whether each impulse response corresponds to a stable system:
(1) $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$
(2) $h(t) = e^{t}u(-t)$
(3) $h(t) = u(t)$

Solution:

(1) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |e^{-t}u(t)| dt = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-t} dt = 1 < \infty$, so the system is stable.

(2) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |e^{t}u(-t)| dt = \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{t} dt = 1 < \infty$, so the system is stable.
Although $e^{t}$ grows in the positive direction, $u(-t)$ restricts the signal to the negative direction, where it decays.

(3) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |u(t)| dt = \int_0^{\infty} 1 dt = \infty$, so the system is unstable.

2.6.3 Joint Assessment of Causality and Stability

For a causal system, where $h(t)=0, t<0$, the stability condition simplifies to

$$
\boxed{\int_0^{\infty} |h(t)| dt < \infty}
$$

For a causal system of the form $h(t) = \sum_{k} C_k e^{\lambda_k t}u(t)$, the necessary and sufficient condition for stability is that every characteristic root have a negative real part:

$$
\boxed{\Re{\lambda_k} < 0, \quad \forall k}
$$

In plain language: all of the system’s “natural modes” must decay over time rather than grow.

Example 9: Combined Assessment

A causal system has the impulse response $h(t) = e^{-2t}\cos(3t)u(t)$. Determine whether it is stable.

Solution:

$$
\int_0^{\infty} |e^{-2t}\cos(3t)| dt \le \int_0^{\infty} e^{-2t} dt = \frac{1}{2} < \infty
$$

The system is therefore stable.

Alternatively, the characteristic roots are $-2 \pm j3$, whose real part is $-2 < 0$, so the system is stable.

2.7 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: Why is convolution called “convolution”?

A: The name comes from the operation itself. In convolution, one function is first folded and then “rolled together” with another. The English word convolution comes from the Latin convolvere, meaning “to roll together.” The Chinese term conveys the same idea.

Q2: What if I cannot remember the four steps of convolution?

A: Remember the sequence fold, shift, multiply, integrate. Draw the process several times. The key is to understand how the overlap area changes with the shift $t$.

Q3: What is the difference between a zero-input response and a zero-state response?

A:

  • Zero-input: energy “stored” inside the system is released on its own, corresponding to the system’s memory or inertia.
  • Zero-state: the response is caused directly by an external input, corresponding to the system’s reaction to its present input.

Analogy: consider a compressed spring that is suddenly released.

  • The spring’s own vibration is the zero-input response, produced by releasing its initially stored energy.
  • If you also keep pushing and pulling the spring, the externally driven motion is the zero-state response.
  • The total motion is the sum of the two.

Q4: How do I choose a method for calculating convolution?

A:

  • If both functions have simple analytical expressions, integrate directly.
  • For piecewise functions such as rectangular pulses, the graphical method is easiest to understand.
  • If suitable transform pairs and properties are known, use those properties.
  • If the functions are complicated, use a transform-domain method after learning the Laplace transform.

Q5: What is the relationship between the impulse response $h(t)$ and the system?

A: $h(t)$ is a “complete description” of the system. Just as a person’s DNA determines that person’s characteristics, $h(t)$ completely determines the behavior of an LTI system. Once $h(t)$ is known, the response to any input can be calculated.

Q6: What is the relationship between causality and stability?

A: Causality and stability are independent concepts:

Causal Noncausal
Stable $e^{-t}u(t)$ $e^{-\lvert t \rvert}$
Unstable $u(t)$ $e^{\lvert t \rvert}$

A system may be causal but unstable, such as an integrator, or noncausal but stable, such as $e^{-|t|}$.

Q7: What does the order of a differential equation mean?

A: The order equals the highest derivative order. A first-order system has one energy-storage element, such as a capacitor; a second-order system has two, such as an inductor and a capacitor. The higher the order, the more complicated the system’s dynamic behavior.

Example 10: Solving with Convolution Properties

Given $x(t) = \delta(t) + \delta(t-1)$ and $h(t) = e^{-t}[u(t) - u(t-2)]$, find $y(t) = x(t) * h(t)$.

Solution:

Use the distributive property:

$$
y(t) = \delta(t) * h(t) + \delta(t-1) * h(t) = h(t) + h(t-1)
$$

Therefore,

$$
y(t) = e^{-t}[u(t) - u(t-2)] + e^{-(t-1)}[u(t-1) - u(t-3)]
$$

This result can be written piecewise:

  • $t < 0$: $y(t)=0$
  • $0 \le t < 1$: $y(t) = e^{-t}$
  • $1 \le t < 2$: $y(t) = e^{-t} + e^{-(t-1)}$
  • $2 \le t < 3$: $y(t) = e^{-(t-1)}$
  • $t \ge 3$: $y(t)=0$

Example 11: Determining System Characteristics

An LTI system has impulse response $h(t) = \sin(2t)u(t)$. Determine
(1) whether it is causal and
(2) whether it is stable.

Solution:

(1) $h(t) = 0$ when $t < 0$ because it is multiplied by $u(t)$, so the system is causal.

(2) Check absolute integrability:

$$
\int_0^{\infty} |\sin(2t)| dt
$$

$\sin(2t)$ does not decay. The integral of its absolute value over one period is positive, so summing across infinitely many periods produces an infinite value. The system is therefore unstable.

In fact, $\sin(2t)u(t)$ corresponds to an undamped oscillatory system: it continues to oscillate forever without stopping.

3.1 From the Time Domain to the Frequency Domain: A Different View of the Problem

3.1.1 Why Do We Need a Transform?

In the first two chapters, we always analyzed signals in the “time domain”—we examined how a signal changes over time. Some problems, however, are difficult to analyze in the time domain. For example:

  • Which frequency components does a signal contain?
  • How can we design a filter to remove noise?
  • How does a system respond differently to inputs at different frequencies?

Frequency-domain analysis means viewing the world from the perspective of “frequency,” rather like using a prism to split white light into its seven constituent colors.

Intuitive explanation:

  • Time domain: examine how the waveform of a music signal changes over time (an amplitude-time plot)
  • Frequency domain: examine which notes the music signal contains (an amplitude-frequency plot)

3.1.2 Fourier’s Core Idea

Any periodic signal can be represented as a superposition of sinusoids (or complex exponentials) at different frequencies.

$$
x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Just as white light can be split into seven colors, any signal can be decomposed into sinusoids at different frequencies. The amplitudes and phases of these sinusoids are determined by the signal’s “spectrum.”

3.2 Fourier Series of Periodic Signals

3.2.1 Trigonometric Form of the Fourier Series

A periodic signal $x(t)$ with period $T$ (and fundamental angular frequency $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$) can be represented as:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \cos(n\omega_0 t) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n \sin(n\omega_0 t)}
$$

where:

$$
a_0 = \frac{1}{T} \int_{T} x(t) dt \quad \text{（直流分量）}
$$

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{T} x(t) \cos(n\omega_0 t) dt
$$

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{T} x(t) \sin(n\omega_0 t) dt
$$

$\int_T$ denotes integration over one period (either from $0$ to $T$, or from $-T/2$ to $T/2$).

Intuitive explanation:

  • $a_0$: the signal’s DC (average) component
  • $a_n\cos(n\omega_0 t)$: cosine components (the even-function component)
  • $b_n\sin(n\omega_0 t)$: sine components (the odd-function component)

Another Equivalent Representation

$$
\boxed{x(t) = c_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \cos(n\omega_0 t + \varphi_n)}
$$

where:

$$
c_0 = a_0, \quad c_n = \sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2}, \quad \varphi_n = -\arctan\left(\frac{b_n}{a_n}\right)
$$

$c_n$ is the amplitude of the $n$th harmonic, and $\varphi_n$ is its phase.

Example 1: Fourier Series of a Square Wave

Find the Fourier series of a square wave with period $T$, amplitude 1, and a 50% duty cycle.

Over one period, the square wave is defined as:

$$
x(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & -T/4 < t < T/4 \ 0, & T/4 < |t| < T/2 \end{cases}
$$

Solution:

$\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$

Calculate $a_0$:

$$
a_0 = \frac{1}{T} \int_{-T/4}^{T/4} 1 \cdot dt = \frac{1}{T} \cdot \frac{T}{2} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

Calculate $a_n$:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{T} \int_{-T/4}^{T/4} \cos(n\omega_0 t) dt = \frac{2}{T} \cdot \left[\frac{\sin(n\omega_0 t)}{n\omega_0}\right]_{-T/4}^{T/4}
$$

$$
= \frac{2}{T} \cdot \frac{2\sin(n\omega_0 T/4)}{n\omega_0} = \frac{4\sin(n\pi/2)}{n \cdot 2\pi/T \cdot T} = \frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi}
$$

Calculate $b_n$: because $x(t)$ is an even function ($x(t) = x(-t)$), every $b_n = 0$.

Therefore:

$$
x(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi} \cos(n\omega_0 t)
$$

Expanding this expression (note the values of $\sin(n\pi/2)$) gives:

$$
x(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\cos(\omega_0 t) - \frac{2}{3\pi}\cos(3\omega_0 t) + \frac{2}{5\pi}\cos(5\omega_0 t) - \cdots
$$

Important observations:

  • Only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5, 7, …)
  • The amplitude decreases as the frequency increases (following a $1/n$ law)
  • Higher-order harmonics contribute less

3.2.2 Exponential Form of the Fourier Series

Using Euler’s formula, the trigonometric form can be converted into the more concise exponential form:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{jn\omega_0 t}}
$$

where the coefficients are:

$$
\boxed{c_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_{T} x(t) e^{-jn\omega_0 t} dt}
$$

Relationship to the coefficients in the trigonometric form:

$$
c_0 = a_0
$$

$$
c_n = \frac{a_n - jb_n}{2}, \quad n > 0
$$

$$
c_{-n} = \frac{a_n + jb_n}{2} = c_n^*, \quad n > 0
$$

Intuitive explanation: The exponential form handles positive and negative frequencies in a unified way. Negative frequencies have no direct physical meaning, but they simplify the mathematics.

Example 2: Exponential Fourier Series of a Square Wave

Find the exponential Fourier series of the square wave from the previous example.

Solution:

$$
c_n = \frac{1}{T} \int_{-T/4}^{T/4} e^{-jn\omega_0 t} dt = \frac{1}{T} \cdot \left[\frac{e^{-jn\omega_0 t}}{-jn\omega_0}\right]_{-T/4}^{T/4}
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{T} \cdot \frac{e^{-jn\omega_0 T/4} - e^{jn\omega_0 T/4}}{-jn\omega_0} = \frac{2\sin(n\omega_0 T/4)}{Tn\omega_0}
$$

Substitute $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$:

$$
c_n = \frac{\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi}
$$

Therefore:

$$
x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi} e^{jn\omega_0 t}
$$

3.2.3 The Concept of a Spectrum

Amplitude spectrum: a plot of $|c_n|$ against frequency $n\omega_0$
Phase spectrum: a plot of $\angle c_n$ against frequency $n\omega_0$

Intuitive explanation: A spectrum is like a signal’s “recipe”—it tells you which frequency “ingredients” make up the signal and how much of each ingredient is present.

For the square wave in the example, its amplitude spectrum consists of discrete spectral lines:

  • DC component: $1/2$
  • Fundamental ($\omega_0$): $1/\pi$
  • Third harmonic: $1/(3\pi)$
  • Fifth harmonic: $1/(5\pi)$

3.2.4 Convergence Conditions for Fourier Series (Dirichlet Conditions)

A sufficient set of conditions for a periodic signal to have a Fourier-series expansion is the Dirichlet conditions:

  1. Absolutely integrable: $\int_T |x(t)| dt < \infty$ over one period
  2. A finite number of extrema: only finitely many maxima and minima occur within one period
  3. A finite number of discontinuities: only finitely many discontinuities occur within one period

Intuitive explanation: Most real-world signals satisfy these conditions. Even a rectangular wave satisfies them, despite being discontinuous at its transitions.

3.3 Fourier Transform of Aperiodic Signals

3.3.1 From the Fourier Series to the Fourier Transform

As the period $T \to \infty$, a periodic signal becomes an aperiodic signal. Then:

  • The spectral-line spacing $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T \to 0$
  • A discrete spectrum becomes a continuous spectrum
  • $c_n$ becomes $X(j\omega)$

Definition of the Fourier transform:

$$
\boxed{X(j\omega) = \mathcal{F}{x(t)} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt}
$$

Inverse Fourier transform:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}{X(j\omega)} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega) e^{j\omega t} d\omega}
$$

Intuitive explanation:

  • Forward transform: “translate” a time-domain signal into a frequency-domain representation (analyze which frequencies the signal contains)
  • Inverse transform: “translate” a frequency-domain representation back into a time-domain signal (synthesize the signal from its frequencies)

3.3.2 Physical Meaning of the Fourier Transform

$X(j\omega)$ is the “density” of the signal $x(t)$ at frequency $\omega$—it indicates how much of the component at frequency $\omega$ the signal contains.

$$
|X(j\omega)| \quad \text{幅度谱密度}
$$

$$
\angle X(j\omega) \quad \text{相位谱}
$$

3.4 Fourier-Transform Pairs for Common Signals

3.4.1 Impulse Signal

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{\delta(t)} = 1}
$$

Interpretation: An impulse signal contains every frequency component, with equal amplitude at every frequency. This is why striking something with a hammer produces a sound containing “all frequencies.”

3.4.2 DC Signal

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{1} = 2\pi\delta(\omega)}
$$

Interpretation: A DC signal contains only the zero-frequency component.

3.4.3 One-Sided Exponential Signal

$$
x(t) = e^{-at}u(t), \quad a > 0
$$

$$
\boxed{X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{a + j\omega}}
$$

Amplitude spectrum:

$$
|X(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + \omega^2}}
$$

Phase spectrum:

$$
\angle X(j\omega) = -\arctan\left(\frac{\omega}{a}\right)
$$

3.4.4 Unit-Step Signal

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{u(t)} = \pi\delta(\omega) + \frac{1}{j\omega}}
$$

Note: A step signal contains both a DC component ($\pi\delta(\omega)$) and an AC component ($1/j\omega$).

3.4.5 Rectangular Pulse

$$
x(t) = \begin{cases} 1, & |t| < T/2 \ 0, & |t| > T/2 \end{cases}
$$

$$
\boxed{X(j\omega) = \frac{2\sin(\omega T/2)}{\omega} = T \cdot \text{sinc}\left(\frac{\omega T}{2\pi}\right)}
$$

Here $\text{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}$ (some textbooks instead define it as $\frac{\sin x}{x}$).

3.4.6 Sinusoidal Signals

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{\cos(\omega_0 t)} = \pi[\delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \delta(\omega + \omega_0)]}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{\sin(\omega_0 t)} = j\pi[\delta(\omega + \omega_0) - \delta(\omega - \omega_0)]}
$$

Interpretation: A pure sinusoid corresponds to only one spectral line (or one pair of spectral lines) in the frequency domain—which is precisely what a “monochromatic wave” means.

3.4.7 Gaussian Signal

$$
x(t) = e^{-at^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{X(j\omega) = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}} e^{-\omega^2/(4a)}}
$$

Interesting property: The Fourier transform of a Gaussian signal is still a Gaussian function.

3.5 Important Properties of the Fourier Transform

3.5.1 Linearity

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{ax_1(t) + bx_2(t)} = aX_1(j\omega) + bX_2(j\omega)}
$$

Verification: Substitute the expression directly into the definition; integration is a linear operation.

3.5.2 Time Shifting

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{x(t - t_0)} = X(j\omega) e^{-j\omega t_0}}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Delaying a signal by $t_0$ in the time domain is equivalent to multiplying it by $e^{-j\omega t_0}$ in the frequency domain (a phase rotation).

Verification:

$$
\mathcal{F}{x(t - t_0)} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t - t_0) e^{-j\omega t} dt
$$

Let $\tau = t - t_0$:

$$
= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) e^{-j\omega(\tau + t_0)} d\tau = e^{-j\omega t_0} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(\tau) e^{-j\omega\tau} d\tau = e^{-j\omega t_0} X(j\omega)
$$

3.5.3 Frequency Shifting

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{e^{j\omega_0 t} x(t)} = X(j(\omega - \omega_0))}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Multiplying a signal by $e^{j\omega_0 t}$ in the time domain shifts it to the right by $\omega_0$ in the frequency domain. This is the basis of modulation!

3.5.4 Time Scaling

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{x(at)} = \frac{1}{|a|} X\left(j\frac{\omega}{a}\right)}
$$

Intuitive explanation:

  • $a > 1$ (time-domain compression): the frequency domain expands and the amplitude decreases
  • $0 < a < 1$ (time-domain expansion): the frequency domain contracts and the amplitude increases

Physical meaning: The faster a signal changes in the time domain, the broader the bandwidth it occupies in the frequency domain. This is the mathematical explanation of “rapidly changing signal = wide frequency band.”

3.5.5 Duality

If $\mathcal{F}{x(t)} = X(j\omega)$, then:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{X(jt)} = 2\pi x(-\omega)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: The Fourier transform is symmetric—the roles of the time and frequency domains can be interchanged.

Application: Given one transform pair, duality can be used to obtain another.

Example 3: Using Duality

Given $\mathcal{F}{\delta(t)} = 1$, use duality to find $\mathcal{F}{1}$.

Solution:

From $\mathcal{F}{x(t)} = X(j\omega)$, if $x(t) = \delta(t)$, then $X(j\omega) = 1$.

By duality, $\mathcal{F}{X(jt)} = 2\pi x(-\omega)$.

Set $X(jt) = 1$ (the constant 1). Then:

$$
\mathcal{F}{1} = 2\pi \delta(-\omega) = 2\pi \delta(\omega)
$$

✓ Correct

3.5.6 Convolution (The Most Important Property!)

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{x(t) * h(t)} = X(j\omega) \cdot H(j\omega)}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{x(t) \cdot h(t)} = \frac{1}{2\pi} X(j\omega) * H(j\omega)}
$$

Meaning: Convolution in the time domain = multiplication in the frequency domain; multiplication in the time domain = convolution in the frequency domain.

Intuitive explanation:

  • A system’s “mixing” of a signal in the time domain (convolution) becomes simple “multiplication” in the frequency domain
  • This is why frequency-domain analysis is so important—it turns a complicated convolution into a simple multiplication!

Example 4: Using the Convolution Property

A system has impulse response $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$ and input $x(t) = e^{-2t}u(t)$. Find the output $y(t)$.

Solution:

$$
X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{2 + j\omega}, \quad H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega}
$$

By the convolution property:

$$
Y(j\omega) = X(j\omega)H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{(2 + j\omega)(1 + j\omega)}
$$

Partial-fraction expansion gives:

$$
Y(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega} - \frac{1}{2 + j\omega}
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
y(t) = (e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t)
$$

This is much simpler than calculating the time-domain convolution!

3.5.7 Modulation

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}{x(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)} = \frac{1}{2}[X(j(\omega - \omega_0)) + X(j(\omega + \omega_0))]}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Modulation shifts a signal’s spectrum to $\pm\omega_0$. This is the principle behind AM broadcasting!

3.5.8 Differentiation in the Time Domain

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}\left{\frac{d^n x(t)}{dt^n}\right} = (j\omega)^n X(j\omega)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Differentiation in the time domain corresponds to multiplication by $j\omega$ in the frequency domain—differentiation emphasizes high-frequency components.

3.5.9 Integration in the Time Domain

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{F}\left{\int_{-\infty}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau\right} = \frac{X(j\omega)}{j\omega} + \pi X(0)\delta(\omega)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Integration in the time domain corresponds to division by $j\omega$ in the frequency domain—integration suppresses high-frequency components.

3.5.10 Parseval’s Theorem

$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)|^2 dt = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |X(j\omega)|^2 d\omega}
$$

Intuitive explanation: A signal’s total energy in the time domain equals its total energy in the frequency domain—the Fourier transform does not change the signal’s energy.

3.7 Frequency Response of an LTI System

3.7.1 Definition of Frequency Response

For an LTI system, the frequency response is defined as:

$$
\boxed{H(j\omega) = \frac{Y(j\omega)}{X(j\omega)}}
$$

where $X(j\omega)$ and $Y(j\omega)$ are the Fourier transforms of the input and output, respectively.

Relationship to the impulse response:

$$
\boxed{H(j\omega) = \mathcal{F}{h(t)}}
$$

Intuitive explanation: The frequency response tells us how the system processes inputs at different frequencies—whether it amplifies or attenuates them, and whether it advances or delays their phase.

3.7.2 Physical Meaning of Frequency Response

If the input is the sinusoid $x(t) = \cos(\omega_0 t)$, the output is:

$$
y(t) = |H(j\omega_0)| \cos(\omega_0 t + \angle H(j\omega_0))
$$

This means:

  • The output frequency is unchanged (it remains $\omega_0$)
  • The amplitude is multiplied by $|H(j\omega_0)|$
  • The phase is increased by $\angle H(j\omega_0)$

This is the eigenfunction property of LTI systems—when a sinusoid is fed into an LTI system, the output remains a sinusoid at the same frequency; only its amplitude and phase change.

3.7.3 Filtering Characteristics of Systems

According to the shape of their frequency responses, systems can be classified as:

Type Characteristic Frequency response
Low-pass Passes low frequencies and attenuates high frequencies
High-pass Passes high frequencies and attenuates low frequencies
Band-pass Passes a particular frequency band
Band-stop Attenuates a particular frequency band

Example 5: RC Low-Pass Filter

The frequency response of an RC circuit is:

$$
H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}
$$

Its magnitude response is $|H(j\omega)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega RC)^2}}$.

  • When $\omega = 0$ (DC): $|H(0)| = 1$, so DC passes completely
  • When $\omega = 1/(RC)$ (the cutoff frequency): $|H| = 1/\sqrt{2}$, so the power is halved (the -3 dB point)
  • When $\omega \to \infty$: $|H| \to 0$, so high frequencies are suppressed

3.8 Sampling Theorem

3.8.1 Time-Domain Sampling

Sampling is the process of converting a continuous signal into a discrete signal:

$$
x_s(t) = x(t) \cdot \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - nT_s) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT_s)\delta(t - nT_s)
$$

Here $T_s$ is the sampling period, and $f_s = 1/T_s$ is the sampling frequency.

3.8.2 Sampling Theorem (Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem)

Theorem: If a signal has a highest frequency of $\omega_M$ (or $f_M$), then the sampling frequency must satisfy the following condition for the original signal to be recovered completely from its samples:

$$
\boxed{f_s \ge 2f_M} \quad \text{或} \quad \boxed{\omega_s \ge 2\omega_M}
$$

Here $2f_M$ is called the Nyquist rate, while $f_s/2$ is called the Nyquist frequency (or folding frequency).

Intuitive explanation: The sampling frequency must be at least twice the signal’s highest frequency to ensure that no information is lost. It is like photographing a fast-moving object: the shutter must be fast enough to capture the details.

3.8.3 Aliasing

If the sampling frequency is lower than the Nyquist rate, high-frequency components are “aliased” into the low-frequency region, causing distortion.

Intuitive explanation: The wagon-wheel effect in films, in which a wheel appears to rotate backward, is an example of aliasing—the wheel’s actual rotation rate exceeds half the sampling frame rate.

3.8.4 Signal Reconstruction

Ideally, the original signal can be recovered from its samples by using a low-pass filter:

$$
x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT_s) \cdot \text{sinc}\left(\frac{t - nT_s}{T_s}\right)
$$

3.9 Gibbs Phenomenon

3.9.1 What Is the Gibbs Phenomenon?

When a Fourier series is used to approximate a signal with jumps (discontinuities), an overshoot appears near each discontinuity. No matter how many terms are included, the magnitude of this overshoot never disappears (it is about 9% of the jump magnitude).

Intuitive explanation: Even if many sinusoids are used to approximate a square wave, “ringing” always appears near its discontinuities—as though the corners of the square wave can never be made entirely square.

3.9.2 Mathematical Explanation of the Gibbs Phenomenon

Consider approximating a square wave with the first $N$ terms of its Fourier series:

$$
x_N(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{2\sin(n\pi/2)}{n\pi} \cos(n\omega_0 t)
$$

As $N$ increases, the approximation becomes progressively better away from the discontinuities. Near each discontinuity, however, the peak approaches about 1.089 times the jump magnitude (that is, an overshoot of 9%).

3.9.3 Practical Significance

  • In filter design, the Gibbs phenomenon causes “ringing” in the transition band between the passband and stopband
  • This is why a real filter needs a transition band, rather than an ideal, abrupt cutoff

3.10 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What Does a Negative Frequency Mean? Does It Exist Physically?

A: A negative frequency is a mathematical concept with no direct physical meaning. It arises from the complex-exponential representations $e^{j\omega t}$ and $e^{-j\omega t}$, which must be combined to produce a real sinusoidal signal. One way to understand it is that negative frequencies exist to preserve mathematical symmetry.

Q2: Is a Spectrum Discrete or Continuous?

A:

  • Periodic signals → discrete spectra (line spectra), with values only at $n\omega_0$
  • Aperiodic signals → continuous spectra, with values at all frequencies

Q3: What Is the Relationship Between the Fourier Transform and the Fourier Series?

A: The Fourier series is a special case of the Fourier transform—namely, the case in which the signal is periodic. The relationship between the Fourier-series coefficients $c_n$ and the Fourier transform $X(j\omega)$ is:

$$
c_n = \frac{1}{T} X(jn\omega_0)
$$

Q4: Why Learn the Fourier Transform? What Are Its Practical Applications?

A:

  • Communication systems: modulation, demodulation, and frequency-division multiplexing
  • Image processing: JPEG compression (which is based on the DCT and related to the Fourier transform)
  • Audio processing: MP3 encoding and noise reduction
  • Medicine: MRI and ECG analysis
  • Any field involving “frequency”

Q5: Can a Signal Be Finite in Both the Time and Frequency Domains?

A: No (the uncertainty principle). If a signal is strictly time-limited (nonzero only over a finite interval), then it must have infinite extent in the frequency domain. The converse is also true. This is the signal-processing manifestation of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

Q6: How Is the Sampling Theorem Used in Practice?

A: In practice, one usually chooses $f_s > 2f_M$ (rather than equality) to leave some margin. For example:

  • Speech signals have a highest frequency of about 4 kHz, and telephone systems sample them at 8 kHz
  • Audio CDs cover frequencies up to about 20 kHz and use a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz
  • Before sampling, an anti-aliasing filter (a low-pass filter) is usually needed to remove frequency components above $f_s/2$

3.12 Comprehensive Examples

Example 6: Finding a Fourier Transform Using Its Properties

Find the Fourier transform of $x(t) = e^{-a|t|}$.

Solution:

Decompose $e^{-a|t|}$ as:

$$
e^{-a|t|} = e^{-at}u(t) + e^{at}u(-t)
$$

We know that $\mathcal{F}{e^{-at}u(t)} = \frac{1}{a + j\omega}$.

Using duality, or calculating directly:

$$
\mathcal{F}{e^{at}u(-t)} = \frac{1}{a - j\omega}
$$

Therefore:

$$
X(j\omega) = \frac{1}{a + j\omega} + \frac{1}{a - j\omega} = \frac{2a}{a^2 + \omega^2}
$$

Verification: This is a real even function, and its Fourier transform is also a real even function.

Example 7: System Analysis

An LTI system has frequency response $H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega}$, and its input is $x(t) = \cos(2t)$. Find the output.

Solution:

At $\omega = 2$:

$$
H(j2) = \frac{1}{1 + j2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} e^{-j\arctan(2)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
y(t) = |H(j2)| \cos(2t + \angle H(j2)) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \cos\left(2t - \arctan(2)\right)
$$

Example 8: Ideal Low-Pass Filter

An ideal low-pass filter has frequency response:

$$
H(j\omega) = \begin{cases} 1, & |\omega| < \omega_c \ 0, & |\omega| > \omega_c \end{cases}
$$

Find its impulse response $h(t)$.

Solution:

$$
h(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c} 1 \cdot e^{j\omega t} d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{e^{j\omega_c t} - e^{-j\omega_c t}}{jt} = \frac{\sin(\omega_c t)}{\pi t} = \frac{\omega_c}{\pi} \text{sinc}\left(\frac{\omega_c t}{\pi}\right)
$$

Note: $h(t)$ is nonzero for $t<0$, so an ideal low-pass filter is noncausal and therefore physically unrealizable. A real filter can only approximate it.

Why is the Laplace transform so important? Because it converts differential equations into algebraic equations, convolution into multiplication, and time-domain analysis into analysis in the complex-frequency domain (the s-domain). It therefore makes problems much easier to analyze and solve. About 40% of the exam questions are related to it.

4.1 Definition of the Laplace Transform

4.1.1 From the Fourier Transform to the Laplace Transform

The Fourier transform $X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt$ can only handle absolutely integrable signals.

Problem: Many important signals, such as $e^{at}u(t), a>0$ and $u(t)$, do not satisfy the absolute-integrability condition.

Solution: Multiply the signal by a decay factor $e^{-\sigma t}$ (where $\sigma$ is real) to make it “converge”:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} [x(t)e^{-\sigma t}] e^{-j\omega t} dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-(\sigma + j\omega)t} dt
$$

Let $s = \sigma + j\omega$ (a complex frequency). This gives the bilateral Laplace transform:

$$
\boxed{X(s) = \mathcal{L}{x(t)} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt}
$$

Intuitive explanation: The Laplace transform is an “upgraded” Fourier transform. By introducing $s = \sigma + j\omega$, it extends frequency-domain analysis into the complex-frequency domain. Multiplication by $e^{-\sigma t}$ makes signals that would otherwise diverge amenable to analysis.

4.1.2 Bilateral vs. Unilateral Laplace Transform

Bilateral Laplace transform (bilateral LT):

$$
X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt
$$

Unilateral Laplace transform (unilateral LT):

$$
\boxed{X(s) = \int_{0^-}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt}
$$

This course primarily uses the unilateral Laplace transform, because:

  • Real systems are usually analyzed beginning at $t=0$
  • It automatically incorporates initial conditions
  • It is suitable for solving initial-value problems

Inverse Laplace transform:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi j} \int_{\sigma - j\infty}^{\sigma + j\infty} X(s) e^{st} ds}
$$

This is a contour integral of a complex function. We will not use this formula directly in this course; instead, we will mainly find inverse transforms through partial-fraction expansion and transform tables.

4.2 Region of Convergence (ROC)

4.2.1 Definition of the ROC

The region of convergence (ROC) is the range of values of $s$ for which the Laplace-transform integral converges.

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |x(t)e^{-st}| dt < \infty
$$

In other words, it is the set of values of $s$ for which $X(s)$ exists and is analytic.

4.2.2 Graphical Representation of the ROC

The ROC is shown in the s-plane (whose horizontal axis is $\Re{s} = \sigma$ and whose vertical axis is $\Im{s} = j\omega$):

4.2.3 Properties of the ROC

  1. The ROC is a strip parallel to the $j\omega$ axis (because convergence depends only on $\sigma = \Re{s}$)
  2. For a rational Laplace transform, the ROC contains no poles
  3. The boundaries of the ROC are determined by the pole locations
  4. If a signal is right-sided (nonzero only for $t>T$), its ROC lies to the right of its rightmost pole
  5. If a signal is left-sided (nonzero only for $t<T$), its ROC lies to the left of its leftmost pole
  6. If a signal is two-sided, its ROC is a strip
  7. If a signal has finite duration, its ROC is the entire s-plane (possibly excluding the origin or the point at infinity)

Signal Laplace transform ROC
$\delta(t)$ $1$ All $s$
$u(t)$ $1/s$ $\Re{s} > 0$
$-u(-t)$ $1/s$ $\Re{s} < 0$
$e^{-at}u(t)$ $1/(s+a)$ $\Re{s} > -a$
$-e^{-at}u(-t)$ $1/(s+a)$ $\Re{s} < -a$
$t^n u(t)$ $n!/s^{n+1}$ $\Re{s} > 0$
$\cos(\omega_0 t)u(t)$ $s/(s^2+\omega_0^2)$ $\Re{s} > 0$
$\sin(\omega_0 t)u(t)$ $\omega_0/(s^2+\omega_0^2)$ $\Re{s} > 0$

Important: The same expression for $X(s)$ can represent different signals when paired with different ROCs. For example, $1/s$ with the ROC $\Re{s} > 0$ represents $u(t)$, whereas with the ROC $\Re{s} < 0$ it represents $-u(-t)$.

Intuitive explanation: The ROC is like an “instruction manual”—an expression for the Laplace transform alone cannot identify the corresponding signal without its ROC.

Example 1: Determining the ROC

Find the Laplace transform and ROC of $x(t) = e^{-2t}u(t) + e^{-t}u(t)$.

Solution:

$$
X(s) = \frac{1}{s+2} + \frac{1}{s+1}, \quad \Re{s} > -1
$$

The ROC of the first fraction is $\Re{s} > -2$, and that of the second is $\Re{s} > -1$. Their intersection is $\Re{s} > -1$.

4.3 Properties of the Laplace Transform

4.3.1 Linearity

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}{ax_1(t) + bx_2(t)} = aX_1(s) + bX_2(s)}
$$

ROC: the intersection of the two signals’ ROCs (although it may be larger).

4.3.2 Time Shifting

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}{x(t - t_0)u(t - t_0)} = e^{-st_0} X(s), \quad t_0 \ge 0}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Delaying a signal by $t_0$ multiplies its transform by $e^{-st_0}$.

Verification:

$$
\mathcal{L}{x(t-t_0)u(t-t_0)} = \int_{0}^{\infty} x(t-t_0)e^{-st}dt
$$

Let $\tau = t - t_0$:

$$
= \int_{-t_0}^{\infty} x(\tau)e^{-s(\tau+t_0)}d\tau = e^{-st_0}\int_{0}^{\infty} x(\tau)e^{-s\tau}d\tau = e^{-st_0}X(s)
$$

4.3.3 Shifting in the s-Domain

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}{e^{s_0 t} x(t)} = X(s - s_0)}
$$

ROC: the original ROC shifted by $\Re{s_0}$.

Intuitive explanation: Multiplication by $e^{s_0 t}$ in the time domain shifts the transform by $s_0$.

4.3.4 Time Scaling

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}{x(at)} = \frac{1}{a} X\left(\frac{s}{a}\right), \quad a > 0}
$$

ROC: $\Re{s}$ is scaled by the same factor.

4.3.5 Differentiation in the Time Domain (Very Important)

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}\left{\frac{dx(t)}{dt}\right} = sX(s) - x(0^-)}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}\left{\frac{d^2 x(t)}{dt^2}\right} = s^2 X(s) - sx(0^-) - x’(0^-)}
$$

General form:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}\left{\frac{d^n x(t)}{dt^n}\right} = s^n X(s) - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} s^{n-1-k} x^{(k)}(0^-)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: A differential equation becomes an algebraic equation! This is the most important application of the Laplace transform. Differentiation in the time domain corresponds to multiplication by $s$ in the s-domain, together with subtraction of the initial conditions.

4.3.6 Integration in the Time Domain

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}\left{\int_{0^-}^{t} x(\tau) d\tau\right} = \frac{X(s)}{s}}
$$

4.3.7 Initial-Value Theorem

If $x(t)$ and its derivative have Laplace transforms, and the limit exists, then:

$$
\boxed{x(0^+) = \lim_{t \to 0^+} x(t) = \lim_{s \to \infty} sX(s)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: The behavior of $X(s)$ at infinity tells us how $x(t)$ behaves near the origin.

4.3.8 Final-Value Theorem

If the limit of $x(t)$ exists (that is, if the system is stable), then:

$$
\boxed{\lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = \lim_{s \to 0} sX(s)}
$$

Condition for use: All poles of $sX(s)$ must lie in the left half-plane (that is, the system must be stable).

Intuitive explanation: The behavior of $X(s)$ near $s=0$ tells us the steady-state value of $x(t)$.

Example 2: Initial- and Final-Value Theorems

Given $X(s) = \frac{s+2}{s(s+1)(s+3)}$, find $x(0^+)$ and $x(\infty)$.

Solution:

Initial value:

$$
x(0^+) = \lim_{s \to \infty} sX(s) = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{s(s+2)}{s(s+1)(s+3)} = \lim_{s \to \infty} \frac{s+2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = 0
$$

Final value (check the condition: the poles are $s=0, -1, -3$. Apart from $s=0$, all are in the left half-plane, but does the pole at $s=0$ allow a final value? We must check):

The poles of $sX(s) = \frac{s+2}{(s+1)(s+3)}$ are at $s=-1, s=-3$, both in the left half-plane. The final-value theorem therefore applies.

$$
x(\infty) = \lim_{s \to 0} sX(s) = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{s+2}{(s+1)(s+3)} = \frac{2}{3}
$$

4.3.9 Convolution

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}{x(t) * h(t)} = X(s) H(s)}
$$

This is one of the most important properties of the Laplace transform! Convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the s-domain.

Example 3: Using the Convolution Property

Use the convolution property to find $y(t) = e^{-t}u(t) * e^{-2t}u(t)$.

Solution:

$$
Y(s) = \frac{1}{s+1} \cdot \frac{1}{s+2} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
y(t) = (e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t)
$$

4.5 Inverse Laplace Transform: Partial-Fraction Expansion

4.5.1 Why Use Partial Fractions?

A Laplace transform usually takes the form of a rational function:

$$
X(s) = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)} = \frac{b_M s^M + b_{M-1} s^{M-1} + \cdots + b_0}{s^N + a_{N-1} s^{N-1} + \cdots + a_0}
$$

Partial-fraction expansion decomposes a complicated rational function into a sum of simple terms, each corresponding to a known transform pair.

4.5.2 The Proper-Fraction Case (N > M)

Case 1: All Poles Are Simple Poles (Distinct Real Roots)

$$
X(s) = \frac{N(s)}{(s-p_1)(s-p_2)\cdots(s-p_N)}
$$

Expand it as:

$$
X(s) = \frac{k_1}{s-p_1} + \frac{k_2}{s-p_2} + \cdots + \frac{k_N}{s-p_N}
$$

where the coefficients are:

$$
\boxed{k_i = (s-p_i)X(s)\Big|_{s=p_i}}
$$

Example 4: Expansion with Simple Poles

Find the inverse transform of $X(s) = \frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)}$.

Solution:

Expand:

$$
X(s) = \frac{k_1}{s+1} + \frac{k_2}{s+2}
$$

$$
k_1 = (s+1)X(s)\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{s+3}{s+2}\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{2}{1} = 2
$$

$$
k_2 = (s+2)X(s)\Big|{s=-2} = \frac{s+3}{s+1}\Big|{s=-2} = \frac{1}{-1} = -1
$$

Therefore:

$$
X(s) = \frac{2}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

Taking the inverse transform (assuming a causal signal with ROC $\Re{s} > -1$):

$$
\boxed{x(t) = (2e^{-t} - e^{-2t})u(t)}
$$

Case 2: Complex-Conjugate Poles

If the denominator of $X(s)$ includes a quadratic factor $s^2 + bs + c = 0$ with complex-conjugate roots $\alpha \pm j\beta$, the quadratic factor may be retained:

$$
X(s) = \frac{As + B}{(s+\alpha)^2 + \beta^2} + \text{其他项}
$$

The corresponding time-domain pairs are:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left{\frac{(s+\alpha)}{(s+\alpha)^2 + \beta^2}\right} = e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\beta t)u(t)
$$

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left{\frac{\beta}{(s+\alpha)^2 + \beta^2}\right} = e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\beta t)u(t)
$$

Example 5: Complex-Conjugate Poles

Find the inverse transform of $X(s) = \frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2 + 4}$.

Solution:

Recognize it directly as $\frac{(s+1)}{(s+1)^2 + 2^2}$, which corresponds to $e^{-t}\cos(2t)u(t)$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = e^{-t}\cos(2t)u(t)}
$$

Case 3: Repeated Poles

If $p$ is a pole of multiplicity $r$:

$$
X(s) = \frac{N(s)}{(s-p)^r D_1(s)}
$$

Expand it as:

$$
X(s) = \frac{k_{11}}{(s-p)^r} + \frac{k_{12}}{(s-p)^{r-1}} + \cdots + \frac{k_{1r}}{s-p} + \text{其他极点项}
$$

The coefficient formula is:

$$
k_{1j} = \frac{1}{(j-1)!} \frac{d^{j-1}}{ds^{j-1}}\left[(s-p)^r X(s)\right]\Big|_{s=p}
$$

The corresponding time-domain pair is:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left{\frac{1}{(s-p)^r}\right} = \frac{t^{r-1}}{(r-1)!} e^{pt} u(t)
$$

Example 6: Repeated Poles

Find the inverse transform of $X(s) = \frac{1}{(s+1)^2(s+2)}$.

Solution:

Expand:

$$
X(s) = \frac{k_{11}}{(s+1)^2} + \frac{k_{12}}{s+1} + \frac{k_2}{s+2}
$$

$$
k_{11} = (s+1)^2 X(s)\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{1}{s+2}\Big|{s=-1} = 1
$$

$$
k_{12} = \frac{d}{ds}\left[(s+1)^2 X(s)\right]\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{d}{ds}\left[\frac{1}{s+2}\right]\Big|{s=-1} = -\frac{1}{(s+2)^2}\Big|_{s=-1} = -1
$$

$$
k_2 = (s+2)X(s)\Big|{s=-2} = \frac{1}{(s+1)^2}\Big|{s=-2} = 1
$$

Therefore:

$$
X(s) = \frac{1}{(s+1)^2} - \frac{1}{s+1} + \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
\boxed{x(t) = (te^{-t} - e^{-t} + e^{-2t})u(t)}
$$

4.5.3 The Improper-Fraction Case (M >= N)

When the degree of the numerator is greater than or equal to that of the denominator, first simplify the expression using polynomial division:

$$
X(s) = \underbrace{c_{M-N} s^{M-N} + \cdots + c_1 s + c_0}{\text{多项式部分}} + \underbrace{\frac{N_1(s)}{D(s)}}{\text{真分式部分}}
$$

The polynomial part corresponds to $\delta(t)$ and its derivatives.

Example 7: Improper Fraction

Find the inverse transform of $X(s) = \frac{s^2 + 2s + 3}{s+1}$.

Solution:

Perform polynomial division:

$$
\frac{s^2 + 2s + 3}{s+1} = (s+1) + \frac{2}{s+1}
$$

Note that $s+1$ corresponds in the time domain to $\frac{d\delta(t)}{dt} + \delta(t)$.

Therefore:

$$
x(t) = \delta’(t) + \delta(t) + 2e^{-t}u(t)
$$

4.6 Solving Differential Equations with the Laplace Transform

4.6.1 Basic Steps

This is the central application of the Laplace transform! The procedure is:

  1. Take the Laplace transform of both sides of the differential equation (using the differentiation property to include initial conditions)
  2. Solve the resulting algebraic equation for $Y(s)$
  3. Expand $Y(s)$ into partial fractions
  4. Take the inverse Laplace transform to obtain $y(t)$

Example 8: Solving a Differential Equation with the Laplace Transform

Solve the differential equation $y’’(t) + 3y’(t) + 2y(t) = 2x’(t) + x(t)$, with initial conditions $y(0) = 1, y’(0) = 0$ and input $x(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Take the Laplace transform of both sides

$$
[s^2Y(s) - sy(0) - y’(0)] + 3[sY(s) - y(0)] + 2Y(s) = 2[sX(s) - x(0)] + X(s)
$$

Substitute the initial conditions $y(0)=1, y’(0)=0$:

$$
(s^2 + 3s + 2)Y(s) - s - 3 = (2s + 1)X(s) - 2x(0)
$$

Step 2: Substitute $X(s)$

$$
x(t) = e^{-t}u(t) \Rightarrow X(s) = \frac{1}{s+1}
$$

$x(0) = 1$ (as follows from $x(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$).

$$
(s^2 + 3s + 2)Y(s) - s - 3 = \frac{2s+1}{s+1} - 2
$$

Step 3: Solve for $Y(s)$

$$
(s^2 + 3s + 2)Y(s) = \frac{2s+1}{s+1} - 2 + s + 3 = \frac{2s+1}{s+1} + s + 1
$$

$$
= \frac{2s+1 + (s+1)^2}{s+1} = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{s+1}
$$

Therefore:

$$
Y(s) = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{(s+1)(s^2 + 3s + 2)} = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{(s+1)^2(s+2)}
$$

Step 4: Partial-fraction expansion

$$
Y(s) = \frac{k_{11}}{(s+1)^2} + \frac{k_{12}}{s+1} + \frac{k_2}{s+2}
$$

$$
k_{11} = (s+1)^2 Y(s)\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{s+2}\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{1 - 4 + 2}{1} = -1
$$

$$
k_{12} = \frac{d}{ds}\left[\frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{s+2}\right]\Big|{s=-1} = \frac{(2s+4)(s+2) - (s^2+4s+2)}{(s+2)^2}\Big|{s=-1}
$$

$$
= \frac{(2)(1) - (-1)}{1} = 3 \quad \text{（详细计算）}
$$

Let us calculate it carefully:

$$
f(s) = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{s+2}
$$

$$
f’(s) = \frac{(2s+4)(s+2) - (s^2+4s+2)}{(s+2)^2} = \frac{(2s^2+8s+8) - (s^2+4s+2)}{(s+2)^2} = \frac{s^2+4s+6}{(s+2)^2}
$$

$$
f’(-1) = \frac{1 - 4 + 6}{1} = 3
$$

Thus $k_{12} = 3$.

$$
k_2 = (s+2)Y(s)\Big|{s=-2} = \frac{s^2 + 4s + 2}{(s+1)^2}\Big|{s=-2} = \frac{4 - 8 + 2}{1} = -2
$$

Therefore:

$$
Y(s) = -\frac{1}{(s+1)^2} + \frac{3}{s+1} - \frac{2}{s+2}
$$

Step 5: Take the inverse transform

$$
\boxed{y(t) = (-te^{-t} + 3e^{-t} - 2e^{-2t})u(t)}
$$

Example 8: Comparison with the Time-Domain Method

Recall that in Chapter 2 we solved a similar problem with a differential equation (Example 6). The steps were cumbersome and error-prone. With the Laplace transform, the entire procedure becomes algebraic manipulation—this is what makes the Laplace transform so powerful!

4.7 System Function $H(s)$

4.7.1 Definition of the System Function

For an LTI system, the system function (also called the transfer function) is defined as:

$$
\boxed{H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}}
$$

when the initial conditions are zero.

4.7.2 Relationship Between the System Function and Impulse Response

$$
\boxed{H(s) = \mathcal{L}{h(t)}}
$$

$$
\boxed{h(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}{H(s)}}
$$

4.7.3 Finding the System Function from a Differential Equation

Given the differential equation:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k} = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k \frac{d^k x(t)}{dt^k}
$$

Take the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k s^k\right) Y(s) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k s^k\right) X(s)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{X(s)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k s^k}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k s^k}}
$$

Example 9: Finding a System Function

A system is described by the differential equation $y’’(t) + 3y’(t) + 2y(t) = x’(t) + 2x(t)$. Find $H(s)$.

Solution:

Take the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
(s^2 + 3s + 2)Y(s) = (s + 2)X(s)
$$

Therefore:

$$
H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{X(s)} = \frac{s + 2}{s^2 + 3s + 2} = \frac{s + 2}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{1}{s+1}
$$

The impulse response is:

$$
h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)
$$

4.8 Pole-Zero Analysis and System Stability

4.8.1 Definitions of Zeros and Poles

A system function can usually be written as a rational function:

$$
H(s) = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)} = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} (s - z_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} (s - p_i)}
$$

Zeros: values of $s$ that make $H(s) = 0$; that is, the roots $z_i$ of $N(s) = 0$.

Poles: values of $s$ that make $H(s) \to \infty$; that is, the roots $p_i$ of $D(s) = 0$.

4.8.2 Pole-Zero Plot

In the s-plane, a zero is represented by “o” and a pole by “x”:

4.8.3 Determining System Stability from Poles and Zeros

Necessary and sufficient condition for a causal LTI system to be stable: every pole of $H(s)$ must lie in the left half-plane of the s-plane (that is, every pole must have a negative real part).

$$
\boxed{\Re{p_i} < 0, \quad \forall i \iff \text{系统稳定}}
$$

Intuitive explanation:

  • Pole in the left half-plane → exponential decay → stable system
  • Pole in the right half-plane → exponential growth → unstable system
  • Pole on the imaginary axis → constant-amplitude oscillation → marginally stable (boundary stability)

Example 10: Determining Stability

Determine the stability of each system function:

(1) $H(s) = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+3)}$
(2) $H(s) = \frac{1}{(s-1)(s+2)}$
(3) $H(s) = \frac{1}{s^2 + \omega_0^2}$

Solution:

(1) Poles: $s=-1$ and $s=-3$, both in the left half-plane → stable

(2) Poles: $s=1$ (right half-plane) and $s=-2$ (left half-plane) → unstable

(3) Poles: $s = \pm j\omega_0$, on the imaginary axis → marginally stable

Pole location Time-domain waveform Stability
Negative real axis $\sigma < 0$ $e^{\sigma t}$, exponential decay Stable
Positive real axis $\sigma > 0$ $e^{\sigma t}$, exponential growth Unstable
Complex-conjugate poles in the left half-plane $\sigma \pm j\omega$ $e^{\sigma t}\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, decaying oscillation Stable
Complex-conjugate poles in the right half-plane $\sigma \pm j\omega$ $e^{\sigma t}\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, growing oscillation Unstable

4.8.5 Estimating Frequency Response from Poles and Zeros

For a stable system, its frequency response $H(j\omega)$ can be estimated from the pole-zero plot:

$$
|H(j\omega)| = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} |j\omega - z_i|}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} |j\omega - p_i|}
$$

$$
\angle H(j\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \angle(j\omega - z_i) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \angle(j\omega - p_i)
$$

Geometric meaning: The lengths of the vectors from each zero or pole to a point on the $j\omega$ axis determine the magnitude, while their angles determine the phase.

Example 13: RLC Circuit Analysis

A series RLC circuit has $R=2\Omega$, $L=1H$, and $C=0.5F$. Its initial conditions are $i(0^-)=1A$ and $v_c(0^-)=0V$, and its input voltage is $v_s(t)=u(t)V$. Find the current $i(t)$.

Solution:

The circuit equation is:

$$
L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri + \frac{1}{C}\int i dt = v_s(t)
$$

Substitute the numerical values:

$$
\frac{di}{dt} + 2i + 2\int i dt = u(t)
$$

Take the Laplace transform, including the initial conditions:

$$
[sI(s) - i(0^-)] + 2I(s) + \frac{2}{s}I(s) = \frac{1}{s}
$$

$$
sI(s) - 1 + 2I(s) + \frac{2}{s}I(s) = \frac{1}{s}
$$

$$
I(s)\left(s + 2 + \frac{2}{s}\right) = \frac{1}{s} + 1
$$

$$
I(s)\frac{s^2 + 2s + 2}{s} = \frac{1 + s}{s}
$$

Therefore:

$$
I(s) = \frac{s + 1}{s^2 + 2s + 2} = \frac{s + 1}{(s+1)^2 + 1}
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
\boxed{i(t) = e^{-t}\cos(t)u(t)}
$$

4.11 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What Exactly Is $s$? Why Is It Complex?

A: $s = \sigma + j\omega$ is a complex frequency. The $j\omega$ component represents oscillation (frequency), while the $\sigma$ component represents decay or growth (the envelope). When $\sigma = 0$, $s = j\omega$, and the Laplace transform reduces to the Fourier transform.

Intuitive explanation:

  • $e^{j\omega t}$: a sinusoidal oscillation with constant amplitude (pure frequency)
  • $e^{\sigma t}$: exponential growth or decay (the envelope)
  • $e^{(\sigma + j\omega)t}$: a sinusoidal oscillation that grows or decays

Q2: What Does the ROC $\Re{s} > -a$ Mean?

A: In the s-plane, $\Re{s} > -a$ is the entire region to the right of the vertical line $s = -a$. For example, the Laplace transform of $e^{-2t}u(t)$ is $1/(s+2)$, with the ROC $\Re{s} > -2$.

Q3: What If the Degree of the Numerator Exceeds That of the Denominator in a Partial-Fraction Expansion?

A: Use polynomial division. For example, for $\frac{s^3+2s}{s+1}$:

Divide $s^3$ by $s$ to obtain $s^2$; multiply by $(s+1)$ to obtain $s^3+s^2$; subtract to obtain $-s^2+2s$, and so on.
The final result is $s^2 - s + 1 - \frac{1}{s+1}$.

Q4: When Can the Initial- and Final-Value Theorems Not Be Used?

A:

  • Initial-value theorem:
    • If $x(t)$ has an impulse or its derivatives at $t=0$, the theorem gives $x(0^+)$ rather than $x(0^-)$
    • If $X(s)$ is not a proper fraction (the numerator degree is $\ge$ the denominator degree), the initial value may be infinite
  • Final-value theorem:
    • If $sX(s)$ has poles in the right half-plane or on the imaginary axis (apart from $s=0$), the theorem does not apply
    • The system must be stable (or at least must not oscillate or diverge)

Q5: What Is the Relationship Between Poles, Zeros, and Frequency Response?

A: This is a very important concept!

  • A zero near the imaginary axis: creates a dip in the magnitude response at that frequency
  • A pole near the imaginary axis: creates a peak (resonance) in the magnitude response at that frequency
  • A pole farther from the imaginary axis: produces a flatter and broader resonance peak

This is why the approximate shape of a system’s frequency response can be estimated quickly from its pole-zero plot.

Q6: If Every Pole of $H(s)$ Is in the Left Half-Plane, Must the System Be Stable?

A: Yes, for a causal LTI system. But note:

  • If the system is noncausal, stability requires the ROC to include the $j\omega$ axis
  • The poles of $H(s)$ must lie to the left of the ROC (for a right-sided signal) or to its right (for a left-sided signal)
  • The ROC of a causal system lies to the right of its poles, so every pole must lie in the left half-plane

Q7: How Is an Integrator in a Block Diagram Realized with an Operational Amplifier?

A: An op-amp integrator circuit is:

$v_o(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int v_i(t) dt$

This is why analog computers use operational amplifiers to perform integration.

Q8: When Should I Use the Laplace Transform, and When Should I Use the Fourier Transform?

Situation Transform Reason
Solving a differential equation Laplace Automatically handles initial conditions
Analyzing frequency characteristics Fourier Directly gives the frequency response
Stability analysis Laplace Stability can be determined from pole locations
Modulation and demodulation Fourier The frequency-shifting property is more intuitive
Signals containing impulses Laplace It has a broader region of convergence

5.1 Overview of Discrete-Time Signals

5.1.1 What Is a Discrete-Time Signal?

A discrete-time signal (also called a sequence) is defined only at discrete time instants. It is usually denoted by $x[n]$, where $n$ is an integer.

Difference from a continuous signal:

Characteristic Continuous signal $x(t)$ Discrete signal $x[n]$
Independent variable $t$ is any real number $n$ is an integer
Brackets Parentheses Square brackets
Values Defined at every instant Defined only at integer indices
Origin Physical signal Generated by sampling or computation

Intuitive explanation:

  • Continuous signal: like a continuous stream of water, changing at every instant
  • Discrete signal: like taking a photograph every second, recording only a “snapshot” at each discrete instant

5.1.2 Ways to Represent a Sequence

Formula:
$$
x[n] = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n u[n]
$$

Plot: use a vertical stem to show the value at each $n$

List:
$$
x[n] = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \cdots}, \quad n \ge 0
$$

5.2 Basic Sequences

5.2.1 Unit-Impulse Sequence $\delta[n]$

Definition:

$$
\boxed{\delta[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n = 0 \ 0, & n \neq 0 \end{cases}}
$$

Intuitive explanation: This is the discrete version of the “impulse signal,” but it is much simpler than the continuous version—it is simply a single value of 1 at $n=0$.

Sifting property:

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\delta[n-n_0] = x[n_0]}
$$

Relationship to the unit impulse: The continuous impulse $\delta(t)$ is a generalized function that is “infinitely tall, infinitely narrow, and has area 1,” whereas the discrete impulse $\delta[n]$ is merely one sequence sample whose value is 1—much simpler!

5.2.2 Unit-Step Sequence $u[n]$

Definition:

$$
\boxed{u[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & n \ge 0 \ 0, & n < 0 \end{cases}}
$$

Note: Unlike the continuous step, the discrete step is defined to equal 1 at $n=0$ (rather than $1/2$).

Relationship to the impulse:

$$
\delta[n] = u[n] - u[n-1]
$$

$$
u[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} \delta[k] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \delta[n-k]
$$

5.2.3 Rectangular Sequence $R_N[n]$

Definition:

$$
\boxed{R_N[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le n \le N-1 \ 0, & \text{其他} \end{cases}}
$$

Representation using steps:

$$
R_N[n] = u[n] - u[n-N]
$$

5.2.4 Real Exponential Sequence

$$
\boxed{x[n] = a^n u[n]}
$$

  • $|a| < 1$: decaying sequence
  • $|a| > 1$: growing sequence
  • $a > 0$: all samples have one sign (monotonic)
  • $a < 0$: alternating positive and negative samples (oscillatory)

5.2.5 Complex Exponential Sequence

$$
\boxed{x[n] = e^{(\sigma + j\Omega_0)n} = e^{\sigma n} e^{j\Omega_0 n}}
$$

Difference from a continuous complex exponential:

One important difference is that a discrete complex exponential is periodic in frequency:

$$
e^{j(\Omega_0 + 2\pi)n} = e^{j\Omega_0 n} e^{j2\pi n} = e^{j\Omega_0 n}
$$

Because $n$ is an integer, $e^{j2\pi n} = 1$.

Therefore, discrete complex exponentials at $\Omega_0$ and $\Omega_0 + 2\pi$ are exactly identical! This means that a discrete frequency is unique only within a range of $2\pi$ (usually taken as $[-\pi, \pi]$ or $[0, 2\pi]$).

Intuitive explanation: A continuous frequency can be arbitrarily high, but a discrete frequency is meaningful only within a range of $2\pi$—a “high frequency” above $2\pi$ is identical to some “low frequency” in $[0, 2\pi]$.

5.2.6 Sinusoidal Sequence

$$
\boxed{x[n] = A\cos(\Omega_0 n + \phi)}
$$

Important: A sinusoidal sequence is not necessarily periodic! It is periodic only when $\Omega_0/2\pi$ is rational.

5.3 Sampling: Conversion from Continuous to Discrete Time

5.3.1 Ideal Sampling

Sampling is performed using a periodic impulse train:

$$
x[n] = x_c(nT_s)
$$

Here $T_s$ is the sampling period, and $f_s = 1/T_s$ is the sampling frequency.

5.3.2 Review of the Sampling Theorem

As we learned earlier (in Chapter 3):

  • If the highest frequency of $x_c(t)$ is $f_M$, then $f_s \ge 2f_M$ must be satisfied
  • Otherwise, aliasing occurs

5.3.3 Relationship Between Continuous and Discrete Frequencies

$$
\Omega = \omega T_s = \frac{2\pi f}{f_s}
$$

Here $\Omega$ is the discrete frequency (radians/sample), while $\omega$ is the continuous frequency (radians/second).

Important relationships:

  • The continuous frequency $\omega_s = 2\pi/T_s$ corresponds to the discrete frequency $\Omega = 2\pi$
  • The continuous frequency $\omega_s/2 = \pi/T_s$ (the Nyquist frequency) corresponds to the discrete frequency $\Omega = \pi$
  • Digital-filter design specifications are usually converted from continuous frequencies

5.4 Difference-Equation Description of a Discrete LTI System

5.4.1 Form of a Difference Equation

A discrete LTI system can be described by a linear constant-coefficient difference equation:

$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k y[n-k] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k x[n-k]}
$$

A more common form is:

$$
y[n] = -\sum_{k=1}^{N} \frac{a_k}{a_0} y[n-k] + \sum_{k=0}^{M} \frac{b_k}{a_0} x[n-k]
$$

Intuitive explanation: The current output $y[n]$ is determined jointly by past output values and present or past input values.

5.4.2 Order of a Difference Equation

The system order is determined by $N$ (the number of past output values needed).

  • First order: $y[n] + a_1 y[n-1] = b_0 x[n]$
  • Second order: $y[n] + a_1 y[n-1] + a_2 y[n-2] = b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1]$

5.4.3 Initial Conditions

Solving a difference equation requires initial conditions. An $N$th-order difference equation requires $N$ initial conditions:

$$
y[-1], y[-2], \cdots, y[-N]
$$

5.5 Solving Difference Equations

5.5.1 Iterative Method

Calculate the output recursively, one sample at a time. This method is suitable for computer implementation.

$$
y[n] = b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] - a_1 y[n-1]
$$

Given $y[-1]$, we can successively find $y[0], y[1], y[2], \cdots$.

Example 1: Iterative Solution

The system is $y[n] + \frac{1}{2} y[n-1] = x[n]$, its initial condition is $y[-1] = 1$, and its input is $x[n] = u[n]$.

Solution:

First rewrite the equation as:

$$
y[n] = x[n] - \frac{1}{2} y[n-1]
$$

Calculate iteratively:

  • $n=0$: $y[0] = x[0] - \frac{1}{2}y[-1] = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{2}$
  • $n=1$: $y[1] = x[1] - \frac{1}{2}y[0] = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{4}$
  • $n=2$: $y[2] = x[2] - \frac{1}{2}y[1] = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{5}{8}$
  • $n=3$: $y[3] = x[3] - \frac{1}{2}y[2] = 1 - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{5}{8} = \frac{11}{16}$

We can see that $y[n] = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3}(-\frac{1}{2})^n$ (a closed-form solution can be found later using the Z-transform).

5.5.2 Classical Method

As with a differential equation, the solution of a difference equation consists of a homogeneous solution and a particular solution.

Homogeneous solution: solve the characteristic equation

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k \lambda^{N-k} = 0
$$

Correspondence between characteristic roots and homogeneous solutions:

Characteristic root Form of the homogeneous solution
Simple real root $\lambda$ $C\lambda^n$
Real root $\lambda$ of multiplicity $r$ $(C_0 + C_1 n + \cdots + C_{r-1} n^{r-1})\lambda^n$
Complex-conjugate roots $\lambda = re^{\pm j\theta}$ $r^n [A\cos(n\theta) + B\sin(n\theta)]$

Particular solution: choose a form according to the input $x[n]$

Input form Form of the particular solution
Constant $A$ Constant $C$
$a^n$ $Ca^n$ (when $a$ is not a characteristic root)
$n^m$ $C_0 + C_1 n + \cdots + C_m n^m$

Example 2: Classical Solution

The system is $y[n] + 3y[n-1] + 2y[n-2] = x[n]$, its input is $x[n] = 2^n u[n]$, and its initial conditions are $y[-1] = 0, y[-2] = 0$. Find $y[n]$.

Solution:

Homogeneous solution: The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = 0$, with characteristic roots $\lambda_1 = -1, \lambda_2 = -2$.

The homogeneous solution is $y_h[n] = C_1(-1)^n + C_2(-2)^n$.

Particular solution: The input is $2^n$, and $2$ is not a characteristic root, so let $y_p[n] = K \cdot 2^n$.

Substitute this into the difference equation:

$$
K \cdot 2^n + 3K \cdot 2^{n-1} + 2K \cdot 2^{n-2} = 2^n
$$

$$
K\left(1 + \frac{3}{2} + \frac{2}{4}\right) = K\left(1 + 1.5 + 0.5\right) = 3K = 1
$$

$$
K = \frac{1}{3}
$$

Thus $y_p[n] = \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2^n$.

Complete solution:

$$
y[n] = C_1(-1)^n + C_2(-2)^n + \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2^n, \quad n \ge 0
$$

Use the initial conditions $y[-1]=0, y[-2]=0$ to determine the constants. For the zero-state response, we can use $y[0], y[1]$:

$$
y[0] = b_0 x[0] = 1
$$

$$
y[1] = -3y[0] + x[1] = -3 + 2 = -1
$$

Substitute these values:

$$
y[0] = C_1 + C_2 + \frac{1}{3} = 1 \Rightarrow C_1 + C_2 = \frac{2}{3}
$$

$$
y[1] = -C_1 - 2C_2 + \frac{2}{3} = -1 \Rightarrow -C_1 - 2C_2 = -\frac{5}{3}
$$

Solving gives $C_1 = 1$ and $C_2 = -\frac{1}{3}$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{y[n] = (-1)^n - \frac{1}{3}(-2)^n + \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2^n, \quad n \ge 0}
$$

5.6 Discrete Convolution Sum

5.6.1 Definition of the Convolution Sum

The discrete convolution sum is an operation on two discrete sequences:

$$
\boxed{y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] h[n-k]}
$$

5.6.2 Physical Meaning of the Convolution Sum

As with continuous convolution, any discrete signal can be decomposed into a superposition of shifted impulse sequences:

$$
x[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] \delta[n-k]
$$

The system output is:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] h[n-k]
$$

5.6.3 Graphical Calculation of a Convolution Sum

The steps are similar to those for continuous convolution:

Reverse: $h[k] \to h[-k]$
Shift: $h[-k] \to h[n-k]$ (shift right by $n$)
Multiply: $x[k] \cdot h[n-k]$
Sum: sum over all $k$

Example 3: Calculating a Convolution Sum

Given $x[n] = {1, 2, 3}$ (for $n=0,1,2$) and $h[n] = {1, 1}$ (for $n=0,1$), find $y[n] = x[n] * h[n]$.

Solution:

Use the definition directly:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k]h[n-k] = \sum_{k=0}^{2} x[k]h[n-k]
$$

Because $h[n] = \delta[n] + \delta[n-1]$, we may also use distributivity:

$$
y[n] = x[n] * (\delta[n] + \delta[n-1]) = x[n] + x[n-1]
$$

Therefore:

  • $y[0] = x[0] + x[-1] = 1 + 0 = 1$
  • $y[1] = x[1] + x[0] = 2 + 1 = 3$
  • $y[2] = x[2] + x[1] = 3 + 2 = 5$
  • $y[3] = x[3] + x[2] = 0 + 3 = 3$
  • $y[4] = x[4] + x[3] = 0 + 0 = 0$

Thus $y[n] = {1, 3, 5, 3}$ (for $n=0,1,2,3$).

Example 4: Graphical Calculation of a Convolution Sum

Let $x[n] = \begin{cases} 1, & 0 \le n \le 2 \ 0, & \text{其他} \end{cases}$ and $h[n] = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n u[n]$. Find $y[n] = x[n] * h[n]$.

Solution:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k] h[n-k] = \sum_{k=0}^{2} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-k} u[n-k]
$$

$u[n-k]$ requires $n-k \ge 0$, or $k \le n$.

Therefore, the summation range is for $k$ from $0$ to $\min(2, n)$.

Case 1: $n < 0$, so $y[n] = 0$

Case 2: $0 \le n \le 2$

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-k} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \sum_{k=0}^{n} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-k} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \sum_{k=0}^{n} 2^k
$$

$$
= \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \cdot \frac{2^{n+1} - 1}{2-1} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \cdot (2^{n+1} - 1) = 2 - \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n
$$

Case 3: $n \ge 2$

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{2} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-k} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \sum_{k=0}^{2} 2^k = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n (1+2+4) = 7\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{y[n] = \begin{cases} 0, & n < 0 \ 2 - (1/2)^n, & 0 \le n \le 2 \ 7(1/2)^n, & n \ge 2 \end{cases}}
$$

5.6.4 Properties of the Convolution Sum

These are exactly analogous to the properties of continuous convolution:

  1. Commutativity: $x[n] * h[n] = h[n] * x[n]$
  2. Distributivity: $x[n] * (h_1[n] + h_2[n]) = x[n] * h_1[n] + x[n] * h_2[n]$
  3. Associativity: $(x[n] * h_1[n]) * h_2[n] = x[n] * (h_1[n] * h_2[n])$
  4. Convolution with an impulse: $x[n] * \delta[n] = x[n]$, and $x[n] * \delta[n-n_0] = x[n-n_0]$

5.7 Impulse and Step Responses of Discrete Systems

5.7.1 Impulse Response $h[n]$

Definition: the system’s response to $\delta[n]$ under zero initial conditions.

$$
\delta[n] \to \boxed{\text{系统}} \to h[n]
$$

Relationship to the difference equation: When $x[n] = \delta[n]$, the solution of the difference equation is $h[n]$.

Example 5: Finding an Impulse Response

For the system $y[n] + \frac{1}{2} y[n-1] = x[n]$, find the impulse response.

Solution:

Let $x[n] = \delta[n]$ and $y[n] = h[n]$, with zero initial condition $h[-1] = 0$.

For $n \ge 0$, the difference equation is:

$$
h[n] + \frac{1}{2} h[n-1] = 0, \quad n \ge 1
$$

The homogeneous solution has the form $h[n] = C\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$.

From the initial condition at $n=0$, $h[0] + \frac{1}{2} h[-1] = \delta[0] = 1$, so $h[0] = 1$.

Substitution gives $C = 1$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{h[n] = \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^n u[n]}
$$

5.7.2 Step Response $s[n]$

Definition: the system’s response to $u[n]$ under zero initial conditions.

Relationship to the impulse response:

$$
s[n] = h[n] * u[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} h[k]
$$

5.8 Causality and Stability of Discrete Systems

5.8.1 Time-Domain Criterion for Causality

Theorem: A discrete LTI system is causal if and only if $h[n] = 0$ for $n < 0$.

Intuitive explanation: The output of a causal system cannot depend on future inputs.

Example 6: Determining Causality

(1) $h[n] = a^n u[n]$ → causal (zero for $n<0$)
(2) $h[n] = a^n u[n+1]$ → noncausal (nonzero at $n=-1$)
(3) $y[n] = x[n] + x[n+1]$ → noncausal (requires a future input)

5.8.2 Time-Domain Criterion for Stability

Theorem: A discrete LTI system is BIBO stable if and only if its impulse response is absolutely summable:

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |h[n]| < \infty}
$$

Example 7: Determining Stability

(1) $h[n] = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n u[n]$:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left|\frac{1}{2}\right|^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = \frac{1}{1-1/2} = 2 < \infty \quad \text{→ 稳定}
$$

(2) $h[n] = 2^n u[n]$:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^n = \infty \quad \text{→ 不稳定}
$$

(3) $h[n] = u[n]$:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 1 = \infty \quad \text{→ 不稳定}
$$

5.9 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What Is the Fundamental Difference Between a Discrete Signal $x[n]$ and a Continuous Signal $x(t)$?

A:

  • In $x(t)$, $t$ is a continuous real number; in $x[n]$, $n$ is an integer
  • Therefore, $x[1.5]$ is not defined! $n$ can only be an integer
  • A discrete signal is fundamentally a sequence (an ordered list of numbers), not a “function” over continuous time

Q2: Why Is the Frequency of a Discrete Complex Exponential Unique Only Within a Range of $2\pi$?

A: For a discrete signal:

$$
e^{j(\Omega + 2\pi)n} = e^{j\Omega n} \cdot e^{j2\pi n} = e^{j\Omega n} \cdot 1 = e^{j\Omega n}
$$

Therefore, the frequencies $\Omega$ and $\Omega + 2\pi$ are indistinguishable. In the digital world, the “highest frequency” is thus $f_s/2$ (corresponding to $\Omega = \pi$).

Q3: What Is the Relationship Between Difference Equations and Differential Equations?

A: Differential equations describe continuous systems, while difference equations describe discrete systems. Their relationship can be understood by approximating a derivative with a difference:

$$
\frac{dy(t)}{dt} \approx \frac{y[n] - y[n-1]}{T_s}
$$

Here $T_s$ is the sampling interval. A differential equation therefore becomes approximately a difference equation after sampling. If you have studied the Z-transform (Chapter 6), you can see that the two take very similar forms in their respective transform domains.

Q4: Are There Any Tricks for Calculating a Convolution Sum?

A:

  1. Finite-length sequences: use “long multiplication” (align corresponding terms, multiply them, and add the products, just as in ordinary multiplication)
  2. Infinite-length sequences: usually derive the result from the formula
  3. Use properties: if $h[n]$ is a simple combination of impulses, distributivity can greatly simplify the calculation

Q5: Is Causality Determined in the Same Way for Discrete and Continuous Systems?

A: Essentially yes, with one important distinction:

Continuous system: $h(t) = 0, t < 0$
Discrete system: $h[n] = 0, n < 0$

Both mean that “the output does not depend on future inputs.”

6.1 Definition of the Z-Transform

6.1.1 From the Laplace Transform to the Z-Transform

Recall that the Laplace transform of a continuous signal is $X(s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-st} dt$.

For a discrete signal $x[n]$, regard it as an impulse train:

$$
x_s(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \delta(t - nT_s)
$$

Its Laplace transform is:

$$
X_s(s) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] e^{-nsT_s}
$$

Let $z = e^{sT_s}$. This gives the Z-transform:

$$
\boxed{X(z) = \mathcal{Z}{x[n]} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] z^{-n}}
$$

Intuitive explanation: The Z-transform is the discrete signal’s “Laplace transform.” Just as the Laplace transform turns a continuous differential equation into an algebraic equation, the Z-transform turns a discrete difference equation into an algebraic equation.

6.1.2 Unilateral and Bilateral Z-Transforms

Bilateral Z-transform:

$$
X(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] z^{-n}
$$

Unilateral Z-transform (used primarily in this course):

$$
\boxed{X(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x[n] z^{-n}}
$$

6.1.3 Relationship Between the Z-Transform and the Laplace Transform

$$
z = e^{sT_s}
$$

where $T_s$ is the sampling period.

The mapping is illustrated below:

Intuitive explanation: The left half of the s-plane maps to the interior of the unit circle in the z-plane, while the imaginary axis in the s-plane maps to the unit circle in the z-plane. This mapping explains why the continuous-system stability criterion (poles in the left half-plane) corresponds to the discrete-system stability criterion (poles inside the unit circle).

6.2 Region of Convergence (ROC)

6.2.1 Definition of the ROC

The region of convergence of the Z-transform is the set of values of $z$ for which the series $\sum x[n]z^{-n}$ converges.

Convergence condition:

$$
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |x[n]| |z|^{-n} < \infty
$$

6.2.2 Graphical Representation of the ROC

The ROC is shown in the z-plane (whose horizontal axis is $\Re{z}$ and whose vertical axis is $\Im{z}$):

6.2.3 Properties of the ROC

  1. The ROC is an annular region in the z-plane (or the interior or exterior of a circle)
  2. The ROC contains no poles
  3. If $x[n]$ is right-sided ($x[n]=0$ for $n < N_0$), the ROC lies outside the pole with the largest magnitude
  4. If $x[n]$ is left-sided ($x[n]=0$ for $n > N_0$), the ROC lies inside the pole with the smallest magnitude
  5. If $x[n]$ is two-sided, the ROC is an annulus
  6. If $x[n]$ has finite length, the ROC is the entire z-plane (possibly excluding 0 or $\infty$)

6.4 Properties of the Z-Transform

6.4.1 Linearity

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{a x_1[n] + b x_2[n]} = a X_1(z) + b X_2(z)}
$$

ROC: the intersection of the two ROCs (although it may be larger).

6.4.2 Time Shifting (Very Important)

For the unilateral Z-transform:

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{x[n-k]} = z^{-k} X(z) + \sum_{m=0}^{k-1} x[m-k] z^{-m}}
$$

For a right-sided sequence ($x[n]=0$ for $n<0$):

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{x[n-k]} = z^{-k} X(z)}
$$

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{x[n+k]} = z^{k} X(z) - \sum_{m=0}^{k-1} x[m] z^{k-m}}
$$

Intuitive explanation: A time shift corresponds to multiplication by $z^{-k}$ (delay) or $z^{k}$ (advance) in the z-domain. This corresponds to $e^{-st_0}$ in the Laplace transform.

6.4.3 Scaling in the z-Domain

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{a^n x[n]} = X\left(\frac{z}{a}\right)}
$$

Intuitive explanation: Multiplying by $a^n$ in the time domain is equivalent to scaling the z variable in the z-domain.

Example 2: Using z-Domain Scaling

Given $\mathcal{Z}{u[n]} = \frac{1}{1-z^{-1}}$, $|z| > 1$, find $\mathcal{Z}{a^n u[n]}$.

Solution:

$$
\mathcal{Z}{a^n u[n]} = \frac{1}{1-(z/a)^{-1}} = \frac{1}{1-az^{-1}}, \quad |z| > |a|
$$

6.4.4 Convolution

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{Z}{x[n] * h[n]} = X(z) H(z)}
$$

This is the most important property of the Z-transform! Convolution in the time domain becomes multiplication in the z-domain.

6.4.5 Initial-Value Theorem

If $x[n]=0$ for $n<0$, then:

$$
\boxed{x[0] = \lim_{z \to \infty} X(z)}
$$

6.4.6 Final-Value Theorem

If the limit of $x[n]$ exists (that is, if the system is stable), then:

$$
\boxed{\lim_{n \to \infty} x[n] = \lim_{z \to 1} (1-z^{-1}) X(z)}
$$

Condition for use: Every pole of $(1-z^{-1})X(z)$ must lie inside the unit circle.

6.5 Inverse Z-Transform

6.5.1 Partial-Fraction Expansion

As with the inverse Laplace transform, expand $X(z)$ into a sum of simple terms, then use a transform table.

Basic approach:

  1. First write $X(z)$ as a rational function of $z^{-1}$
  2. Perform a partial-fraction expansion of $X(z)/z$ (or $X(z)$)
  3. Multiply by $z$ to obtain the expansion of $X(z)$
  4. Find the inverse transform of each term from a table

Example 3: Partial-Fraction Expansion

Find the inverse transform of $X(z) = \frac{1}{(1-z^{-1})(1-0.5z^{-1})}$ for $|z| > 1$.

Solution:

First write it as a rational function of $z$: $X(z) = \frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z-0.5)}$.

Expand $X(z)/z$ into partial fractions:

$$
\frac{X(z)}{z} = \frac{z}{(z-1)(z-0.5)} = \frac{A}{z-1} + \frac{B}{z-0.5}
$$

$$
A = \frac{z}{z-0.5}\Big|_{z=1} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2
$$

$$
B = \frac{z}{z-1}\Big|_{z=0.5} = \frac{0.5}{-0.5} = -1
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{X(z)}{z} = \frac{2}{z-1} - \frac{1}{z-0.5}
$$

$$
X(z) = \frac{2z}{z-1} - \frac{z}{z-0.5} = \frac{2}{1-z^{-1}} - \frac{1}{1-0.5z^{-1}}
$$

Because the ROC is $|z| > 1$, this corresponds to a right-sided (causal) sequence:

$$
\boxed{x[n] = (2 - (0.5)^n) u[n]}
$$

Example 4: Repeated Poles

Find the inverse transform of $X(z) = \frac{z^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})^2}$ for $|z| > 1$.

Solution:

$$
X(z) = \frac{z^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})^2}
$$

We know that $\mathcal{Z}{n u[n]} = \frac{z^{-1}}{(1-z^{-1})^2}$ for $|z| > 1$.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{x[n] = n u[n]}
$$

6.5.2 Power-Series Expansion (Long Division)

Expand $X(z)$ as a power series in $z^{-1}$; its coefficients are the samples $x[n]$.

$$
X(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x[n] z^{-n} = x[0] + x[1]z^{-1} + x[2]z^{-2} + \cdots
$$

Method: Perform long division on $X(z)$; the coefficient of each power of $z^{-1}$ is the corresponding $x[n]$.

Example 5: Long Division

Find the inverse transform of $X(z) = \frac{1}{1-0.5z^{-1}}$ for $|z| > 0.5$.

Solution:

Perform long division:

Thus $x[n] = {1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, \cdots} = (0.5)^n u[n]$.

Suitable for:

  • Finding the first few values
  • Not suitable for obtaining a closed-form expression

6.6 Solving Difference Equations with the Z-Transform

6.6.1 Solution Procedure

  1. Take the Z-transform of both sides of the difference equation (using the time-shifting property to include initial conditions)
  2. Solve the algebraic equation for $Y(z)$
  3. Expand $Y(z)$ into partial fractions
  4. Take the inverse Z-transform to obtain $y[n]$

Example 6: Solving a Difference Equation with the Z-Transform

The system is $y[n] + 3y[n-1] + 2y[n-2] = x[n]$, the initial conditions are $y[-1] = 0$ and $y[-2] = 0$, and the input is $x[n] = 2^n u[n]$. Find $y[n]$.

Solution:

Step 1: Take the Z-transform

Note that $y[-1]=y[-2]=0$ (zero state) and $x[n]$ is a causal sequence, so shifting a right-sided sequence simply multiplies its transform by $z^{-k}$.

$$
Y(z) + 3z^{-1}Y(z) + 2z^{-2}Y(z) = X(z)
$$

$$
(1 + 3z^{-1} + 2z^{-2})Y(z) = X(z)
$$

Step 2: Substitute $X(z)$

$$
X(z) = \mathcal{Z}{2^n u[n]} = \frac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
Y(z) = \frac{1}{(1+3z^{-1}+2z^{-2})(1-2z^{-1})} = \frac{1}{(1+z^{-1})(1+2z^{-1})(1-2z^{-1})}
$$

Step 3: Partial-fraction expansion

In terms of $z$, $Y(z) = \frac{z^3}{(z+1)(z+2)(z-2)}$.

Expand $Y(z)/z$:

$$
\frac{Y(z)}{z} = \frac{z^2}{(z+1)(z+2)(z-2)} = \frac{A}{z+1} + \frac{B}{z+2} + \frac{C}{z-2}
$$

$$
A = \frac{z^2}{(z+2)(z-2)}\Big|_{z=-1} = \frac{1}{(1)(-3)} = -\frac{1}{3}
$$

$$
B = \frac{z^2}{(z+1)(z-2)}\Big|_{z=-2} = \frac{4}{(-1)(-4)} = 1
$$

$$
C = \frac{z^2}{(z+1)(z+2)}\Big|_{z=2} = \frac{4}{(3)(4)} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{Y(z)}{z} = -\frac{1/3}{z+1} + \frac{1}{z+2} + \frac{1/3}{z-2}
$$

$$
Y(z) = -\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{z}{z+1} + \frac{z}{z+2} + \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{z}{z-2}
$$

$$
= -\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{1+z^{-1}} + \frac{1}{1+2z^{-1}} + \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}
$$

Step 4: Take the inverse transform

Because the system is causal (its ROC lies outside the pole of greatest magnitude, $|z| > 2$):

$$
\boxed{y[n] = \left[-\frac{1}{3}(-1)^n + (-2)^n + \frac{1}{3} \cdot 2^n\right] u[n]}
$$

Verification: This agrees with the result obtained using the classical method in Example 2 of Chapter 5!

6.6.2 Z-Transform Solution with Nonzero Initial Conditions

When the initial conditions $y[-1], y[-2]$ are nonzero, we must use the time-shifting properties that include the initial conditions:

$$
\mathcal{Z}{y[n-1]} = z^{-1}Y(z) + y[-1]
$$

$$
\mathcal{Z}{y[n-2]} = z^{-2}Y(z) + y[-2] + y[-1]z^{-1}
$$

Example 7: Nonzero Initial Condition

The system is $y[n] - \frac{1}{2} y[n-1] = x[n]$, its initial condition is $y[-1] = 1$, and its input is $x[n] = u[n]$. Find $y[n]$.

Solution:

Take the Z-transform:

$$
Y(z) - \frac{1}{2}[z^{-1}Y(z) + y[-1]] = X(z)
$$

$$
Y(z) - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}Y(z) - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{1-z^{-1}}
$$

$$
Y(z)\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}\right) = \frac{1}{1-z^{-1}} + \frac{1}{2}
$$

$$
Y(z) = \frac{\frac{1}{1-z^{-1}} + \frac{1}{2}}{1 - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}} = \frac{\frac{2 + (1-z^{-1})}{2(1-z^{-1})}}{1 - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}} = \frac{3 - z^{-1}}{2(1-z^{-1})(1-0.5z^{-1})}
$$

Expand:

$$
Y(z) = \frac{3 - z^{-1}}{2(1-z^{-1})(1-0.5z^{-1})}
$$

Let $w = z^{-1}$:

$$
Y(z) = \frac{3 - w}{2(1-w)(1-0.5w)} = \frac{A}{1-w} + \frac{B}{1-0.5w}
$$

$$
A = \frac{3-w}{2(1-0.5w)}\Big|_{w=1} = \frac{2}{2 \cdot 0.5} = 2
$$

$$
B = \frac{3-w}{2(1-w)}\Big|_{w=2} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot (-1)} = -\frac{1}{2}
$$

Therefore:

$$
Y(z) = \frac{2}{1-z^{-1}} - \frac{1/2}{1-0.5z^{-1}}
$$

Taking the inverse transform (for a causal sequence):

$$
\boxed{y[n] = \left(2 - \frac{1}{2}(0.5)^n\right) u[n] = (2 - (0.5)^{n+1}) u[n]}
$$

6.7 System Function $H(z)$ of a Discrete System

6.7.1 Definition of the System Function

For a discrete LTI system, the system function is defined as:

$$
\boxed{H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}}
$$

under zero initial conditions.

6.7.2 Relationship Between the System Function and Impulse Response

$$
\boxed{H(z) = \mathcal{Z}{h[n]}}
$$

$$
\boxed{h[n] = \mathcal{Z}^{-1}{H(z)}}
$$

6.7.3 Finding the System Function from a Difference Equation

Given the difference equation $\sum a_k y[n-k] = \sum b_k x[n-k]$, take the Z-transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
\left(\sum a_k z^{-k}\right) Y(z) = \left(\sum b_k z^{-k}\right) X(z)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k z^{-k}}}
$$

Example 8: Finding a System Function from a Difference Equation

For the system $y[n] + \frac{1}{2} y[n-1] = x[n] + 2x[n-1]$, find $H(z)$ and $h[n]$.

Solution:

Take the Z-transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
Y(z) + \frac{1}{2} z^{-1} Y(z) = X(z) + 2z^{-1} X(z)
$$

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{1 + 2z^{-1}}{1 + \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}} = \frac{z+2}{z+0.5}
$$

Expand $H(z)/z$:

$$
\frac{H(z)}{z} = \frac{z+2}{z(z+0.5)} = \frac{A}{z} + \frac{B}{z+0.5}
$$

$$
A = \frac{z+2}{z+0.5}\Big|_{z=0} = \frac{2}{0.5} = 4
$$

$$
B = \frac{z+2}{z}\Big|_{z=-0.5} = \frac{1.5}{-0.5} = -3
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{H(z)}{z} = \frac{4}{z} - \frac{3}{z+0.5}
$$

$$
H(z) = 4 - \frac{3z}{z+0.5} = 4 - \frac{3}{1+0.5z^{-1}}
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
h[n] = 4\delta[n] - 3(-0.5)^n u[n]
$$

6.8 Pole-Zero Analysis and Stability

6.8.1 Zeros and Poles

$$
H(z) = \frac{N(z)}{D(z)} = K \frac{\prod (z - z_i)}{\prod (z - p_i)}
$$

Zero $z_i$: a root of $N(z) = 0$
Pole $p_i$: a root of $D(z) = 0$

6.8.2 Stability Criterion

Necessary and sufficient condition for a causal discrete LTI system to be stable: every pole of $H(z)$ must lie inside the unit circle.

$$
\boxed{|p_i| < 1, \quad \forall i \iff \text{系统稳定}}
$$

Intuitive explanation:

  • Pole inside the unit circle → exponential decay of the impulse response → stable system
  • Pole outside the unit circle → exponential growth of the impulse response → unstable system
  • Pole on the unit circle → constant-amplitude oscillation of the impulse response → marginally stable system

Example 9: Stability of a Discrete System

Determine the stability of each system function (assuming causality):

(1) $H(z) = \frac{1}{(1-0.5z^{-1})(1+0.2z^{-1})}$

Poles: $z=0.5$ and $z=-0.2$, both inside the unit circle → stable

(2) $H(z) = \frac{1}{1-2z^{-1}}$

Pole: $z=2$, outside the unit circle → unstable

(3) $H(z) = \frac{1}{1+z^{-2}}$

Poles: $z = \pm j$, with $|z|=1$, on the unit circle → marginally stable

6.9 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What Exactly Is $z$, and How Is It Related to $s$?

A: $z$ is a complex variable, related to the Laplace-transform variable $s$ by $z = e^{sT_s}$.

The mapping can be understood intuitively as follows:

  • $s = 0$ (DC) → $z = 1$
  • $s = j\omega_s/2$ (Nyquist frequency) → $z = e^{j\pi} = -1$
  • Left half of the $s$-plane → interior of the unit circle in the $z$-plane
  • Imaginary axis in the $s$-plane → unit circle in the $z$-plane

Q2: Why Is the ROC of a Z-Transform Always Circular or Annular?

A: Because the convergence condition depends on $|z|$, not on the angle of $z$:

$$
\sum |x[n]| |z|^{-n} < \infty
$$

$|z|$ is the radial coordinate, so the region of convergence is circular or annular in polar coordinates.

Q3: Why Is the Partial-Fraction Expansion Sometimes Applied to $X(z)$ and Sometimes to $X(z)/z$?

A: Either approach is valid. The difference is:

  • Expanding $X(z)/z$: the standard form $\frac{z}{z-p}$ in $X(z)$ is easy to obtain from $\frac{1}{z-p}$
  • Expanding $X(z)$ directly: if $X(z)$ is written as a polynomial in $z^{-1}$, it can be expanded directly

Recommendation: Expand $X(z)/z$ to reduce the chance of mistakes.

Q4: When Is Long Division Used?

A: Long division is suitable when:

  • Only the first few values of $x[n]$ are required (rather than a closed-form solution)
  • You want to verify a partial-fraction expansion
  • You are implementing the calculation in a program

Its disadvantage is that it usually does not produce a closed-form expression.

Q5: What Advantages Does the Z-Transform Have over the Classical Method for Solving Difference Equations?

A: The advantages of the Z-transform are:

  1. It automatically handles initial conditions (there is no need to separate the zero-input and zero-state responses)
  2. Every step is algebraic
  3. The system function $H(z)$ provides a unified framework for analysis

Just as the Laplace transform does for differential equations, the Z-transform makes the solution of difference equations systematic and procedural.

Q6: How Does Stability Differ Between Discrete and Continuous Systems?

A:

Continuous system Discrete system
Stable region Left half of the s-plane Inside the unit circle
Criterion $\Re{p_i} < 0$ $vert p_i
vert < 1$

This difference comes from the mapping $z = e^{sT_s}$: the left half of the s-plane maps to the interior of the unit circle in the z-plane.

7.1 Review of the System Function of a Continuous System

7.1.1 Definition of the System Function

For a continuous LTI system, the system function (transfer function) is defined as:

$$
H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{X(s)} = \mathcal{L}{h(t)}
$$

Physical meaning: $H(s)$ describes how the system transforms the complex exponential signal $e^{st}$.

7.1.2 General Form of a System Function

$$
H(s) = \frac{b_M s^M + b_{M-1} s^{M-1} + \cdots + b_0}{s^N + a_{N-1} s^{N-1} + \cdots + a_0}
$$

Zeros: roots of the numerator polynomial $N(s) = 0$
Poles: roots of the denominator polynomial $D(s) = 0$

7.1.3 From the System Function to the Frequency Response

For a stable system, the frequency response equals the system function evaluated at $s = j\omega$:

$$
H(j\omega) = H(s)\big|_{s=j\omega}
$$

7.2 Review of the System Function of a Discrete System

7.2.1 Definition of the System Function

For a discrete LTI system:

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \mathcal{Z}{h[n]}
$$

7.2.2 General Form

$$
H(z) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k z^{-k}}
$$

Zeros: roots of the numerator polynomial $N(z) = 0$
Poles: roots of the denominator polynomial $D(z) = 0$

7.3 Pole-Zero Analysis

7.3.1 Pole-Zero Plot

A pole-zero plot marks the locations of zeros and poles in the complex plane:

  • For $H(s)$, use the s-plane: horizontal axis $\Re{s}$ and vertical axis $\Im{s}$
  • For $H(z)$, use the z-plane: horizontal axis $\Re{z}$ and vertical axis $\Im{z}$
  • “o” denotes a zero, while “x” denotes a pole

Example pole-zero plot for a continuous system:

For $H(s) = \frac{s+1}{(s+2)(s+3)}$, the zero is at $s = -1$, and the poles are at $s = -2, -3$.

Example pole-zero plot for a discrete system:

For $H(z) = \frac{1}{1-0.5z^{-1}}$, the pole is at $z = 0.5$.

7.3.2 Estimating Frequency Response from a Pole-Zero Plot

Continuous system:

$$
H(j\omega) = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} (j\omega - z_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} (j\omega - p_i)}
$$

$$
|H(j\omega)| = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} |j\omega - z_i|}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} |j\omega - p_i|}
$$

$$
\angle H(j\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \angle(j\omega - z_i) - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \angle(j\omega - p_i)
$$

Geometric explanation: The lengths of the line segments from a point on the $j\omega$ axis to each zero and pole determine the magnitude, while their angles determine the phase.

Intuitive explanation:

  • $|j\omega - p_i|$: the length of the vector from pole $p_i$ to point $j\omega$ on the imaginary axis
  • When $\omega$ approaches the imaginary-axis position corresponding to a pole, the denominator becomes smaller and $|H|$ increases (a resonance peak)
  • When $\omega$ approaches the imaginary-axis position corresponding to a zero, the numerator becomes smaller and $|H|$ decreases (a dip)

Example 1: Estimating Frequency Response from Poles and Zeros

The system function is $H(s) = \frac{s+1}{(s+2)^2 + 9}$, with poles at $s = -2 \pm j3$ and a zero at $s = -1$.

Analysis:

  • The poles lie at $s = -2 \pm j3$, with negative real parts → the system is stable
  • The poles have imaginary parts $\pm 3$ → there is a resonance peak near $\omega \approx 3$
  • Because the poles are relatively far from the imaginary axis ($\Re{p} = -2$), the resonance peak is broad and flat

Example 2: Frequency Response of a Discrete System

The system function is $H(z) = \frac{1}{1-0.9z^{-1}}$ (a first-order low-pass filter). Find its frequency response.

Solution:

The frequency response is $H(e^{j\Omega}) = \frac{1}{1-0.9e^{-j\Omega}}$.

The magnitude response is:

$$
|H(e^{j\Omega})| = \frac{1}{|1-0.9e^{-j\Omega}|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.81 - 1.8\cos(\Omega)}}
$$

  • At $\Omega = 0$ (DC): $|H(e^{j0})| = \frac{1}{0.1} = 10$
  • At $\Omega = \pi$ (the highest frequency): $|H(e^{j\pi})| = \frac{1}{1.9} \approx 0.53$

This is a low-pass filter: it amplifies DC and attenuates high frequencies.

7.4 Relationship Between System Stability and Poles and Zeros

7.4.1 Stability Criterion for a Continuous System

Necessary and sufficient condition for a causal continuous LTI system to be stable: every pole must lie in the left half of the s-plane.

$$
\boxed{\Re{p_i} < 0, \quad \forall i}
$$

Equivalent statements:

  1. The system’s impulse response $h(t)$ is absolutely integrable: $\int_0^\infty |h(t)|dt < \infty$
  2. Every pole of the system function $H(s)$ satisfies $\Re{p_i} < 0$
  3. The system’s frequency response $H(j\omega)$ exists (it is finite for every $\omega$)

7.4.2 Stability Criterion for a Discrete System

Necessary and sufficient condition for a causal discrete LTI system to be stable: every pole must lie inside the unit circle in the z-plane.

$$
\boxed{|p_i| < 1, \quad \forall i}
$$

Equivalent statements:

  1. The impulse response $h[n]$ is absolutely summable: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |h[n]| < \infty$
  2. Every pole of the system function $H(z)$ satisfies $|p_i| < 1$
  3. The system’s frequency response $H(e^{j\Omega})$ exists

7.4.3 Routh-Hurwitz Stability Criterion

For a high-order continuous system, stability can be determined by the Routh-Hurwitz criterion without finding the poles explicitly.

Method:

  1. Write the characteristic polynomial $D(s) = a_N s^N + a_{N-1} s^{N-1} + \cdots + a_0$
  2. Construct the Routh array
  3. Check the sign changes in its first column

Criterion: If every entry in the first column of the Routh array is positive (or every entry is negative), the system is stable. The number of sign changes in the first column equals the number of poles in the right half-plane.

Example 3: Routh Criterion

Determine the stability of the system whose characteristic polynomial is $D(s) = s^4 + 2s^3 + 3s^2 + 4s + 5$.

Solution:

Construct the Routh array:

The first column is $1, 2, 1, -6, 5$. There are sign changes ($1 \to -6$ and $-6 \to 5$), so the system is unstable and has two poles in the right half-plane.

7.4.4 Jury Stability Criterion (Discrete Systems)

The Jury criterion determines whether all roots of the characteristic polynomial $D(z)$ of a discrete system lie inside the unit circle.

Necessary conditions:

  1. $D(1) > 0$
  2. $(-1)^N D(-1) > 0$
  3. $|a_0| < a_N$

For a low-order system, the roots can instead be found directly, or these conditions can be applied.

7.5 State-Variable Description

7.5.1 Why Are State Variables Needed?

The previous analysis methods (differential equations and system functions) are all input-output descriptions concerned only with a system’s external characteristics. However, they:

  1. Describe only the relationship between input and output, not the system’s interior
  2. Cannot handle multiple-input, multiple-output (MIMO) systems
  3. Cannot handle the effects of initial conditions (which lie outside the system function)

A state-variable description, by contrast, completely describes a system’s behavior through a set of internal variables (state variables).

7.5.2 Definition of State

State: The state of a system at any time $t_0$ is the minimum set of information that, together with the input, uniquely determines the system’s future behavior.

Intuitive explanation: The state is the system’s “memory” at a particular time—given the state and input, we can calculate its future output.

7.5.3 State Equation and Output Equation

A state-variable description consists of two equations:

State equation (describing how the state changes over time):

Continuous system:

$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)}
$$

Discrete system:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{x}[n+1] = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}[n] + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}[n]}
$$

Output equation (describing the relationship between the output, state, and input):

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t)}
$$

where:

  • $\mathbf{x}(t)$: state vector ($N \times 1$)
  • $\mathbf{u}(t)$: input vector ($P \times 1$)
  • $\mathbf{y}(t)$: output vector ($Q \times 1$)
  • $\mathbf{A}$: system matrix ($N \times N$)
  • $\mathbf{B}$: input matrix ($N \times P$)
  • $\mathbf{C}$: output matrix ($Q \times N$)
  • $\mathbf{D}$: feedthrough matrix ($Q \times P$)

7.5.4 Choosing State Variables

State variables are usually chosen as the physical quantities associated with the system’s energy-storage elements:

System type State variables
Circuit Capacitor voltage and inductor current
Mechanical system Position and velocity
Thermal system Temperature

For a SISO (single-input, single-output) system described by a differential equation, the state variables are usually chosen as the output and its successive derivatives.

Example 4: Formulating State Equations

A system is described by the differential equation $y’’(t) + 3y’(t) + 2y(t) = x(t)$. Formulate its state equations.

Solution:

Choose the state variables:

$$
x_1(t) = y(t), \quad x_2(t) = y’(t)
$$

Then:

$$
\dot{x}_1(t) = x_2(t)
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2(t) = y’’(t) = -3y’(t) - 2y(t) + x(t) = -3x_2(t) - 2x_1(t) + x(t)
$$

In matrix form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1 \ \dot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} x(t)
$$

The output equation is:

$$
y(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + [0] x(t)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{D} = [0]
$$

Example 5: State Equations with an Input Derivative

For the system $y’’(t) + 3y’(t) + 2y(t) = x’(t) + 2x(t)$, formulate the state equations.

Solution:

When the right-hand side of the equation contains an input derivative, we cannot simply choose $y$ and its derivatives as the state variables. We must use the phase-variable method.

Let the state variables be:

$$
x_1 = y - \beta_0 u
$$

$$
x_2 = \dot{x}_1 - \beta_1 u = \dot{y} - \beta_0 \dot{u} - \beta_1 u
$$

where the $\beta$ coefficients are determined by the following relationship:

For $y’’ + a_1 y’ + a_0 y = b_1 u’ + b_0 u$:

$$
\beta_0 = b_0 - a_0 \beta_0 \quad \text{???}
$$

A more standard method is as follows.

The system function is $H(s) = \frac{s+2}{s^2+3s+2}$.

Let the intermediate variable $w(t)$ satisfy $w’’ + 3w’ + 2w = x(t)$; then $y(t) = w’(t) + 2w(t)$.

Choose the state variables:

$$
x_1 = w, \quad x_2 = w’
$$

Then:

$$
\dot{x}_1 = x_2
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2 = w’’ = -3w’ - 2w + x = -3x_2 - 2x_1 + x
$$

$$
y = x_2 + 2x_1
$$

In matrix form:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x}_1 \ \dot{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 1 \end{bmatrix} x
$$

$$
y = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \ x_2 \end{bmatrix} + [0] x
$$

7.6 State-Transition Matrix

7.6.1 Definition of the State-Transition Matrix

For the continuous system $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$, the state-transition matrix is defined as:

$$
\boxed{\Phi(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}}
$$

where $e^{\mathbf{A}t}$ is a matrix exponential.

7.6.2 Physical Meaning of the State-Transition Matrix

$\Phi(t)$ transfers the initial state $\mathbf{x}(0)$ to the state at time $t$ when the input is zero:

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \Phi(t) \mathbf{x}(0)
$$

Intuitive explanation: The state-transition matrix describes how a system’s state evolves from its initial state when there is no external input.

7.6.3 Methods for Calculating the State-Transition Matrix

Method 1: Laplace-transform method

$$
\boxed{\Phi(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathcal{L}^{-1}{(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}}}
$$

Method 2: Power-series method

$$
e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{A}t + \frac{\mathbf{A}^2 t^2}{2!} + \frac{\mathbf{A}^3 t^3}{3!} + \cdots
$$

Example 6: Finding a State-Transition Matrix

Given $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, find the state-transition matrix $\Phi(t)$.

Solution:

Calculate $(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$:

$$
s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 \ 0 & s \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \ 2 & s+3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Find its inverse:

$$
(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \frac{1}{s(s+3) + 2} \begin{bmatrix} s+3 & 1 \ -2 & s \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} \begin{bmatrix} s+3 & 1 \ -2 & s \end{bmatrix}
$$

Partial-fraction expansion:

$$
\frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

Therefore:

$$
(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} & \frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} \ \frac{-2}{(s+1)(s+2)} & \frac{s}{(s+1)(s+2)} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Expand each entry into partial fractions:

$$
\frac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{2}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{1}{s+1} - \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

$$
\frac{-2}{(s+1)(s+2)} = -\frac{2}{s+1} + \frac{2}{s+2}
$$

$$
\frac{s}{(s+1)(s+2)} = -\frac{1}{s+1} + \frac{2}{s+2}
$$

Taking the inverse transform gives:

$$
\Phi(t) = \begin{bmatrix} 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t} & e^{-t} - e^{-2t} \ -2e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t} & -e^{-t} + 2e^{-2t} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Verification: $\Phi(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 2-1 & 1-1 \ -2+2 & -1+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}$ ✓

7.6.4 Solution of the State Equation

Continuous system:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{x}(t) = e^{\mathbf{A}t}\mathbf{x}(0) + \int_0^t e^{\mathbf{A}(t-\tau)}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(\tau) d\tau}
$$

Zero-input component + zero-state component

Discrete system:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf{x}[n] = \mathbf{A}^n \mathbf{x}[0] + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \mathbf{A}^{n-1-k} \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}[k]}
$$

7.7 Controllability and Observability

7.7.1 Controllability

Definition: A system is controllable if there exists an unconstrained input $\mathbf{u}(t)$ that can transfer the system from any initial state to any desired state in finite time.

Intuitive explanation: Can we make the system’s state take any desired value by manipulating its control input?

Controllability criterion: A necessary and sufficient condition for an $N$th-order system to be controllable is for its controllability matrix to have full rank.

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{AB} & \mathbf{A}^2\mathbf{B} & \cdots & \mathbf{A}^{N-1}\mathbf{B} \end{bmatrix}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = N}
$$

7.7.2 Observability

Definition: A system is observable if its initial state can be determined uniquely from the output $\mathbf{y}(t)$ over a finite interval.

Intuitive explanation: Can we determine the system’s internal state by observing its output?

Observability criterion: A necessary and sufficient condition for an $N$th-order system to be observable is for its observability matrix to have full rank.

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \ \mathbf{CA} \ \mathbf{CA}^2 \ \vdots \ \mathbf{CA}^{N-1} \end{bmatrix}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{rank}(\mathcal{O}) = N}
$$

Example 7: Determining Controllability and Observability

The system has $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Controllability:

$$
\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{AB} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$\det(\mathcal{C}) = 0 \cdot (-3) - 1 \cdot 1 = -1 \neq 0$, so $\text{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = 2 = N$ and the system is controllable.

Observability:

$$
\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{C} \ \mathbf{CA} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$\det(\mathcal{O}) = 1 \neq 0$, so $\text{rank}(\mathcal{O}) = 2 = N$ and the system is observable.

Example 8: An Uncontrollable System

For $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (no input), the system is clearly uncontrollable.

A subtler example is $\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \ 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

$$
\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B} & \mathbf{AB} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$\text{rank}(\mathcal{C}) = 1 < 2$, so the system is uncontrollable. The reason is that the input can affect only the first state variable; it cannot control the second.

7.8 Relationship Between the System Function and the State Equations

7.8.1 Finding the System Function from the State Equations

Take the Laplace transforms of the state and output equations under zero initial conditions:

$$
s\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{B}U(s)
$$

$$
Y(s) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}(s) + \mathbf{D}U(s)
$$

Solve the first equation for $\mathbf{X}(s)$:

$$
(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B}U(s) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{X}(s) = (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}U(s)
$$

Substitute this into the output equation:

$$
Y(s) = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B}U(s) + \mathbf{D}U(s)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{H(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \mathbf{C}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{D}}
$$

7.8.2 Relationship Between Poles and Eigenvalues

The expression for the system function shows that:

$$
H(s) = \frac{\mathbf{C} \cdot \text{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) \cdot \mathbf{B}}{\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})} + \mathbf{D}
$$

The system’s poles are the eigenvalues of matrix $\mathbf{A}$ (possibly with pole-zero cancellations).

Proof:

  • The system’s poles are the roots of $D(s) = \det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$
  • $\det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = 0$ is precisely the characteristic equation of $\mathbf{A}$
  • Therefore, the poles are the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$

7.9 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: What Is the Difference Between a State Variable and an Output?

A:

  • State variables: describe the system’s internal state (its “memory”); there may be many of them
  • Output: the quantity you can observe; there are usually fewer outputs than state variables

For example, in an RLC circuit, the capacitor voltage and inductor current are state variables (they describe the system’s interior, though they may not be measured directly), while the total voltage is an output (and can be measured directly).

Q2: Why Does a System Function Sometimes Fail to Describe a System Completely?

A: A system function loses some information when pole-zero cancellation occurs. For example:

$$
H(s) = \frac{(s+1)}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{1}{s+2}
$$

From the input-output viewpoint, this appears to be a first-order system, but its interior may actually contain second-order dynamics (the canceled pole corresponds to an uncontrollable or unobservable mode). The state-variable method can describe this situation completely.

Q3: What Is the Practical Significance of Controllability and Observability?

A:

Concept Practical meaning Example
Controllable I can control every part of the system The volume of every loudspeaker can be adjusted
Uncontrollable Some part cannot be controlled One loudspeaker is broken, so its volume cannot be adjusted
Observable I can determine the system’s internal state The dashboard shows the condition of the engine
Unobservable I do not know what is happening inside The dashboard is broken, so the engine’s condition is unknown

Q4: How Is a Matrix Exponential Calculated?

A: Three common methods are:

  1. Laplace transform: $\Phi(t) = \mathcal{L}^{-1}{(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1}}$
  2. Diagonalization: if $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$, then $e^{\mathbf{A}t} = \mathbf{P}e^{\mathbf{\Lambda}t}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$
  3. Cayley-Hamilton theorem: calculate it using a finite power series

Q5: What Properties Does the State-Transition Matrix Have?

A:

  1. $\Phi(0) = \mathbf{I}$
  2. $\Phi(t_2 - t_0) = \Phi(t_2 - t_1)\Phi(t_1 - t_0)$ (the semigroup property)
  3. $\Phi^{-1}(t) = \Phi(-t)$ (invertibility)
  4. $\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(t) = \mathbf{A}\Phi(t) = \Phi(t)\mathbf{A}$

8.1 Relationship Between the Z-Transform and the Laplace Transform

8.1.1 Mapping from the s-Plane to the z-Plane

Recall the definition $z = e^{sT_s}$, where $T_s$ is the sampling period.

Let $s = \sigma + j\omega$. Then:

$$
z = e^{(\sigma + j\omega)T_s} = e^{\sigma T_s} \cdot e^{j\omega T_s}
$$

Therefore:

  • $|z| = e^{\sigma T_s}$ (the radius depends on $\sigma$)
  • $\angle z = \omega T_s$ (the argument depends on $\omega$)

8.1.2 Mapping Relationships from the s-Plane to the z-Plane

Important observation: A horizontal strip of width $\omega_s = 2\pi/T_s$ in the s-plane maps to the entire z-plane. This means that infinitely many horizontal strips in the s-plane all map to the same z-plane—a many-to-one mapping.

Intuitive explanation: The s-plane is like a building of infinite height, while the z-plane is like its ground floor—every floor (a strip of height $\omega_s$) is projected onto the same ground. This is why a discrete signal’s frequency is unique only within a range of $2\pi$ (corresponding to $\omega_s$).

8.1.3 Mapping from a Continuous System to a Discrete System

If a continuous system has system function $H_c(s)$, then after sampling, the discrete system’s system function can be obtained using impulse invariance:

$$
H(z) = \mathcal{Z}{h_c(nT_s)}
$$

where $h_c(t)$ is the continuous system’s impulse response.

8.2 System Function of a Discrete System

8.2.1 Review of the System Function’s Definition

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k z^{-k}}
$$

System function and impulse response:

$$
H(z) = \mathcal{Z}{h[n]}, \quad h[n] = \mathcal{Z}^{-1}{H(z)}
$$

8.2.2 Zeros, Poles, and ROC of the System Function

$$
H(z) = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} (1 - z_i z^{-1})}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} (1 - p_i z^{-1})}
$$

Or, equivalently, in polynomial form in $z$:

$$
H(z) = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} (z - z_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} (z - p_i)} \cdot z^{N-M}
$$

ROC of a causal system: $|z| > \max_i |p_i|$ (the region outside the circle whose radius is the largest pole magnitude).

8.2.3 From a Difference Equation to a System Function

Given the difference equation:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k y[n-k] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k x[n-k]
$$

Take the Z-transform under zero initial conditions:

$$
\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k z^{-k}\right) Y(z) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}\right) X(z)
$$

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k z^{-k}}
$$

Example 1: From a System Function to a Difference Equation

Given the system function $H(z) = \frac{1 + 2z^{-1}}{1 - 0.5z^{-1} + 0.2z^{-2}}$, write its difference equation.

Solution:

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{1 + 2z^{-1}}{1 - 0.5z^{-1} + 0.2z^{-2}}
$$

Cross-multiply:

$$
Y(z)(1 - 0.5z^{-1} + 0.2z^{-2}) = X(z)(1 + 2z^{-1})
$$

Taking the inverse transform:

$$
\boxed{y[n] - 0.5y[n-1] + 0.2y[n-2] = x[n] + 2x[n-1]}
$$

8.3 Frequency Response $H(e^{j\Omega})$

8.3.1 Definition of Frequency Response

For a stable discrete system, the frequency response is defined as:

$$
\boxed{H(e^{j\Omega}) = H(z)\big|_{z = e^{j\Omega}}}
$$

Physical meaning: When the system input is $e^{j\Omega n}$, the output is $H(e^{j\Omega}) e^{j\Omega n}$.

8.3.2 Calculating the Frequency Response

Given the system function $H(z)$, let $z = e^{j\Omega}$:

$$
H(e^{j\Omega}) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k e^{-jk\Omega}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k e^{-jk\Omega}}
$$

Magnitude response: $|H(e^{j\Omega})|$
Phase response: $\angle H(e^{j\Omega})$

8.3.3 Periodicity of the Frequency Response

The frequency response of a discrete system is periodic in $\Omega$, with period $2\pi$:

$$
H(e^{j(\Omega+2\pi)}) = H(e^{j\Omega})
$$

This is why we usually consider the frequency response only over $\Omega \in [-\pi, \pi]$ or $\Omega \in [0, 2\pi]$.

Intuitive explanation: In a discrete system, the frequencies $\Omega$ and $\Omega + 2\pi$ are indistinguishable—just as multiple strips of the s-plane map to the same z-plane.

Example 2: Frequency Response of a First-Order System

Find the frequency response of the system with $H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5z^{-1}}$.

Solution:

$$
H(e^{j\Omega}) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5e^{-j\Omega}} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5\cos\Omega + j0.5\sin\Omega}
$$

Magnitude response:

$$
|H(e^{j\Omega})| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-0.5\cos\Omega)^2 + (0.5\sin\Omega)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.25 - \cos\Omega}}
$$

Phase response:

$$
\angle H(e^{j\Omega}) = -\arctan\left(\frac{0.5\sin\Omega}{1 - 0.5\cos\Omega}\right)
$$

  • At $\Omega = 0$: $|H(e^{j0})| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.25 - 1}} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2$, with phase $0$
  • At $\Omega = \pi$: $|H(e^{j\pi})| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.25 - (-1)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2.25}} \approx 0.67$, with phase $\arctan(0) = 0$

This is a low-pass filter.

8.3.4 Estimating Frequency Response from a Pole-Zero Plot

As with a continuous system, the frequency response of a discrete system can be estimated from its pole-zero plot:

$$
|H(e^{j\Omega})| = K \frac{\prod_{i=1}^{M} |e^{j\Omega} - z_i|}{\prod_{i=1}^{N} |e^{j\Omega} - p_i|}
$$

Geometric explanation: $e^{j\Omega}$ is a point on the unit circle. The lengths of the vectors from each zero or pole to that point determine the magnitude.

Key observations:

  • A pole close to the unit circle produces a resonance peak near that frequency
  • A zero close to the unit circle produces a dip near that frequency
  • A zero on the unit circle completely blocks transmission at that frequency (a notch)

8.4 Analyzing Discrete Systems with the Z-Transform

8.4.1 Analysis of the System Response

Total system response:

$$
y[n] = \underbrace{y_{zi}[n]}{\text{零输入响应}} + \underbrace{y{zs}[n]}_{\text{零状态响应}}
$$

The Z-transform method can find both components at once by using the time-shifting properties that include initial conditions.

8.4.2 Steady-State Response of a System

For a stable system with sinusoidal input $x[n] = \cos(\Omega_0 n)$, the steady-state output is:

$$
y_{ss}[n] = |H(e^{j\Omega_0})| \cos(\Omega_0 n + \angle H(e^{j\Omega_0}))
$$

Intuitive explanation: Just as in a continuous system, the steady-state output of a discrete LTI system in response to a sinusoidal input is a sinusoid at the same frequency; its amplitude and phase are determined by the frequency response.

Example 3: Sinusoidal Steady-State Response

The system is $y[n] - 0.5y[n-1] = x[n]$, and its input is $x[n] = \cos(\pi n/2)$. Find the steady-state output.

Solution:

The system function is $H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5z^{-1}}$.

The frequency response is $H(e^{j\Omega}) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5e^{-j\Omega}}$.

At $\Omega = \pi/2$:

$$
H(e^{j\pi/2}) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.5e^{-j\pi/2}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.5j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1.25}} e^{-j\arctan(0.5)}
$$

$$
= 0.894 e^{-j26.6^\circ}
$$

Therefore, the steady-state output is:

$$
\boxed{y_{ss}[n] = 0.894 \cos\left(\frac{\pi n}{2} - 26.6^\circ\right)}
$$

8.5 Stability Criteria for Discrete Systems

8.5.1 Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Stability

Necessary and sufficient condition for a causal discrete LTI system to be stable:

$$
\boxed{\text{所有极点 } p_i \text{ 满足 } |p_i| < 1}
$$

Equivalent form: The ROC of the system function $H(z)$ contains the unit circle.

8.5.2 Determining Stability from the System Function

For a causal system, every root of the denominator polynomial $D(z)$ of $H(z)$ must have magnitude less than 1.

First-Order System

For $H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1}}$, the pole is at $z = -a_1$.

The stability condition is $|a_1| < 1$.

Second-Order System

$H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}$

The stability conditions (from the Jury criterion) are:

$$
|a_2| < 1
$$

$$
|a_1| < 1 + a_2
$$

Example 5: Determining Stability

Determine whether $H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - 1.5z^{-1} + 0.5z^{-2}}$ is stable, assuming the system is causal.

Solution:

The poles are the roots of $z^2 - 1.5z + 0.5 = 0$.

$$
z = \frac{1.5 \pm \sqrt{2.25 - 2}}{2} = \frac{1.5 \pm 0.5}{2}
$$

$p_1 = 1$ and $p_2 = 0.5$.

$|p_1| = 1$ (on the unit circle), while $|p_2| = 0.5 < 1$.

Because pole $p_1 = 1$ lies on the unit circle, the system is marginally stable.

8.6 Common Questions from Beginners

Q1: Why Is a Discrete Frequency $\Omega$ Meaningful Only over $[-\pi, \pi]$?

A: Because $\Omega$ and $\Omega + 2\pi$ correspond to the same position $z = e^{j\Omega}$ on the unit circle. The “highest frequency” in a discrete signal is therefore $\Omega = \pi$ (corresponding to the continuous frequency $f_s/2$).

Q2: How Can Knowledge of Continuous Systems Be Applied to Discrete Systems?

A: Use the following correspondences:

Continuous Discrete
$s$ (differential operator) $z$ (shift operator)
$H(s)$ $H(z)$
$H(j\omega)$ $H(e^{j\Omega})$
Stable in the left half-plane Stable inside the unit circle
Convolution integral Convolution sum
Differential equation Difference equation
Laplace transform Z-transform

Q3: How Does Pole-Zero Analysis Differ Between Discrete and Continuous Systems?

A:

  • Continuous system: the stability boundary is the imaginary axis (a vertical line), and the frequency response is read along the imaginary axis
  • Discrete system: the stability boundary is the unit circle, and the frequency response is read around the unit circle

Their geometric interpretations differ, but their methods of analysis are entirely analogous.

Q4: Why Is the Frequency Response of a Discrete System Periodic?

A: Because $e^{j\Omega}$ in $H(e^{j\Omega})$ is periodic in $\Omega$, with period $2\pi$:

$$
H(e^{j(\Omega+2\pi)}) = H(e^{j\Omega} e^{j2\pi}) = H(e^{j\Omega})
$$

This property is unique to discrete systems; the frequency response $H(j\omega)$ of a continuous system is not periodic.

Q5: Does Frequency Response Still Have Meaning If a System Is Unstable?

A: No. The output of an unstable system diverges, so no steady state exists. Although the expression for $H(e^{j\Omega})$ can still be “calculated” by substituting $z = e^{j\Omega}$, it no longer has the physical meaning of a “frequency response.”

8.8 Comprehensive Example

Example 6: Complete System Analysis

A discrete LTI system is described by the difference equation:

$$
y[n] - \frac{1}{2}y[n-1] + \frac{1}{4}y[n-2] = x[n] + x[n-1]
$$

(1) Find the system function $H(z)$
(2) Determine system stability
(3) Find the values of the frequency response $H(e^{j\Omega})$ at $\Omega=0$ and $\Omega=\pi$
(4) Find the impulse response $h[n]$

Solution:

(1) Take the Z-transform:

$$
Y(z) - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1}Y(z) + \frac{1}{4}z^{-2}Y(z) = X(z) + z^{-1}X(z)
$$

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{1 + z^{-1}}{1 - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1} + \frac{1}{4}z^{-2}}
$$

(2) The poles are the roots of $z^2 - \frac{1}{2}z + \frac{1}{4} = 0$.

$$
z = \frac{0.5 \pm \sqrt{0.25 - 1}}{2} = \frac{0.5 \pm j\sqrt{0.75}}{2} = 0.25 \pm j0.433
$$

$$
|p| = \sqrt{0.25^2 + 0.433^2} = \sqrt{0.0625 + 0.1875} = \sqrt{0.25} = 0.5 < 1
$$

Therefore, the system is stable.

(3) The frequency response is:

$$
H(e^{j\Omega}) = \frac{1 + e^{-j\Omega}}{1 - \frac{1}{2}e^{-j\Omega} + \frac{1}{4}e^{-j2\Omega}}
$$

At $\Omega = 0$:

$$
H(e^{j0}) = \frac{1 + 1}{1 - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{2}{\frac{3}{4}} = \frac{8}{3}
$$

At $\Omega = \pi$:

$$
H(e^{j\pi}) = \frac{1 - 1}{1 + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{0}{\frac{7}{4}} = 0
$$

The system therefore has a gain of $8/3$ at DC ($\Omega=0$) and a gain of 0 at the highest frequency ($\Omega=\pi$)—it is a low-pass filter.

(4) The impulse response:

$$
H(z) = \frac{1 + z^{-1}}{1 - \frac{1}{2}z^{-1} + \frac{1}{4}z^{-2}}
$$

Write it in terms of $z$:

$$
H(z) = \frac{z(z+1)}{z^2 - \frac{1}{2}z + \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{z(z+1)}{(z - 0.25 - j0.433)(z - 0.25 + j0.433)}
$$

Expand $H(z)/z$:

$$
\frac{H(z)}{z} = \frac{z+1}{(z - 0.25 - j0.433)(z - 0.25 + j0.433)}
$$

(The actual calculation requires partial fractions with complex numbers and is omitted.)

Because the poles lie inside the unit circle, the impulse response is an exponentially decaying oscillatory sequence.

I. Mathematical Prerequisites

1. Complex Numbers and Euler’s Formula — ⭐ The Single Most Important Topic

Why you need it: The “language” of Signals and Systems is the complex exponential signal $e^{st}$ (where $s = \sigma + j\omega$). From the complex exponential signals in Chapter 1 to the Laplace transform in Chapter 4, the entire course uses complex numbers.

Core concepts:

  • Complex-number representation: $z = a + jb$, where $j = \sqrt{-1}$ (EE uses $j$ rather than $i$, because $i$ is already used for electric current)

  • Complex plane: the real part is the horizontal axis and the imaginary part is the vertical axis

  • Polar form: $z = re^{j\theta} = r(\cos\theta + j\sin\theta)$

    • $r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ is the magnitude
    • $\theta = \tan^{-1}(b/a)$ is the argument

  • Euler’s formula (the foundation of the entire course):
    $$
    e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta
    $$

  • Important relationships derived from Euler’s formula:
    $$
    \cos\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta}}{2}, \quad
    \sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}
    $$

  • Complex conjugate: $z^* = a - jb$; the magnitude is unchanged and the argument changes sign

  • Complex-number operations: for addition and subtraction, operate separately on the real and imaginary parts; for multiplication and division, multiply or divide the magnitudes and add or subtract the arguments in polar form

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 1: complex exponential signal $x(t) = e^{j\omega_0 t}$
  • Chapter 3: exponential form of the Fourier series
  • Chapter 4: Laplace transform with $s = \sigma + j\omega$
  • Chapter 6: Z-transform with $z = re^{j\Omega}$

2. Calculus Fundamentals

Why you need them: Signal processing fundamentally involves integrating functions (transforms) and differentiating them (system descriptions).

Essential knowledge:

  • Derivatives: basic differentiation formulas

    • $\frac{d}{dt}e^{at} = ae^{at}$
    • $\frac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t) = \omega\cos(\omega t)$
    • $\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega t) = -\omega\sin(\omega t)$

  • Integrals: basic integration formulas

    • $\int e^{at}dt = \frac{1}{a}e^{at} + C$
    • $\int \sin(\omega t)dt = -\frac{1}{\omega}\cos(\omega t) + C$
    • Integration by parts: $\int u dv = uv - \int v du$

  • Limits:

    • $\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sin t}{t} = 1$ (appears in the sampling theorem and Fourier transform)
    • $\lim_{t \to \infty} e^{-at} = 0$ (when $a > 0$; used in system-stability criteria)

  • Improper integrals: Both the Fourier and Laplace transforms involve integrals from $-\infty$ to $\infty$

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 2: describing systems with differential equations
  • Chapter 3: integral definition of the Fourier transform, $X(j\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt$
  • Chapter 4: integral definition of the Laplace transform

3. Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs)

Why you need them: In the time domain, an LTI system is described by a linear constant-coefficient differential equation.

Essential knowledge:

  • First-order ODE: $\frac{dy(t)}{dt} + ay(t) = x(t)$

    • Homogeneous solution: $y_h(t) = Ce^{-at}$
    • The particular solution depends on the form of the input
    • Intuitive explanation: the charging and discharging processes of a first-order RC circuit

  • Second-order ODE: $\frac{d^2y}{dt^2} + a_1\frac{dy}{dt} + a_0y = x(t)$

    • Characteristic equation: $\lambda^2 + a_1\lambda + a_0 = 0$
    • The homogeneous solution depends on the characteristic roots (real, complex, or repeated)

  • Solution procedure:

    1. Solve the homogeneous equation (using the characteristic equation)
    2. Find a particular solution (choose undetermined coefficients based on the form of the input)
    3. Use the initial conditions to determine the unknown coefficients

  • The most important idea: system response = zero-input response (caused by the initial state) + zero-state response (caused by the input)

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 2: solving differential equations for continuous systems (core material)
  • Chapter 4: solving differential equations with the Laplace transform (the easier method)

4. Trigonometry

Why you need it: Sine and cosine signals are fundamental signals, and a Fourier series is fundamentally a superposition of trigonometric functions.

Essential knowledge:

  • Basic identities:

    • $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$
    • $\sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta \pm \cos\alpha\sin\beta$
    • $\cos(\alpha \pm \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta \mp \sin\alpha\sin\beta$

  • Sum-to-product identities (used in Fourier-series derivations):

    • $\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}$
    • $\cos A + \cos B = 2\cos\frac{A+B}{2}\cos\frac{A-B}{2}$

  • Orthogonality (the core of the Fourier series!):
    $$
    \int_0^T \sin(m\omega_0 t)\sin(n\omega_0 t)dt =
    \begin{cases}
    0, & m \neq n \
    T/2, & m = n \neq 0
    \end{cases}
    $$

    • The integral of the product of sinusoids or cosinusoids at different frequencies over a period is zero $\rightarrow$ each frequency component can be isolated

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 3: Fourier series (trigonometric form)
  • Orthogonal decomposition of signals

5. Series and Sequences

Why you need them: Discrete-time signals are sequences, and the Z-transform involves summing infinite series.

Essential knowledge:

  • Geometric series (used constantly—the lifeblood of the Z-transform):

    • Finite: $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} r^n = \frac{1-r^N}{1-r}$
    • Infinite ($|r| < 1$): $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} r^n = \frac{1}{1-r}$
    • Infinite ($|r| > 1$): $\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1} r^n = \frac{r^{-1}}{1-r^{-1}}$

  • Power series: $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^{-n}$ (the Z-transform is a power series!)

  • Convergence: the conditions under which a series converges $\rightarrow$ the region of convergence (ROC) of the Z-transform

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 6: the definition of the Z-transform is a power series
  • Chapter 6: determining the ROC depends on the convergence conditions of a geometric series

II. EE Circuit Fundamentals

1. Characteristics of the Three RLC Elements

Why you need them: Many examples in Signals and Systems come from circuits. You need to understand component characteristics before you can understand system behavior.

Element Time-domain relationship Meaning
Resistor R $v = iR$ Ohm’s law
Capacitor C $i = C\frac{dv}{dt}$ A changing voltage produces current
Inductor L $v = L\frac{di}{dt}$ A changing current produces voltage

Intuitive explanation:

  • A capacitor is like a bucket—accumulating charge takes time, so its voltage cannot change instantaneously
  • An inductor is like a flywheel—its current has inertia and cannot change instantaneously

2. Transient Responses of First-Order RC and RL Circuits

Why you need them: The response of a first-order system is the best intuitive example for understanding a “system time constant” and “exponential decay.”

  • Charging an RC circuit: $v_c(t) = V_s(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, where $\tau = RC$ is the time constant
  • Discharging an RC circuit: $v_c(t) = V_0e^{-t/\tau}$
  • The time constant $\tau$ determines how quickly the system responds

Key idea: A larger $\tau$ means a slower response. In Signals and Systems, a time constant corresponds to the location of a pole.

3. Frequency-Domain Impedance

Why you need it: This is the bridge from the time domain to the frequency domain.

  • Resistor: $Z_R = R$
  • Capacitor: $Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$ (time-domain differentiation $\rightarrow$ division by $j\omega$ in the frequency domain)
  • Inductor: $Z_L = j\omega L$ (time-domain differentiation $\rightarrow$ multiplication by $j\omega$ in the frequency domain)

Intuitive explanation: In the frequency domain, capacitors and inductors become “frequency-dependent resistors.” At high frequencies, a capacitor has low impedance (a short circuit), while an inductor has high impedance (an open circuit).

Where this appears in the course material:

  • Chapter 4: analyzing circuits with the Laplace transform ($s$-domain models)
  • Chapter 7: relationship between the system function $H(s)$ and circuit impedance
