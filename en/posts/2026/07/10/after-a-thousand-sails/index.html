After a Thousand Sails

Something rather irritating keeps happening when I work through exercises. My mind remains quiet while I eat, sleep, or scroll through videos. The moment I begin serious mathematics—especially when I cannot solve a question—my former partner, the stupid things I once did, and the possibility of failing the postgraduate entrance examination all arrive together. It begins with an unsolved limit and soon accelerates into the question of whether my entire life is finished. Not one additional symbol appears on the page.

Today I came across an explanation. The human brain, it seems, is not nearly as modern as we imagine. A tiger attempting to eat you is obviously a danger. To the brain, however, an action that may fail, cause humiliation, or prove one’s inadequacy is dangerous too. This is especially true of the postgraduate entrance examination, into which I have placed far too many other things. It is no longer a collection of mathematics problems; it is connected to education, the future, and the question of whether I am an idiot.

When I cannot solve something, uncertainty and the fear of being classified as a failure rise together. The sympathetic nervous system starts first. Adrenaline and noradrenaline prepare the body for conflict; the slower HPA axis and cortisol response follow. The heart accelerates, attention narrows, and the body assumes that I am about to fight or flee. In reality, I am calculating a limit.

To be precise, cortisol does not carry a loudspeaker through the brain ordering it to “begin searching the archive of shame.” The process is closer to this: once stress rises, working memory and quiet reasoning are impaired, while attention tilts toward danger. The feeling that “I may fail again” becomes a retrieval cue in its own right. Memory is recovered through association, so earlier failures, embarrassments, and moments of shame become easier to call up, as though old surveillance footage had suddenly begun playing on a loop.

But the system is probably not attacking me.

It has merely detected a scent resembling an earlier danger and is trying to prevent me from falling into the same trap. The program was written a long time ago, when rejection by the group or a mistake during a hunt might genuinely have been fatal. In the twenty-first century, it still cannot reliably distinguish “I cannot solve this question” from “the tribe is about to cast me out.”

Damn it. The firmware needs an update.

Once I understood this, the experience became slightly easier to interpret. The reason old memories return whenever a question defeats me may not be that those events urgently need to be discussed. The present state of “perhaps I am incapable” simply points toward earlier moments in which “I genuinely failed to do well.” The memories are not appearing without cause. The mind is searching for like terms.

Love is among them. I think I understand a little better why she may once have liked me, although this is only a model I have fitted to the past from one side; I do not know what she actually thought. Perhaps the person I was then genuinely did think about many things and, after all that thought, remained willing to love something. Later, as the models multiplied and I learned more unpleasant facts, I grew colder. Love was a transaction, friendship an exchange of needs, dreams a release of dopamine, and research an evaluation system. Everything could be explained. Nothing remained interesting.

Love, however, is only one example. What truly concerns me is the person I want eventually to become.

I do not think I want to return to the self who knew nothing. Innocence untouched by experience is beautiful, but part of its beauty comes merely from not having seen. Once it encounters interest, failure, departure, and helplessness, it can break at once. Then a person begins to call himself mature: everyone is selfish; love is false; ideals cannot purchase food; look after yourself first.

None of these statements is entirely wrong. Yet if maturity ends by seeing an interest behind every person and a fraud behind every object, has one not merely travelled from one kind of childishness to another?

What I want may be one step farther on. I want to see something of the world: to know how society operates, how to speak, how to handle affairs, how to keep accounts, and how to make something real. I want to have been rejected and defeated, to have watched relationships dissolve, and to understand that effort does not always produce a result. After passing through all of that, I do not want to pickle myself into a smooth old cynic. I want to remain capable of believing in something again.

To believe that love can exist; that a formula can truly be beautiful; that a problem may deserve investigation for reasons beyond papers and employment; that one person sometimes reaches out simply because another person is in pain. Not because I remain ignorant of the mechanisms, interests, and hormones beneath these events, but because knowledge of a mechanism does not make the event false.

This is probably what I meant by those earlier sentences: idealism at its furthest point becomes inseparable from realism; complete maturity from complete innocence; a genuine dream from the power to act. If an ideal is real, it must understand how reality operates. If maturity is real, it cannot stop at “I have seen through everything and therefore believe in nothing.” If a dream is real, it must eventually become the number of exercises completed today.

The innocence that remains after a thousand sails is not the innocence present at the beginning. The first believes because it does not yet know. The second knows and remains willing. It is not a return to the point of origin. It is arriving there after a long circle, carrying both the wounds and the abilities acquired along the way.

As Romain Rolland is so often quoted that the sentence has nearly worn smooth: true heroism consists in loving life after recognising its truth. I used to regard that as another correctly phrased piece of nonsense. Now I can feel a little of its weight.

What I should do next, then, has two sides. I must learn the operations of reality, become shrewder, acquire the ability to act, earn money, and assume responsibility. At the same time, I must avoid pickling myself entirely into an old cynic. I would like to become someone who can still be delighted by a flower, a formula, or a particular person.

An innocent person who has passed a thousand sails.

Or, to put it differently, a father who has retained the spirit of a boy.

That phrase is rather good.

Of course, this entire essay emerged while I was supposed to be solving problems. Once again, I have successfully explained why I was not working and substituted the explanation for the work itself. How the hell is it still the same recursion?

At least this version can be shorter.

Let the alarm sound. Its sounding does not mean that a tiger is present, nor that the old events must be resolved now. If I cannot solve the question, then I cannot. Read the solution, cover it, and write the answer again. If the brain insists on playing old footage, it may do so in the background. I will finish the next line first.
