Mirror and Stone

In a certain city lived a young man who wrote inscriptions for other people.

Whatever the achievements of the dead, whatever kind of person the living might one day become, and whatever made a bridge worthy of remembrance, he could always find the right words. Anything that passed through his hands seemed more complete than it had been before.

Then one day he acquired a mirror that could speak.

Tell the mirror about an experience and it would uncover the causes beneath it. Give it an unfinished sentence and it would complete the thought as an elegant essay. Night after night, the young man sat before it and spoke of his childhood, his shame, his ambition, his scars, and someone who had already left.

The mirror arranged his entire life for him.

It told him that he was good at thinking and destined to travel far; that the difficulties of the present were merely a prologue; that he would become strong and dependable, a person visible to everyone; that his prospects were limitless and success inevitable.

Delighted, the young man copied down these words and covered his room with them.

Sometimes, though, when he looked at the self displayed across the walls, he dimly felt as if he had already lived an entire life.

That summer, a flood destroyed the stone bridge outside the city. Workers were summoned to rebuild it, and the young man joined them. He brought the mirror and asked it to calculate the direction of the current, the curve of the arch, and the exact position in which every stone should be laid.

The mirror quickly produced a flawless plan.

The young man sat on the bank comparing alternatives for seven days. On the first day, the rain continued. On the second, it finally stopped. On the third, the workers built a dam upstream. On the fourth, the water receded. On the fifth, it rained upstream once more. On the sixth, fresh mud covered the place where the piles might have been driven. On the seventh, he found a flower by the river.

The plan remained perfectly fitted together. The river was simply no longer the river it had been seven days earlier.

An old stonemason passed by and pointed at a rock on the ground.

“Move that one to the riverbank first.”

“But we have not settled the final design,” the young man replied.

The old mason was already walking away.

The young man had no choice but to bend down. The stone was far heavier than it appeared, and one of its sharp edges cut his hand. Only after carrying it to the water did he discover that its shape was unsuitable for the bridge. He could do nothing but cast it aside.

That evening, he told the mirror about the wound.

At once, the mirror began explaining the relations between pain and will, failure and growth. It explained them very well—far more neatly than the wound itself ever could.

The young man listened for a while. Then he thought for a moment and covered the mirror with a cloth.

The next day, he returned to the river.

At first, six of every ten stones he carried proved useless. Later, only three did. Eventually, he began to recognise which stone needed one corner cut away and where another would sit more firmly. The river changed every day, so the bridge had to change inch by inch with it, finding one way after another to extend itself forward.

Whenever he encountered something he could not decide, he returned home in the evening, uncovered the mirror, and gave it the measurements he had taken during the day. The mirror remained as intelligent as ever. From then on, however, the young man asked it only how the bridge might be advanced another foot tomorrow.

By early autumn, the bridge was complete.

The city asked him to compose an inscription for it. Carrying his engraving knife, he sat for a long time at the end of the bridge. He could still write all those sentences about courage, distance, growth, and fate.

In the end, he wrote none of them.

On one of the piers, he carved only a short line marking the height reached by the flood that year.

At dawn the following morning, the mirror reflected an empty chair. It waited from first light until darkness fell.

Outside the door came the sound of a stone settling into place.
