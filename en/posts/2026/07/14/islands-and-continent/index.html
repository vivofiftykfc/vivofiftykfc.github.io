Islands and the Continent

No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the continent, a part of the main.

—John Donne, Meditation XVII

0. Two People Apparently Saying the Same Thing

Feynman once conducted a rather interesting little experiment.

He practised counting at a steady pace in his head and used the number he reached to estimate one minute. Once he became skilled at it, he could read while silently counting and still remember roughly what he had read. Yet the moment he spoke aloud, the counting broke off.

John Tukey, a mathematician he knew at Princeton, was precisely the opposite. Tukey could count and speak at the same time, but could not read simultaneously.

After investigating for a while, they discovered that when Feynman counted, a voice in his mind recited one, two, three; Tukey, by contrast, seemed to watch a ribbon printed with numbers pass before his eyes. Speech collided with the voice in Feynman’s head, while reading blocked Tukey’s visible numbers.

If you are interested, open any Zhihu answer and try it: count inwardly while reading, then keep counting and attempt to read the words aloud. Feynman later tried to find a way to count while reading aloud, but failed; Tukey could not do it either. Apparently, providing voice-overs for Zhihu answers was slightly beyond the syllabus for both gentlemen.

Both said, “I am counting,” yet what they actually experienced was not the same thing.

Human communication is probably always like this. We use the same set of words: happy, sad, lonely, caring, fine. Yet in different people, those words connect to entirely different memories, feelings, and lives. One person has said everything he can say, while the other still receives only a part.

And friendship somehow arises within precisely this part.

We cannot enter another person’s consciousness in full, yet we gradually learn when he really is fine and when we had better ask once more. We remember details meaningless to everyone else, and after misunderstanding him once, we may still be willing to listen again.

Limited understanding has not prevented people from knowing one another.

I find that romantic in itself.

1. How Friendship Begins

Two People Must First Look at Something Together

Friendship seldom begins with, “I want to establish a friendship with you.”

It usually begins with a third thing: a course, a dormitory, a job, a ball game, a video game, a book, or perhaps some person whom both find equally absurd.

Two people first look at something together, and only then notice each other beside it.

This may be one important difference between friendship and love. Love often begins with “I noticed you,” whereas friendship frequently begins with “So you noticed this too.” If two people sit solemnly opposite each other at their first meeting and demand that each present reasons for being suitable as a friend, the scene rather resembles a job interview without a job description.

Shared activity makes closeness natural and gradually reveals a person. Does he take defeat in a game out on others? Can he keep a promise? Does he constantly interrupt? When trouble comes, does he first shift blame or first handle the problem? Much of personality does not live in self-introductions; it lies scattered among small events.

Friends, therefore, are often not selected. They are gradually recognised while doing things together.

Friendship Also Contains Need

Aristotle divided friendship into friendship for utility, for pleasure, and for admiration of the other person’s character.

This classification is easily read as a hierarchy of contempt: people who work on a project together are merely connected by interest, gaming companions are fair-weather friends, and only those who admire each other’s virtue possess true friendship. By that standard, friends should begin dinner by discussing the Idea of the Good—or, failing that, at least exchange their ideals of life instead of merely eating.

Real relationships are not so clean.

Inviting a friend to dinner requires his time; confiding in him requires his attention; working together requires his ability; staying with an interesting person is pleasant to me. From the beginning, friendship includes need, convenience, pleasure, and accident.

The issue is not whether I receive anything from a friend.

The issue is whether, when he temporarily cannot provide those things, a person still remains before my eyes.

A friend who always comforts me is exhausted today: can I allow him not to catch me? The project is over: do I still want to eat with my collaborator? A formerly entertaining friend has little to say lately: do I immediately decide that he has lost his value?

If the person disappears together with his usefulness, perhaps I never knew more than a function.

But if friendship cannot rest on utility alone, when does it begin to care about this particular person?

That is no longer a question of why people meet, but of how a relationship changes.

2. How Friendship Changes

“Being with You Is Quite Nice”

At first, friendship depends on a shared setting.

The same class, dormitory, or organisation repeatedly places people together. They eat, slack off, rush to meet deadlines, and incidentally complain about several mutual acquaintances. The environment arranges the meetings, concrete events supply the topics, and the two people need only avoid disliking each other too much.

The relationship may perfectly well stop here.

“There Is Something I Want to Tell You”

After repeated contact, the other person begins to step out of the setting.

I see something and want to send it to him; when arranging dinner, I naturally leave him a place; when something happens, I imagine beforehand what he might say. At first we merely happened to meet; later I began to expect the meeting. At first we discussed only what was before us; later, lives unrelated to the shared activity gradually entered the relationship.

It looks like only a few more messages, yet an important change has occurred within them: a friend begins to participate in how I understand my own life.

Trust Is Not the Number of Secrets Exchanged

Later still, people hand over parts unsuitable for public display: failure, jealousy, fear, ambition, childish wishes, and certain deeds that remain rather difficult to evaluate even now.

Secrets alone, however, do not prove intimacy. Two people may exchange childhood traumas at three in the morning and still not become close friends the next day. They may finish reporting their entire family histories before the waiter even serves the first dish. Secrets prove only that the other person knows more. Trust comes from what happens after a secret is given: whether it becomes gossip, whether it is turned into a weapon during an argument, and whether a friend who has seen one terrible version of me uses that version to pass a life sentence.

What truly deepens a relationship is not how much was said once, but the many later occasions on which what was known was not abused.

A Shared History Makes Someone Particular

A friend’s particularity does not arise entirely from his virtues.

There are, of course, people in the world who are cleverer, funnier, or better at comforting others. Yet nobody can participate again in a past that has already happened. Who was there during the hardest period? Why did some apparently trivial event matter so much at the time? Through how many transformations did an old joke pass before becoming what it is today? None of this can be regained simply by meeting someone with better qualifications.

A shared history does not make a friend eternally right, but it does make him impossible to replace casually.

From here, friendship acquires a little responsibility. I know that my silence may worry him, that a certain remark may hurt him, and what he has recently been enduring. My choices are therefore no longer wholly unrelated to him.

But trouble arrives as well.

If shared history, trust, and daily participation all make someone particular, does a friendship necessarily become superior when people have known each other longer, know more, and communicate more often? Is a playmate merely an ordinary friend awaiting an upgrade, and an ordinary friend a best friend still loading?

Obviously not.

3. Friends Are Not Ranks but Different Shapes

“Playmate—ordinary friend—growth companion—best friend” may roughly describe relationships, but it cannot be treated as a single route of promotion. A playmate need not eventually become a best friend, and a best friend may not suit every activity in my life.

Some people are good travel companions but poor colleagues. Some can discuss my most private troubles, yet seldom contact me in ordinary times. Others are bad at comforting words but arrive fastest when something really happens.

Instead of asking what rank a friendship occupies, we might ask what shape it has. That shape contains at least three elements.

Where the Relationship Grew From

It may arise from a shared interest, class, dormitory, or organisation; from working on a project, enduring difficulty together, mutual admiration, mutual understanding, or simply the comfort of being with this person.

Its origin influences the relationship’s most natural form. Friends who met through an interest may always depend upon a fixed activity. Those who endured hardship together may have little to say in ordinary times, yet retain a deep confirmation through shared experience. Still others did nothing remarkable together; they merely happened to share many ordinary days.

What the Relationship Can Carry

It may carry play, cooperation, and everyday company, or intellectual exchange, exposed vulnerability, mutual criticism, practical help, and responsibility in emergencies. Plainly, these capacities do not appear automatically as one package.

Being able to confide does not mean being suited to cooperation. Knowing someone for years does not make every subject discussable. Meeting every day does not guarantee reliability in a crisis. The person who listens to my trauma may not be good at helping me move house, while the person who helps me move may have no idea how to comfort me.

We easily demand every other capacity once one is present. If you can listen to my heart, you should understand me at all times; if you are an old friend, you should support every choice; if you are called a best friend, you should preferably provide companionship, advice, entertainment, emergency assistance, and lifelong witness all at once. This is no longer a friend but a full-stack position. The job description is excessively long, and the salary is paid mainly in reaction memes. One can hardly blame the applicant.

What Keeps the Relationship Alive

Some friendships rely on frequent meetings, others on a regular activity or shared project. Some require both sides to volunteer news of their lives; others rest mainly upon shared history and long-standing trust. Some relationships pause for years, yet grow new content when both people remain willing to approach each other again.

Frequency of contact alone therefore proves neither closeness nor distance. Friends who talk every day may chiefly share the present; people who meet once in several years may preserve profound historical trust. The former is not shallow, nor is the latter necessarily truer. They exist through different things.

The same friend may be “low-frequency contact, high historical trust, highly reliable in emergencies,” or “high-frequency contact, high entertainment value, low capacity for vulnerability.” Neither stands above the other; their shapes differ.

“Best friend” still has meaning, of course. It usually means that a relationship is deep in several respects and has endured time and reality. But it is not every friendship’s graduation certificate. A relationship that honestly carries what it is suited to carry, without being forced to take charge of an entire life, is already very good.

These three dimensions explain how friendship operates, but not why, if a friend is merely a collection of functions, someone with similar functions cannot replace him when the relationship fades.

The reason is that a friend eventually enters more than my schedule.

4. Friends Enter “Who I Am”

Nobody grows in a sealed room.

How I see myself, remember my past, decide what is funny, and speak to comfort myself when things happen—all bear, to some degree, the traces of other people’s participation. Friends especially do so.

Some friends remember versions of me that I have forgotten. They know what I once sincerely believed before doing what I do today; they know that a decision now fit for a joke tormented me badly at the time; and they know how a part of my character that now seems natural first developed within a shared life.

This is why meeting an old friend can feel strange. Although neither has participated in the other’s daily life for years, after several sentences I suddenly remember a manner of speaking long unused. The relationship has not automatically returned to the past. Rather, the other person preserves a road towards my former self.

Friends also become reference points.

When something happens, I imagine how he would judge it. When choosing, I remember what we once argued about. When a friend follows another road, his life reveals a possibility I did not choose. This includes not only support, but comparison, jealousy, and defiance. In My Brilliant Friend, Lenù and Lila admire one another while continually treating the other as a coordinate for measuring their own lives. A friend’s light does not always merely illuminate; sometimes it is rather dazzling.

This explains why drifting apart hurts.

What is lost may be more than someone to chat, dine, or ask for help. Sometimes it is a shared language, a witness to an earlier self, and a part of life to which I could once return through that person. Another equally interesting and reliable individual cannot reproduce this history.

Yet someone’s entry into “who I am” does not mean that he belongs to me.

Precisely because a friend has acquired weight in my life, I can easily replace “you matter to me” with “I am entitled to demand things from you.” Here begins friendship’s real difficulty.

5. Closeness Does Not Create Ownership

A friend is not another version of me. Even after the relationship deepens, he will still refuse and change, and live in ways unfamiliar to me.

Knowing Someone Does Not Mean Explaining Him Completely

The more familiar we become with a person, the more readily we skip the question.

He has not replied, so he must be withdrawing. She says “whatever,” so she must be unhappy. A friend did not follow my advice, so he must reject my judgement. After guessing correctly several times, we easily promote guesses into facts, then complete our understanding, disappointment, and downgrading of the relationship alone, before the other person even knows what has happened.

The most valuable part of “I know you” is not that I never need to ask again, but that I know where guessing is insufficient.

A friend is not somebody who never misunderstands me. Such a person probably lives only in my imagination, rent-free at that.

A friend is closer to someone who lets me explain myself, and who updates his judgement after discovering a misunderstanding. I must also allow him to overturn the version I wrote for him.

Help Must Not Secretly Become Control

When I advise a friend, I may sincerely wish him well.

Yet advice also contains other things: a wish that my ability be recognised, that the time I spent produce a result, and that he act according to my method, thereby proving that I understood the problem.

So when he listens but does nothing, I feel my time has been wasted and do not want to interfere again.

But rejecting my advice does not necessarily mean rejecting me. The method may be unsuitable; he may be unprepared; or he may merely have wanted to speak, rather than open a public tender for a complete solution to his life.

I may offer a view and decide how far I am willing to help. Yet if so-called help does not permit refusal, it has already begun to resemble control.

A Friend May Say I Was Wrong

True friendship neither rationalises everything I do nor takes my side unconditionally within a shared social circle.

A friend may believe both that “you handled this terribly” and that “you are not reducible to this one act.” Understanding someone does not cancel consequences; criticising one act need not burn his entire history with it.

Of course, a friend has the right to be disappointed and to change the distance of the relationship. Friendship is not a card of absolution with a clause saying, “Since you understand me, you must forgive me.”

My wish that a friend not summarise me by one mistake may be real; his unwillingness to continue the former relationship may also be real. Holding both together is painful, but pain does not make only one of them true.

Being Particular Does Not Mean Being Owned

Friends enter new circles, devote more time to work, partners, and new lives, decline invitations, cease sharing everything first, and become somewhat different from who they were.

The deeper a relationship is, the easier it becomes to interpret these changes as betrayal: we have known each other for so long—why did you not put me first?

Shared history makes someone particular; it does not make him mine. Closeness gives me reasons to care and requires me to bear some consequences of my actions. It does not qualify me to manage his life.

If friendship contains freedom without responsibility, it becomes two people always prepared to retreat. If it contains responsibility without the freedom to refuse or leave, it becomes a contract nobody signed and nobody may resign from.

A real relationship can only remain between the two.

This sounds quite complete in principle, but friends do not live inside principles.

They face unanswered messages, promised dinners, contact after graduation, and the question of whether one person will actually be present when the other truly needs him.

6. Reality

Friendship has real costs; nobody simply sends the bill most of the time.

Some Time May Be Entirely Useless

Two people may discuss literature, philosophy, trauma, and the future until they feel they have reached each other’s souls. Yet profound conversation creates a sense of closeness, not necessarily a reliable relationship.

Real friends also eat, play games, do separate things in the same room, and say nonsense forgotten the next day. Nobody solves the other’s life, and no minutes are produced.

If a friend must provide insight, mutual growth, or crisis rescue, usefulness has merely taken over friendship under a more progressive name.

Relationships Require Maintenance

In the same class, dormitory, or organisation, the environment delivers people to each other every day. After graduation, relocation, or the end of a project, automatic meetings stop, and only then must both sides walk towards each other themselves.

Nobody needs to report daily. An occasional “still alive?”, a ridiculous image only the other person understands, or a meal without a theme adds a little present to a shared history. The past matters, but it does not send messages to today by itself.

Reciprocity Is Not Settled Transaction by Transaction

Friendship certainly requires reciprocity. If one person alone keeps initiating, listening, and helping while the other appears only in need, even a tolerant person grows tired. Yet reciprocity does not require every matter to be repaid immediately, nor reply times, gift prices, and minutes of confiding to be equal. People possess different energy at different stages and care in different ways.

Mutuality means something closer to this: over enough time, both people are sometimes willing to walk towards the other, and neither treats the other’s presence as natural entitlement. If every meal, confidence, and favour is recorded exactly, friendship may achieve quantitative management, but the people will probably be dismissed by the accounting system.

Drifting Apart

Love at least has the word “break-up.” Friendship usually ends without notice: messages become fewer, and “let’s meet next time” never reaches a next time. One day, two people discover that they still call each other friends, yet no longer know what life the other is living.

The later ending of a relationship does not rewrite the fact that it once happened. Someone may no longer participate in my life today, yet already have participated in the process by which I became who I am today. The past can have been real, and departure can also be real.

At this point, friendship need no longer be arranged into a checklist of qualifications. Everything above grew from the initial facts. Because nobody can directly enter another consciousness, understanding depends upon repeated questions and revisions. Because a friend has no duty to bind himself to me, remaining is a choice. Because that choice forms a shared history over time, a particular person becomes impossible to replace casually. And because he remains independent, this particularity brings only limited responsibility, never ownership.

So for now, I would probably understand friendship like this:

Friendship is the approach of two independent people who can never understand each other completely, brought close by sharing a part of life; through repeated contact, they gradually care about this particular person and form trust and a shared history; without compulsory obligation, they nevertheless remain willing to give one another limited but genuine care, freedom, and responsibility.

It does not demand that two people become each other’s only answer, nor promise that they will never drift apart. It asks only that while the relationship exists, I treat the person before me seriously and take seriously what my actions may leave in him.

7. Reflection

All the preceding principles are convenient when used to analyse other people. Turning them back upon myself is less dignified.

Maintenance and Accounting

I desire a free and mutual friendship, yet often interpret “freedom” as freedom from maintenance, and “mutuality” as the other person’s duty to know when to find me. I hope friends understand what I leave unsaid, then quickly doubt the entire relationship when they occasionally fail to catch it.

To put it unpleasantly, I sometimes treat friends as old archive files that never expire: I do no maintenance in ordinary times, yet when needed, expect to read them from the old location—with version compatibility, complete data, and preferably no rubbish explanation after opening. This is plainly unreasonable. A person is not a cloud-storage subscription; besides, even that subscription renews only when the other side acts.

I once judged best friends by several things: whether we knew each other’s secrets, had seen each other at our worst, told each other important news first, maintained contact over time, and kept the relationship sufficiently equal. These standards are not wholly wrong, but I readily turned them into rankings. Who hears first may indicate closeness, but also become an examination. Knowing many secrets may indicate trust, but cannot ensure mutual understanding. Equality, if it means receiving the same response for every effort, ends only in a meticulously kept account book.

I also valued whether someone could “understand me.” When a friend failed to catch my meaning or adopt my advice, I sometimes instinctively wondered whether the relationship was less deep than I thought, or whether my ability had gone unrecognised.

Yet this still turns a friend’s response into confirmation of me.

He may be willing to listen but not know what to say; he may understand and still choose another method; or he may genuinely lack energy today. A friend who does not care for me in the manner familiar to me is not necessarily uncaring. Rejecting my answer does not mean rejecting me.

Keep in Touch, Even When Nothing Is Wrong

Another obvious problem is that most of my friends came from shared settings.

Classmates, organisations, people with whom I did things. While the setting remained, I could be devoted because the environment handled meetings and topics for me. Once it disappeared, I scarcely knew how to contact people for no particular reason. I seemed to require a sufficiently proper excuse before daring to appear.

Consequently, I do not share ordinary life in ordinary times, yet suddenly remember somebody when disaster comes and hope the relationship can immediately recover its former depth. Afterwards I fear having disturbed him and become even less willing to make contact. The cycle can hardly be called ingenious. I mainly manage to claim the benefits on both sides: solitude in ordinary times and a relationship waiting at attention during emergencies.

More contradictorily, I want friendship to be mutual while often waiting for the other person to come first. When he does not appear, I suspect the relationship is fading; from his perspective, however, the person who seldom initiates may be me.

Understanding and Criticism

The shared social circle left by my previous intimate relationship also made me reconsider what “understanding” and “criticism” mean.

I did make mistakes and hurt someone. Naturally, I hope friends can believe I acted wrongly without sentencing my whole person to death for a single mistake. Yet when somebody really remains willing to associate with me, I feel indebted to his tolerance and become constrained. The ideal scene appears to be one in which he simply curses me for a while. On reflection, this also hides an account: as if, once I accept a scolding, the punishment is complete and the relationship should automatically return to normal.

But criticism is not a fine, and friendship is not automatically unfrozen after payment. Friends may be disappointed and choose their distance anew. What I can do is admit what happened and change how I treat people afterwards, rather than demand that somebody stay to prove I have improved.

What to Do

So perhaps I need to do several things that are not particularly grand.

  • I may contact people even when nothing important has happened—friends need communication. Before confiding, ask whether the other person has energy. Before advising, ask whether he wants me merely to listen or to think of a solution together. When a friend does not follow my advice, resist imagining myself as an unappreciated strategist.

  • I must also accept that different relationships have different shapes. A playmate need not understand my whole self, and somebody who listens to my heart need not remain on call. If another person stays, I may be grateful without immediately recording a debt of tolerance.

  • Finally, allow friends to know me again, and allow myself to know them again.

People truly are islands. We can speak only across our separate experiences, and some part will never cross. A friend cannot live for me and has no duty to remain docked here forever. Yet people truly are also pieces of a continent. Not because boundaries finally disappear, but because limited understanding, time spent together, and the influence we leave upon one another genuinely change a person.

A friend does not make the island disappear. But the island’s shape is no longer quite what it would have been had they never met.
