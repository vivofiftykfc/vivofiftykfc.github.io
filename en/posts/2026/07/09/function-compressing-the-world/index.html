Chapter One: Functions, Limits, and Continuity—The First Foundation of Calculus Viewed Through Epistemology

This is the single-chapter version of Chapter One. The same epistemological thread was later expanded into Advanced Mathematics: An Epistemological Map from Functions to Fields.

Wang Yangming said that before you looked at a flower, it and your mind both returned to silence; when you came to look at it, its colours became clear at once. The rough meaning is that the world does not enter the mind in a completely naked form. When you find a flower beautiful, the beauty does not arise merely because the flower is “objectively beautiful.” Its colour, form, light, and shadow produce an image in your mind, and your mind brightens with it.

Epistemologically speaking, human beings are continually compressing, abstracting, and modeling the objective world. We do not know the world itself directly. Instead, we compress its enormous, confused, and complicated information into models that can be understood, used, and transmitted. This is particularly evident in natural science: physics compresses motion and force into formulas; chemistry compresses changes in matter into structures; mathematics uses still more abstract symbols to compress relations themselves.

A good model cannot be excessively bulky. Ideally, it consumes little energy, remains concise and elegant, and is nevertheless useful. As for beauty, I currently suspect that it may be whatever reduces information entropy. Symmetry, unity, and the sudden appearance of a simple structure beneath a complicated phenomenon all have this quality. Upon seeing such a structure, one thinks, “So that is how it works.” This is a kind of cognitive pleasure. At a more fundamental level, perhaps the pleasure arose from the pressure for survival written into human genes?

Consider the first chapter of advanced mathematics. Calculus studies change, but before studying change we must identify the object that changes. We therefore begin with functions.

A function is a model of material, quantitative, and changing relations, represented through algebra and graphs. Reality contains many complicated relations: time and position, temperature and time, voltage and current, cost and profit. Mathematics abstracts them into a correspondence between an independent and a dependent variable: y=f(x). A complicated relation in reality is thereby compressed into a model that algebra and a graph can describe together.

We consequently study a function’s domain and range; inverse, implicit, and composite functions; and such properties as monotonicity, parity, periodicity, and boundedness. These topics are all asking what the model looks like. Over what range does it hold? Can it be understood in reverse? Can several simpler relations be composed to produce it? Does it contain symmetry, repetition, boundaries, or order?

At this point, the model called a function has been provisionally established.

A function alone, however, is insufficient. It describes a correspondence but does not adequately answer questions about approach. Often, what concerns us is not an isolated point, but the tendency shown by f(x) as (x) gradually approaches a value or travels towards infinity.

We therefore introduce the limit.

A limit enables us to study stability within a process. It is concerned not with the static result at one point, but with the destination approached by one variable while another continually draws nearer. Infinitesimals and infinities enter mathematical language in the same way. An infinitesimal is not simply a very small ordinary number; it is a quantity tending towards zero within a process. Infinity is not a number that can literally be held in one’s hand, but a tendency for function values to increase without bound.

Within the rigorous system used for postgraduate mathematics, limits are normally defined over the real numbers through the language of $\varepsilon-\delta$. For purposes of understanding, however, I find it illuminating to imagine extending the reals into the hyperreals. Hyperreal numbers offer a more intuitive picture: beyond ordinary real numbers, there seem actually to exist infinitesimal quantities “infinitely close to zero without being zero,” as well as infinite quantities greater than every ordinary real number.

As a proof, 1=3/3=3*1/3=0.33333333*3=0.99999999 is of course valid. The real-number system is continuous and complete and contains no infinitesimal gaps; infinitesimals belong to the hyperreal system. In other words, there is no gap between 0.999999999 and 1 among the reals.

This does not mean that a postgraduate examination will test hyperreal numbers. Rather, they provide a cognitive model for understanding limits. “Approach” ceases to be merely a formal definition and becomes something that can almost be handled in imagination. Standard analysis constrains approach rigorously with $\varepsilon-\delta$, whereas non-standard analysis seems to place infinitesimals directly inside the number system. The former is more rigorous and the latter more intuitive. At different levels, both help us understand the same thing.

The limit thus adds a sense of process to the model of a function. A function states what a relation is; a limit states what happens to it at a boundary, during an approach, or at infinity.

We then study the definition and properties of limits and the methods by which they are calculated. Arithmetic operations on limits, the squeeze theorem, the two important limits, and equivalent infinitesimals are all devices for treating relations of approach more effectively.

L’Hôpital’s rule and the Taylor formula can be regarded as more advanced instruments for calculating limits.

L’Hôpital’s rule is particularly interesting. Many limits are difficult because they appear in an indeterminate form such as $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. Looking directly at the function values tells us little. L’Hôpital’s rule says that we may instead examine the rates of change of numerator and denominator. When both quantities tend towards zero or both towards infinity, we compare not the quantities themselves but the speeds at which they change.

This, too, is a form of modeling. When the original model is difficult to interpret or calculate, we change perspective from the “size of a quantity” to its “rate of change.” If two runners are both standing at the starting line at one instant, their positions do not reveal who is faster. Their velocities do.

The Taylor formula is an extreme form of model compression. A complicated function may be difficult to understand near a point, but Taylor tells us that a polynomial can approximate it:

$$f(x)\approx f(a)+f’(a)(x-a)+\frac{f’’(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\cdots $$

This is remarkably beautiful. Polynomials are among the most familiar and easily calculated objects in mathematics, yet near a point a complicated function can be decomposed into a constant term, a linear term, a quadratic term, a cubic term, and so on. It is as though an intricate curve were opened near one point and revealed as an ordered sequence. Adding more terms makes the model more precise; retaining only the first few makes it simpler. The aesthetic force is strong: complexity is compressed into simplicity, a curve into a polynomial, and local variation into information carried by derivatives.

L’Hôpital and Taylor are therefore more than techniques for solving examination questions. Both have an epistemological meaning. When a model is difficult to handle in its original form, we replace it with a more workable model that approaches the same object. L’Hôpital compares change through rates of change; Taylor approximates a complicated function locally through a polynomial.

Continuity and discontinuity then appear as natural applications.

If a limit studies the tendency of a function near a point, continuity asks whether that tendency meets the function’s actual value at the point. If, as $x$ approaches $x_0$, the limit of $f(x)$ equals $f(x_0)$, the function is continuous there.

Continuity means that the model does not break locally. Its tendency and value connect. A discontinuity means that the connection has failed: perhaps a removable point has not been filled; perhaps the left and right sides move towards different values; perhaps the function rushes towards infinity; or perhaps it oscillates without settling.

Seen in this way, the first chapter of advanced mathematics does not merely pile functions, limits, and continuity together. It establishes the most basic cognitive model of calculus. First, functions compress relations found in reality. Limits then describe the tendencies of those relations during a process of approach. Infinitesimals, infinities, and the intuitive picture of hyperreal numbers help us imagine such tendencies. Methods of calculation allow us to handle them, and continuity finally judges whether the local model remains stable and joined together.

This is my present understanding: mathematics does not move away from the world. It recompresses the world in a form that consumes less energy and is more concise and beautiful. A function models a relation; a limit models a tendency; L’Hôpital models comparison through rates of change; Taylor models local approximation; and continuity models stability. The whole structure of calculus begins here.
