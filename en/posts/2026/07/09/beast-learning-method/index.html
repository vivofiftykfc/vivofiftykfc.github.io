The Beast's Method of Studying

Original version:

Recently I have been thinking about a kind of beast’s method of studying. The idea is to treat myself as a beast: stop thinking about everything, stop applying a whole moral code in order to condemn myself, and regard myself as an idiot. I am simply too stupid for this question, so I have to study it more. I am a beast that must pile up enough hours before these things can be learned properly. If I think of myself as a work animal and get on with the labour, there seems to be much less internal friction. Different parts of the brain also need to take turns; I cannot study the same subject without interruption. I have done mathematics for two days in a row and now feel close to vomiting. I need some English, politics, or a major course in the middle to slow down and recover. Sometimes philosophy should remain philosophy. Too much thought, philosophising, and summarising of methods delays actual study, just as listening to lesson after lesson without producing any output interferes with progress.

GPT-organised version:

I have recently been considering a beast’s method of studying. That means, first of all, ceasing to treat myself as a defendant before a moral court and treating myself as a beast instead.

If I cannot solve the question, it is not because my life has failed, my soul is inferior, I lack the mentality of the strong, or I have not discovered some ultimate method of learning.

I am simply bad at it.

If you are bad, practise. If you are slow, look again. If you do not know, put in the hours.

I am an idiot. I cannot solve this question, and this concept has not yet grown inside my brain. What can be done? Nothing particularly clever. Read it again. Write it again. Get it wrong tomorrow, then crawl back the day after that.

It sounds unpleasant, but the statement has a peculiar advantage: it produces much less internal friction.

Once I admit that “I am simply slow,” a great deal of additional judgment disappears. Previously, the process after a wrong answer looked something like this: get the question wrong; ask why I got it wrong again; wonder whether I am unsuited to mathematics; wonder whether I will fail the postgraduate entrance examination; and eventually arrive at existentialism, family structure, the education system, and the limitations of human civilisation.

The question remains uncorrected.

Good grief. Why am I writing a sociological elegy for another wrong answer?

Here is a rougher model:

flowchart TD
    A[做错题] --> B[我不会]
    B --> C[看答案]
    C --> D[遮住重做]
    D --> E[明天再做]

The process is not elegant. It contains no epistemological beauty and no philosophical depth. It is effective. Or perhaps it is effective precisely because it is not elegant. It leaves the brain too little room for performance and prevents my overdeveloped analytical mind from calling another meeting.

The second thing I have felt very clearly is that different parts of the brain really do need to take turns.

After two consecutive days of mathematics, I feel close to vomiting. This is not the ordinary “oh, I do not feel like working hard” kind of nausea. A particular region of the brain genuinely feels as though it has been rubbed with sandpaper. Show me another limit, derivative, or integral and I want to throw both the book and myself out of the room.

The book cannot be thrown away, of course. It was rather expensive.

So the same task cannot remain at full load indefinitely. When mathematics makes me sick, I switch to English. When English vocabulary turns numb, I switch to politics. When politics makes me feel that I am memorising a pile of correctly phrased nonsense, I switch to a major course. When that material begins to scatter, I return to basic mathematics problems.

This is not avoidance.

Avoidance means opening Bilibili and announcing, “I am taking a short break.”

Switching mental gears is still study. It simply stops one part of the brain from being squeezed to death. Even a work animal cannot pull the same cart without pause; a beast occasionally needs a different trough. The language is crude, but the fact is real.

The third rule is that philosophy should shut up for a while.

The sentence makes even me laugh. One of my strongest abilities is turning a specific problem into an abstract one. If I merely failed to review vocabulary today, I can transform the matter into the question of “whether language acquisition is essentially the subject’s domestication of a symbolic system.” It sounds cultured.

The vocabulary remains unlearned.

If I merely failed to produce solutions in mathematics, I can diagnose a “knowledge–action gap between input and output.”

The questions remain unwritten.

That is idiotic.

I am not saying that the analyses are wrong. On the contrary, most of them may be correct. That is precisely the problem. A correct idea can still delay the work, and its correctness may make the delay even easier to defend.

The same applies to lessons. The teacher explains everything smoothly; you follow without difficulty; the notes fill page after page in several carefully distinguished colours. The brain develops the illusion that the knowledge has already been mastered.

Then the video ends and the exercise sheet comes out.

Oh dear.

Who was the person who had mastered the subject a minute ago? Has he logged off?

I may therefore need a brutal rule: input that is not received by output counts only as passing traffic.

A lesson without exercises is passing traffic. A book that cannot be restated is passing traffic. Vocabulary that cannot be recalled is passing traffic. Understanding a worked solution without covering it and solving the question again is also passing traffic.

Knowledge entered through the eyes and ears, took a tourist photograph at the gate of the brain, and left. You cannot claim that it moved in merely because it once visited.

In plain language:

I am a beast.

I am a work animal.

I am a slow creature that can be built only through time and repetition.

The sentence sounds insulting, yet it contains a certain kindness. A beast does not question the meaning of life because it cannot differentiate today. A work animal does not write an existentialist essay because it pulled the mill poorly.

It continues pulling.

I do not literally want to become a beast. A human being must still think; without thought, one merely reaches a different form of numbness. At this stage, however, excessive thought is not the ability in short supply.

For me, what is scarce is the ability not to think.

At least during these hours at the desk, think a little less.

Write a little more.

If you do not know, admit that you do not know.

If one subject makes you sick, change subjects.

After input, produce output.

When you want to summarise another methodology, ask one question first: have today’s exercises been completed?

If not, this essay should not become too long either.

It is already long.

Damn it.

Sleep.
