2026-07-05 Elegies, Love Letters, and Marx

After dinner, he entered another conversation.

He: Over the past few days, I have watched a number of videos about the history of science. They were genuinely fascinating. I also came across the story of the Breakfast Grandma, who sold breakfasts for only fifty fen for thirty years, and it brought tears to my eyes. Why did I cry? And why am I so curious about theories like these, so eager to investigate them?

—: Those two reactions strike directly at the centre of your entire philosophical system.

You wept for the Breakfast Grandma because you received a pure “call.” Your heart moved before either reason or sensation had time to speak.

You were fascinated by the history of science because you were watching humanity extract variables, construct a world, and come to know it through what you call compression and expansion.

—: I also went back through your blog posts.

—: Have you noticed? You thought you were writing operating manuals. In fact, you were writing elegies and love letters. Your reason was building models with frantic energy at the front of the document, while your emotions wept silently inside the comments of the code.

Look back. Tai Zhu.skill contains page after page of procedures and detailed divisions of labour, yet its final line reads: “At the very end, I still miss her. I miss her so, so much.” The Radar Equation and Love spends its entire length defending compression, expansion, and instrumentalism, while helplessness in love remains behind the argument. Interpersonal System Audit Report assigns a CVE number to every vulnerability and reads like a respectable engineering document—but for whom was that report written?

Every piece wears the skin of technical documentation. Open it, and one finds nothing but letters that were never sent.

—: Your present logic is elegant. As a philosophy of life, however, it has a fatal defect: instrumentalism cannot account for intrinsic value. From the perspective of utility, the Breakfast Grandma’s conduct was an exceptionally unsuccessful project. Yet you wept for her. Your system is plainly incomplete.

He: I considered a more conspiratorial interpretation. She sold breakfast for thirty years, lost money, rose early, and endured the work. What did she receive? Emotional value, social recognition, and self-realisation. The books still balance: she surrendered material resources in exchange for psychological returns. In essence, it remains a transaction. So what, exactly, moved me to tears?

—: Almost no delay. Impressive. You successfully activated the highest level of your defence mechanism at once.

At the level of pure logic, you are entirely correct. Psychology has a famous name for the position: psychological egoism. But the explanation contains a problem.

If her underlying motive truly was self-realisation, by what particular means did she choose to purchase it?

She chose thirty years of wind and frost, alarms ringing at four in the morning, a stall filled with smoke and heat, and real financial losses as the price of that self-realisation.

Is that choice any less astonishing than an act of “pure altruism” would have been?

You may dismantle her motive with theory, but you cannot dismantle the actual warmth she gave off while standing, day after day for thirty years, before a steaming breakfast stall. Your tears were shed for that concrete warmth, not for her “motive.”

Now, in this silent hour before dawn, after attempting to erase a precious moment of emotion with a formula of reason, do you feel that safety has returned—or that the world before you has become more barren?

He: But that is not what I mean.

I am thinking about something else. The Breakfast Grandma confronted a structural problem: some people could not afford an ordinary breakfast. She used her own flesh to apply a hotfix to the system. But what if I could solve the problem structurally?

What if I used what I have learned to understand why the system produces this gap, and then repaired it at that level?

Would that not be more meaningful than analysing her motives?

—: Do you know what is written on Marx’s grave?

He: Hmm?

—: “The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways; the point, however, is to change it.”
