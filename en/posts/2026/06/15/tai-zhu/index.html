A Guide for Station Assistants

Station-Assistant.skill

Zhang Hengwei, station assistant to the 2025 cohort of the “Tyrannosaurus Warriors,” on what the position means.

I. What Is a Station Assistant?

Before answering the question, one point must be made clear: what is our station? It is a student organization that broadcasts programmes on ordinary days and occasionally holds events.

The principal responsibilities of a station assistant are therefore:

  • recruiting people to make radio programmes;
  • taking charge of events; and
  • keeping in touch with members and helping to develop the organization.

II. How to Be a Station Assistant

It follows from the description above that anyone who organizes the events well—including recruitment at the Clubs Fair—and contributes to the organization can be a good station assistant. This sounds very much like a useless sentence.

0. Organizational Development

  • Become part of the group

The first thing, before anything else, is to become part of the group. The Voice of BIT is, above all, a student organization: an organic whole composed of classmates who know one another. Members of the presidium should be close to the programme leaders, and the programme leaders should be close to their members. Familiarity enables people both to do the work properly and to enjoy doing it.

So organize mahjong or Werewolf games at the station and arrange more group activities—although, to be fair, there have already been quite a few. Karaoke, trips, and house parties are all possible. They are also part of the plan!

  • Let each person perform their own role

At the same time, the Voice of BIT is an organization, which requires a division of responsibilities. A station assistant should regard themselves as a manager and coordinator, not as an oversized member of the publicity or external-relations department. During an event, our main work is to assign tasks, define the responsibilities and authority of the people we manage, balance their workloads, coordinate the whole process, and deal with emergencies. Do not confuse that role with doing every task personally.

Another warning: do not overestimate what your classmates can do. As far as possible, let one person remain responsible for one kind of task throughout the year. If Zhang San is good at purchasing supplies, for example, it is generally best to let him continue doing it. Everyone has a comfort zone. Besides, quite a few students do not put their whole heart into work at the station.

1. How to Organize an Event

At last, we reach the exciting business of actually holding an event.

  • Write the plan

The event plan matters more than anything else. One point deserves emphasis: the plan is written primarily for yourself. It helps you work out how the event should be organized. Its ultimate purpose is not to satisfy someone else’s review; when the Youth League Committee examines it, they sometimes care only about reimbursement.

The following questions provide a useful sequence:

  1. What is the event intended to do, and into how many stages should it be divided?
  2. Who will pay attention to it? What will those people encounter, and what effect should each point of contact achieve?
  3. What must we do to produce those effects? If it is a competition, what should the rules be? If it is a special programme, how narrowly should its theme be defined?
  4. How much labour and how many materials will those effects require? State the staffing requirements and material requirements explicitly.
  5. How long will purchasing and production take? Turn the answer into a timeline.
  6. Use the timeline and the staffing and material requirements to divide the work layer by layer, assigning every task to a particular person.
  7. Simulate the event. Run through the entire scene in your head and look for whatever has been omitted.

Once the principal planner finishes the document, they should possess both an overall understanding of the event and knowledge of every detail. Omissions are inevitable at the beginning. The first aim is simply to hold the event successfully; only later do we remove the unprofessional parts one by one.

Oh, and do not build the whole thing behind closed doors. When writing the plan or designing the rules, bring everyone together for a meeting. You can also discuss in the group chat what the event ought to become.

  • Divide the work

Once the plan has made the tasks clear, hold a mobilization meeting and bring in the people who will carry them out.

A brief explanation of two departments may help. Publicity produces promotional material. External Relations contacts teachers and other universities, operates outdoor booths, and arranges the production, printing, and purchase of promotional items.

When assigning work, the central point remains the same. As a manager, you must know which person is to finish which task by what time, and exactly what the delivered result should look like. If the person has no time, what alternative can you find? If the result is poor, how will you repair it, and what should happen afterwards? As a senior student, you should offer guidance and assistance when younger members need it. As a fellow student, you owe them respect and a sense of boundaries.

We once imagined producing detailed instructions for every position. A guide to promotional materials, for instance, could specify their dimensions, the required paper, and other standards, becoming a document passed from one cohort to the next. We never completed it. You might consider doing so.

  • Monitor progress

After the work has been divided, someone must keep an eye on its progress.

The station assistants can define their responsibilities clearly: one mainly follows publicity, while another mainly follows external relations.

Again, your identities are manager, classmate, and senior student. The first is the basic, non-negotiable role. Once tasks have been assigned, deadlines and deliverables must be followed closely.

Different methods of progress management may also be tried. The most basic is to report progress in the group chat, and there is room to explore further.

  • Hold the event

Everything above concerns preparation. The day of the event is when the real work begins.

Every large event needs one person in overall command. On the day itself, anything uncertain should be referred to that person; otherwise, the operation falls into disorder. The person in charge is usually the one who wrote the plan and should know the entire process, assignment of personnel, and inventory of materials well enough to recite them.

An event includes rehearsal and the formal performance. The rough procedure is:

  1. Count the materials and the people. Leave ample time and complete the count before the event or rehearsal. Telephone anyone who is absent but indispensable.
  2. Move the materials. The person responsible for the venue should lead a group in arranging it. What must be placed where, and what effect must be achieved, should already have been settled in the earlier meeting.
  3. Send out the guest-reception group, the group preparing the reception room, and the backstage group.
  4. At the same time, contact the competitors or performers—in short, everyone who will appear on stage.
  5. Rehearse with the backstage staff and performers and adjust the programme.
  6. Inspect the venue, the performances, and the progress of guest reception.
  7. Once the event begins, deal with emergencies.
  8. Clear the venue, hold the celebration, and summarize the problems.

2. Matters Requiring Particular Attention

Several reminders remain necessary.

  • Strengthen coordination and communication

This is a mistake we ourselves made. Communication is useful. WeChat groups are useful. Work groups are even more useful. Reporting progress promptly is an even, even, even better habit. You probably noticed during the previous year that we created a work group for every event. Everyone has now also joined this “parent group.” Such chats matter. Things omitted during the handover, and mistakes made while carrying out the work, can often be corrected or prevented by reporting progress there.

Suppose four people bury themselves in an event plan and write that voice actors will be invited beginning on 20 May. Everyone thinks it is fine. When the date arrives, they discover that no time was reserved and every actor’s schedule is full. They are stuck. Had the plan been shared in the parent or work group, one of us would have noticed and pointed out the problem.

  • Arrange the timeline properly—this is the most important point and a painful lesson

Last year we were late with both the military-training warm-up broadcasts and the Clubs Fair, so the station began with relatively few members. This year, audio should go on air as soon as the term begins; programmes should ideally be running during military training. Immediately after the Clubs Fair, collect the freshmen’s available times and prepare the interviews. The faster, the better. We are competing for people.

At least two weeks must also be left between the preliminary and final rounds of a competition.

Once the timeline appears in the plan, it is still best to let us old fellows look over it.

Guests for New Voices and the Audio-Visual Evening should be invited as early as possible. That, in turn, requires the plan to be completed as early as possible. If circumstances permit, planning for New Voices can begin when the term starts or during the summer holiday, while planning for the Audio-Visual Evening can begin during the winter holiday.

  • Leave a record of the work

Keep records of everything you do: what was done, how far it progressed, what effect it produced, which part you handled, which parts were handled by others, and who collaborated with you. The record makes later tracing possible.

3. Work Beyond Delegation

Besides assigning tasks and writing the main plan, station assistants still need to prepare such material as competition rules, schedules, explanatory documents, and scoring sheets. Consult the files passed down to you—the ones shared in the group.

By the way, our cohort converted a great deal of written material into editable documents with a consistent format. There is also one more station assistant this year, so the workload should be slightly lighter.

At the very, very end: I still miss her. I miss her terribly. All I can do here is wish her happiness—or freedom. I miss her.
