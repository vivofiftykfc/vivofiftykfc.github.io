Ten Years

It is an afternoon in 2036. You, who are also me ten years from now, are sitting before a computer and typing this letter.

I thought for a long time before deciding to write it, because the act itself is rather strange. You want to say something to the person you were ten years ago, while knowing perfectly well that he will not listen. He cannot even manage what is immediately before him and is still talking about whether life is worth living. Why would he have the patience to hear the voice of some old fellow a decade away?

Still, I want to write. Some things are not said because anyone is expected to listen.

Let me begin with the warnings.

First, almost everything that now seems impossible to survive will pass. Notice that I said almost, not everything. Some things pass and are truly over, but the marks they leave do not disappear. You continue to live with them and gradually become accustomed to carrying the weight. This is not an attractive lesson, but it is true: people do not really forget pain; they merely learn to live beside it.

Second, most of your self-analysis is, frankly, a matter of walking in circles. You think you are deconstructing yourself, when in fact you are using thought to escape action. Put less politely, you are a daydreamer. You spend a great deal of time asking, “Why am I like this?” and “What should I do?” Yet some answers cannot be reached by thought alone. Even if you complete the reasoning in your head, it is useless unless you act. Only when you do something, strike the world, and feel it push back can you discover your own shape.

The most important thing I have learned in these ten years is this: you cannot solve, merely by thinking, the problems that thinking has produced.

Third, about her.

You are probably still immersed in the feeling that you will owe her for the rest of your life, convinced that you ruined everything. Ten years later, I can tell you that you did not ruin everything. You ruined that particular relationship. It is certainly nothing to celebrate, but you did not commit some unforgivable wrong. At the time, you simply did not yet possess the ability to maintain an intimate relationship. That is hardly surprising; no one had taught you.

What I want to say, however, is something else. You later meet some people and miss some others. Yet the feeling of “I miss her so much” returns on an ordinary afternoon without explanation, like a needle that has somehow entered your finger unnoticed. You cannot deal with it, and it does not disappear. All you can do is grow used to its presence.

To put it plainly, regret is not a problem to be solved. It is something one carries. Nevertheless, one must move forward—keep moving forward and do not turn back.

Then come the thanks.

The first thing I must thank you for is beginning to write all this. At the time, you wrote a blog for no grand reason. Certain words hurt when held inside, and there was nowhere to put them. You kept the habit for so long that writing became one of your principal ways of dealing with the world. Every word you now type helps me preserve a traceable coordinate system. Without those words, I could never recall what I was like at twenty. The sleepless nights, the inexplicable moments of obsession, and every “I don’t know” written at half past two in the morning—all of them have acquired meaning when viewed ten years later.

The second thing I must thank you for is not actually killing yourself.

I know that you considered it seriously. It was not the joking kind of thought; you weighed it in earnest. Thank you for not taking the final step. This is not because life ten years later becomes wonderfully good—it does not. The Earth still turns, and time continues to pass. But if you had ceased to exist, many things would genuinely have become impossible for both of us.

There is, for example, a summer evening when you sit with friends on a rooftop as the wind blows past. Perhaps you have brought two bags of craft beer, or perhaps just Corona. You sit there drinking without saying anything, and for a moment you think it is good to be alive. Had you not been there, that moment would not have existed. There are many such moments. None appears important, yet not one can be spared.

The third thing I must thank you for is recording honestly how bad your life was.

You did not pretend on the blog that everything was fine. You wrote about lying in a dormitory bed and scrolling through your phone until four in the morning; about ruining a relationship; about making a complete mess of your studies; about not knowing why you should remain alive—and then about continuing forward. These articles are embarrassing to read now. Yet precisely because they are sufficiently truthful, they are reliable. When you look back many years later, you are surprised to discover that the things you once found most shameful are exactly the things in you most worth preserving.

Honesty is the hardest quality to sustain, but you began practising it at twenty.

At this point the whole thing suddenly feels rather funny.

Here I am, a man ten years in the future, telling twenty-year-old you this and that as though I could provide an answer. In truth, I am like you: there are still many answers I do not know.

The only difference is that I can now accept the condition of “not knowing” more readily, move forward anyway, and make the ideas in my head take material form.

You now demand an answer to every question because an answer offers security. You depend heavily upon that security, much as you depended throughout the first eighteen years of life on the existence of a correct answer. It feels reassuring. Thus you pursue a general solution and place your faith in certainty. Unfortunately, the world is not like that. It is an AWGN channel in continual change. You will never understand it completely; you can only learn to coexist with it.

There is, however, one thing of which I am certain: ten years from now, you will not regret the time spent writing these pieces. Compared with the things done to satisfy other people’s expectations, and the energy spent feeding what you called the ego, this writing without any utilitarian purpose becomes the only real evidence that you once lived.

That is about it, I suppose.

The next time you cannot sleep, remember to pour yourself a glass of water.

One more thing: two questions are attached below.

Faced with a problem in a wholly unfamiliar field—for example, “How can the pollination routes of urban bee colonies be optimized?”—describe the steps by which you move from complete ignorance to the first workable hypothesis. Be as specific as possible.

Step 1: Define the problem

Do not rush to search for solutions. First determine what is actually to be done. What is the objective, and how can it be expressed more precisely in natural or mathematical language? How far must the solution go, and where are its boundaries?

Step 2: Break the problem down

Divide the large problem into smaller ones: data acquisition, mathematical modeling, algorithm selection, dynamic adjustment, and practical deployment. Deal with them one by one. In a team, the division also makes work easier to assign.

Step 3: Transfer by analogy

Actively search other fields for mature solutions. A “bee-colony route,” for example, may be compared with the traveling salesman problem in logistics. The key is to identify structural similarities beneath surface differences—route planning and resource allocation, in this case—so that an existing algorithmic framework or method of reasoning can be borrowed.

Step 4: Clarify the central solution further

Express the central method first in the simplest possible formula. This makes it clearer what we are doing and exposes both the principal variables and the real constraints: missing data, a bottleneck in computational complexity, or something else. Hidden difficulties are thus brought into view.

Step 5: Design an executable scheme

Use the simplest logic currently available to assemble the smallest process that can actually run. Then patch its largest defect. Continue iterating, layer by layer, until a better version emerges.

Step 6: Verify and iterate rapidly

Test the main hypothesis with a small experiment or simulated data, while actively seeking criticism from domain experts or opponents. Verification has two sides: it asks whether the scheme can run, and it introduces an opposing perspective to expose defects and omissions. Each round of feedback can act upon the product and improve the next iteration.

Suppose you discover that your entire life is a simulation and that you have just found a vulnerability in its console. Would you exploit it to become the “god” of this world, or use it to send the system a command saying, “I want to exit”? Why?

I would exit.

Once I knew about the vulnerability, I could no longer experience the world as I had before. The option of “pretending not to know” would have been destroyed at the instant of discovery.

There is a further problem. If this whole life were a simulation, our universe could be summarized by a theory. Everything would have an answer, but, being inside the system, I could never reach it. There is a vast sadness in that thought. Everything you investigate each day may have been calculated and prescribed by the system in advance.

Under that condition, what use would any of my abilities be? If the entire stage were false, every skill I had developed upon it would also fail to be fully real. Its meaning would be enclosed by the boundary of the system. I do not want merely to play a character in a game.

I am reminded of the people watching firelight in Plato’s cave; the resemblance to this question is close. The person who left the cave must have spent a long time doubting, confused, and unable to endure the light, yet he did not walk back inside. I hope to be such a person. I want to move forward and face reality. Whatever lies outside, I want to see it for myself. I refuse to sink into a falsehood once I know it to be false.
