The Final Three Kilometres from Knowledge to Action

0. A Piece of Dark Humour

Last night, I spent half an hour discussing with DeepSeek why I never execute an SOP after writing it. When the conversation ended, I did something perfectly consistent with my character: I organized the chat into a new SOP.

If you find that funny, congratulations. You have located one of the central loops in my mental life with remarkable precision.

Still, it is not entirely a joke. I have spent a long time colliding with the problem of execution, and recently, after all those collisions, something has finally loosened a little. At two in the morning, I therefore decided to record the process by which it loosened—even though I know perfectly well that the act of recording it is itself an ideal example of the problem I am describing.

Look. I have begun again. This is exactly how it works.

I. The Gap Between Knowledge and Action

The problem is hardly new. Wang Yangming said several centuries ago:

“To know and not to act is not yet to know.”

In modern language: if you think you understand something but do not act upon it, you never truly understood it.

The first time I read the sentence, I felt that he was scolding me. Later I discovered that he really was scolding me, and that the scolding was thoroughly deserved. Of course, saying that does not prove I am McDonald’s.

I had been making a subtle substitution, passing off the ability to summarize as knowledge. After completing something, I would review it and write an elegant SOP. At that instant the brain released a strong sense of completion and decided that the matter had been settled. But the satisfaction corresponded not to “the task is complete,” but to “the summary is complete.” These are separate accounts; the brain quietly settled them together.

To put it plainly, I did not write an SOP to guide my next action. I wrote it to give the previous action a respectable conclusion. Here the SOP was not an operating manual, but a congratulatory address—or an elegy.

The better the address or elegy was written, the less necessary genuine change became next time. The brain had already received the reward it wanted.

Oh, Wang Yangming said something else:

“Knowledge is the beginning of action; action is the completion of knowledge.”

Knowledge begins in action, and action completes knowledge. Notice the wording. He does not say, “First finish knowing, and then go and act.” He says that action is the final form of knowledge. In his system, knowledge that has not passed through action does not count as knowledge. It is a hallucination.

This has exactly the same structure as the “compression and expansion” discussed in my essay on the radar equation. An SOP is compression; execution is expansion. You performed the compression and never expanded it. A zip archive that is never extracted is about as useful as—well, never mind. It is useless.

Another layer is added by CVE-0003 from the audit report: the observer is absent. The self who writes the SOP and the self expected to execute it are not the same. The former is calm, rational, and looking down upon the whole system; the latter is sleepy, irritated, and wants to scroll through a phone. When the first writes the plan, he repeatedly forgets that the second does not want to move.

Wang Yangming called this “the separation of knowledge and action.” People treat knowing and acting as two things, he argued, even though “the moment a thought is aroused, it is already action.” The thought “I do not want to do this,” arising in your mind, is itself a kind of action. Knowledge and action were never separate; they have always been feeding one another. You imagine the problem as “I know what to do but cannot do it.” In reality, every action—including lying there and doing nothing—acts in return upon what you “know.”

Suppose you know that waking early is beneficial, and then turn off the alarm. The act of turning it off is itself knowledge: it teaches you more deeply that “getting up early is optional.” Every failure to act patches the cognitive system, except that the patch says, “This item may be skipped.”

The gap between knowledge and action is therefore not an obstacle to be crossed. It is a false problem. There is no gap. Everything you do corresponds precisely to what you truly “know” at that moment. Your résumé is judged not by the SOPs you wrote, but by what you actually did.

II. The Six Steps from Ten Years

In Ten Years, written a few days ago, I answered a question: when confronted with an entirely unfamiliar problem, how does one move from knowing nothing to proposing the first workable solution? I gave six steps:

  1. Define the problem.
  2. Break it down.
  3. Transfer a model by analogy.
  4. Clarify the central solution.
  5. Design an executable scheme.
  6. Verify and iterate rapidly.

There is nothing fundamentally wrong with the framework. When I read it again, it does describe how I think upon entering an unfamiliar field.

But it contains a fatal defect. It never answers one question: what makes me actually begin the first step?

The six steps describe the operation of a system that has already started. The real difficulty is getting the damned and beautiful machine to turn.

The central sentence of Ten Years was, “You cannot solve, merely by thinking, the problems that thinking has produced.” What is missing, then, is not a seventh step. It is a “Step Zero.”

III. Step Zero

Wang Yangming’s answer is radical. Knowledge and action were always one thing; if they appear separate, private desire has cut them apart. Remove that desire, and their unity returns naturally.

The claim may be philosophically sound. In practice, however, I need something more concrete—methods that bypass the analytical brain and make the hand begin to move.

Here are several small pieces of material I have collected.

1. Count Down from Five

When the thought “I should do X” appears, you have a window of about five seconds. After those seconds, the brain—an organ shaped by millions of years of evolution and primarily committed to conserving the two thousand calories it consumes each day—constructs a complete argument proving that now is not the ideal time and another five minutes of preparation would be wiser.

The countdown works very simply. By occupying working memory, it prevents the system of rationalization from starting in time. Count from five to one. At one, the body must move. Before a choice can be made, you have already boarded the vehicle.

2. Reduce the Friction of Beginning to an Absurd Degree

The greatest enemy is not “I cannot finish,” but “I do not want to begin.” The latter arises because “beginning” has been defined as something too heavy.

Not “write the paper,” but “open Word, type the title, and close it if you want.”
Not “review Principles of Communication,” but “open the book, read one line of a formula, and close it if you want.”
Not “organize the SOP,” but “create a Markdown file, type a title, save it, and declare today’s task complete.”

When action is reduced to something absurdly small, the brain decides that launching the procrastination system would cost more than the task itself.

This is more effective than one might expect. Once Word is open and the title has been typed, the system has warmed up; continuing is far more probable than beginning from a cold stop. Did not our great Sir Isaac tell us that a body at rest tends to remain at rest, while one in uniform motion tends to remain in uniform motion?

3. Decide in Advance

If most internal conflict occurs at the moment of asking whether to do something, eliminate that moment.

The night before, write down three things to be done the following morning. Specify when, where, what, and for how long. When the appointed time arrives, you are not “making a decision.” You are “executing a decision already made.”

The brain resembles any embedded processor: the fewer interruptions it receives, the fewer resources it spends handling them and the more steadily the main loop runs. “Should I do this?” is the most expensive interrupt in the system. Pre-decision moves that interrupt outside the main loop.

4. Let Action Precede Motivation

This is the most important of the five and the one I took longest to accept.

I used to believe that motivation must precede action: a feeling arrives → work begins → work is completed. The model appears perfectly natural, but reverses cause and effect.

When you type the final word of a blog post at two in the morning, did motivation exist before you began? No. It grew by itself somewhere in the middle.

Motivation does not produce action. Action produces motivation.

The proper sequence is therefore: begin → acquire the feeling while working → continue → finish. Do not become stuck upon “wanting to do it” as a prerequisite. It is not a prerequisite. It is an intermediate product.

5. Do It Three Times Before Writing the SOP

Of the five methods, this one is most directly prescribed for my condition.

I am too skilful at building a model before doing anything. Once the model is complete, the brain releases its satisfaction and the motivation leaks away. Anything completed once in imagination suffers a dramatic decline in marginal attraction when it must be done in reality.

So I impose a rule: before writing an SOP for anything, do it three times. The results may be terrible. Summarize them only after the third attempt.

Wang Yangming stated the philosophical foundation long ago: train through concrete affairs. Grind against the actual thing, not against an image inside the head. The usefulness of an SOP depends on the time spent “doing,” not the time spent “thinking.”

Good. Step Zero is complete.

Except that I have suddenly noticed what happened: I built another SOP about how to execute SOPs. There is a certain dark humour in this. Heh.

If it were a recursive function:

I do not know its termination condition. Perhaps none exists. I can only hope that the value returned by each iteration comes slightly closer than the last to the thing called “having done it.”

If Wang Yangming can see this from heaven, he is probably shaking his head. “I spent five hundred years saying that knowledge and action are one, and this boy translated it into five little tricks. Fine. At least it is better than doing nothing.”

IV. The Six Steps, Plus a Patch

Since I have already come this far, I may as well add one:

Stage The difficulty you imagine The actual difficulty Patch
① Define the problem Not knowing what to ask “I have not thought it through” becomes infinite delay Method 2: write one line; Method 1: count down from five
② Break it down Not knowing where to divide it Each subproblem becomes a black hole of internal conflict Method 3: decide in advance where the decomposition will stop
③ Transfer by analogy Finding no model to borrow “I do not know enough” turns research into another name for inaction Method 5: act three times before summarizing; Method 4: action produces motivation
④ Clarify the central solution The formula is not elegant enough Perfectionism repeatedly demolishes and rebuilds the scheme in the mind Method 3: decide beforehand what counts as “good enough”
⑤ Design an executable scheme Not knowing the order of the steps Treating “designing the scheme” as though it were execution Method 5: do it three times before writing the SOP; Method 1: begin in five seconds
⑥ Verify and iterate Not knowing how to obtain feedback Fear that verification will prove you wrong Method 4: action precedes motivation; only after acting have you earned the right to judge

The six-step method is sound. It merely assumes, without saying so, that whenever a step begins, you possess the ability to begin with it. For someone accustomed to substituting analysis for action, that assumption is precisely the problem requiring a solution.

V. Epilogue

After finishing, I read the piece again. To be honest, I will probably forget it immediately.

Not because it is badly written. On the contrary, because it is rather good, it may activate the brain’s standard procedure: “A good article! Completed! Release the reward!” The article itself may become the perfect excuse for failing to execute it.

That is what dealing with one’s analytical brain feels like. Every act of “self-examination” can become a new round of escape. There is no end, only deeper recursion. Recursion upon recursion—how abundant recursion is!

Still, I have to write.

Not because writing will solve the problem, but because “every word you type at the keyboard helps me maintain a traceable coordinate system.”

Today I wrote, “I may not execute anything in this article.” If the person I am three days from now really fails to execute it, at least the evidence is available: look, you predicted this three days ago. Perhaps the next encounter with the sentence will be slightly different.

If the person I am ten years from now sees it, he may laugh—

and then continue writing his own Final Three Kilometres Between Knowledge and Action.

That is about it, I suppose.
