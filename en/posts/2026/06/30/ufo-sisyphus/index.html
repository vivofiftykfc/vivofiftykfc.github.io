30 June 2026: The UFO versus Sisyphus

Here is the article itself.

Before you look at this flower, it and your mind return together to silence. When you come and look at it, its colour becomes clear all at once.

—Wang Yangming, Instructions for Practical Living (admittedly useful)

0. A Prelude

For me, the final two hundred days of high school were wasted. I simply could not study. A voice in my head kept asking me, “And what, darling, is the point of doing any of this?”

This was not the utilitarian question of whether the college entrance examination was useful. It was something else. You do one thing exceptionally well—and then what? You rank somewhere above one thousand in the province—and then what? You attend a good university and find a good job—and then what? Human civilization reaches its highest possible stage—and then what? The universe ends in heat death—and then what? In the end, everything comes to nothing.

Every layer of questioning arrived at the same wall. Naturally, the wall could not hand me an answer. Yet the wall itself was an answer: there was no “then.”

At the time, I still read Zhihu now and then, so I knew that this thing was called nihilism. It was not a passing doubt but nihilism as the ground colour of the mind: the realization that the universe had prepared no reason for your existence and that, before the second law of thermodynamics, the ultimate meaning of everything you did was merely borrowed.

Still, I had laid a fairly good foundation during the first two years of high school and obtained a respectable rank. I carried that ground colour into university. During the first three years there, all sorts of disorderly things collided with me—a failed relationship, a change of leadership at the station, upheaval at home, rises and falls in my studies. I tried reading some philosophy and psychology, and meanwhile turned the things knocked loose by these collisions into blog posts. One article followed another. By the sixteenth, they had somehow assembled themselves into a system that was at least internally coherent: compression and expansion, existentialism, instrumentalism, the absent observer, the unity of knowledge and action, recursive self-reference. Seven axes locked into one another. I even had Instructor D help anchor my philosophical and psychological coordinates.

Then I asked AI to summarize and organize the whole thing and gave it the name V1.

The logic of V1 was coherent, which was good. The problem was that it was too coherent. Things outside it—the clumsiness of other people, unreasonable love, tears that had never been analysed—seemed capable of being summarized and classified, yet could never enter my world in their entirety. I had made my iron room far too comfortable.

There was another question: what, exactly, were my values? V1 could not answer it. That was interesting. Seven axes, seventeen blog posts, tens of thousands of words: I could explain how to understand the world and handle all sorts of events, but not what was worth doing.

I said I did not know. I said nihilism was my ground colour. I said I had tried to resist it with the existentialist claim that “I choose, and I give things meaning.” Yet it was equally clear that I could not remain Sisyphus forever, permanently maintaining that posture of struggle. It was fucking exhausting. Most of the time, I was not choosing. I was merely responding, allowing biological instinct, pressure to survive, worldly expectations, other people’s opinions, and deadlines to decide what I did. Or, put differently, I was being pushed along by them. I imagined that I was pushing the stone. Often, the stone was actually rolling by itself while I ran beside it, writing a rather good explanatory report about the running and reassuring myself that, yes, I could at least keep up.

Then I remembered the period when I pursued her.

At the moment I chose to pursue her, it was not because I lacked a relationship to relieve my boredom, nor for some other reason. I simply saw how lovely she was, saw her wounds, and wanted to do something for her. My body moved by itself.

In other words, my brain did not first run a simulation and then issue an execution order to my body. At one particular moment, one particular girl was sitting there. I looked at her, and then I moved. The subject was not me. The subject was her.

And then? Then I quickly slipped backwards. I returned to the Alpha version running on bare metal—anxious, parasitic, mistaking need for love and emotional dumping for sharing. After the break-up, pain forced reflection, but I merely slipped into V1.0—a binary classifier, calculating the correct dose before exposing myself. Clearly, losing her had a certain historical inevitability. Just as clearly, my previous version could never have held a good relationship when one arrived.

But looking back, how did that moment of pursuing her actually operate—that half-second before Ti had even had time to boot?

This article exists to make sense of that question.

I. Deficit-Driven

First, let me explain the underlying algorithm of V1. In one sentence: whatever is missing, build something to compensate for it.

Why did its epistemology centre on “compression and expansion”? Because the world contains too much information for the brain to process. You therefore compress it, choose a few variables from an infinitely complicated input, and construct an equation with a useful mathematical tool. The radar equation is uglier than F=ma not because it is less exact, but because it omits fewer things. A formula is not truth. It is a compressed archive.

Why did its theory of relationships take the form of a V1.0 transaction system? Because in the Alpha period, you had no boundaries, did not know how to get along with people, and blew relationships apart. You therefore built a listen-for-needs-and-supply model. Before the other person had finished three sentences, it classified the need: an emotional request called mirroring plus extension, while a solution request called analysis plus advice. This was undeniably an improvement on Alpha. At the very least, you were no longer hurting people.

Why did its theory of practice contain those five mechanisms? Because you wrote SOPs and never followed them. So you invented the five-second countdown, reducing the friction of starting, pre-decisions, action before motivation, and doing something three times before writing its SOP. You used a cognitive strategy to compensate for a deficit in executive function.

Pull the whole matter one level higher. The seventeen essays and seven axes of the entire V1 philosophical system were, in essence, another deficit-driven program. I was confused, so I needed to put myself in order. I did not know who I was, so I built a coordinate system. Once I had organized myself and constructed the coordinates, surely everything would be fine.

The deficit-driven constitution has a distinctive feature: the subject is always “I,” and the starting point is always “I should.” I should socialize. I should study. I should execute. Then a model is built to translate “should” into “how.” Once the model is complete, the brain releases a sense of completion—the gap has been explained, though the gap itself remains.

The most fatal weakness of deficit-driven action is not that it fails to work. It is that it cannot be sustained.

Existentialism says that the world has no meaning and that human beings give it meaning themselves. Sisyphus pushes his stone. His position is hopeless, but he is willing to imagine himself happy. Sounds hard-core, does it not? The problem is that after three days of pushing, the passion remains. After three weeks, you can still hold on. After three months, one afternoon you suddenly ask yourself, “Why was I supposed to give this meaning again?”

Then the bed says: come and lie down.

So you lie down. The prime mover of deficit-driven action is “I choose,” but the force behind “I choose” is powered by will. You must keep charging yourself, keep telling yourself, “This matters because I chose it,” and keep maintaining a posture of combat. Honestly, I cannot do it. I can write a blog post at two in the morning proving the unity of knowledge and action, then silence the alarm the following morning.

Put bluntly, the existentialist Sisyphus is a masochist who asks himself every day, “Why am I pushing this stone?” and then forces himself to answer, “Because I chose to.” He can go and sit at the same table as Ah Q.

II. Call-Driven

But when I pursued her, I plainly was not like that. Had I been, I would never have won her over in the first place.

I built no model. I ran no need classifier and calculated no rate of return. I merely saw her—her loveliness and her wounds—and wanted to protect her.

The subject here was not “I.” It was “she.”

There was no “I should” in that moment. She was there, her loveliness and wounds were there, and my body responded first. My brain caught up afterwards and wrote the comments.

Nor was this simply obeying emotion. Emotion is the part that reason cannot process. In that moment before Ti booted, neither reason nor emotion was online. I was still in a state of perception.

Someone in the history of philosophy has described this very clearly.

Max Scheler called it the logic of the heart. Value, he argued, is not “assigned” after rational criticism. It is directly “given” at the moment of contact with something. When you see her wounds, you do not first prove that wounds deserve protection and only then respond. You simply know. The heart has an order and structure of its own. It is neither the disorder of emotion nor a deduction of reason.

Wang Yangming called it innate knowing. “Before you look at this flower, it and your mind return together to silence. When you come and look at it, its colour becomes clear all at once.” A calling has roughly this structure. There are many people and many things we do not need to understand. In themselves, they have no colour. Their colour is something that lights up directly within us when we encounter them. Apart from the object, apart from your mind and mine, apart from the act of observation, that colour does not exist.

Mencius put it more directly: “Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion.” Not a conclusion that rescue is proper after completing an analysis. Suddenly: before thought has time to begin, the heart has already moved.

The moment when I pursued her, the moment in high school when I stared at a physics formula and thought, “This thing is fucking beautiful,” the moment when I typed the last word at two in the morning, looked up, and found daylight outside the window—all had this structure. They did not arise from deficit, from “I should,” or from “I lack.” They were call-driven. Something lit up before Ti had switched on.

In V1, I said that pursuing her lay somewhat beyond my system. Looking back, that was far too modest. She did not transcend the system. She was the point from which the entire system began. Ti was built only after I had found and lost her. I used my Ti to audit why I had answered so badly. My entire V1 framework was not a high platform from which I surveyed the world. It was a monument erected after she left. In my first blog post, I wrote that the SOP was an elegy rather than an operating manual. Now it seems that all seventeen articles were elegies: for the Alpha version of myself, for the people I had hurt, and for the twenty-year-old who wanted to protect her but did not know how.

The elegies merely became better written and more self-contained, until I slowly forgot whom they mourned.

Oh, right: one calling is not necessarily the same as another.

This was a crucial patch from a conversation a few days ago. I said that the bed calls me too. Does that count as a calling? Yes. But what happens afterwards? My mind grows foggier, I want to move even less, and I dislike myself more.

And after answering the call to pursue her, finishing a blog post at dawn, or being struck in high school by the beauty of a formula? I can feel myself becoming sharper, know more clearly who I am, and find another marker added to my coordinates. Plainly, I have become better.

Moreover, the distinction cannot be made in the moment. From inside the event, you cannot tell which thing is a calling and which is inertia disguised as love. The difference appears only afterwards: collide with it → rebound → look back and audit → “Wait. What I took for a calling last time was only my attempt to escape another, harder calling.”

Notice that this audit mechanism is not a concept I have newly invented today. It lies scattered through several articles in V1. At the time, I simply had not connected them.

In A Brief Note (November 2025), I typed one sentence: “Sometimes I wonder whether ‘self-reflection’ is itself a form of sweet self-deception.” That was the first time I realized that the self you reflect upon is never the self presently doing the reflecting.

In The Audit Report (June 2026), I assigned the phenomenon the number CVE-0003: the observer is never inside his own equation.

In The SOP Article (June 2026), I wrote the entire predicament as recursive code. Each level believes it has solved the problem of the previous level, yet every level is doing the same thing.

III. Blind Spots

Multiple callings. Life has never been single-threaded. Several interrupts may arrive at the same moment. Last time, I chose badly: I let her down, gave my family no satisfactory account, and ruined my grades as well. Yet whether a choice has failed cannot be known at the instant of choosing. The cruelty of multiple callings is that whichever one you answer, some part will fail. For the rest of your life, you may feel indebted to the thing you did not choose.

If anything can help a little at the moment of choice, perhaps it is only this: ask which unanswered calling will still hurt in ten years. This is not regret. Regret is a normal condition of life and time can wear it away. Pain is knowing that I should have gone and did not. Pain is not erased.

A calling does not need you. She no longer needs you. She has left. The person you wanted to protect now has other support and another life. At the time, I instinctively clung to the relationship like a child. Unfortunately, when a calling does not need you, holding it tighter changes nothing. But viewed from the other direction, the fact that she no longer needs you does not mean the calling never existed. The moment in which you wanted to protect her was real. You answered it, failed it, and were changed by it. Perhaps that is enough.

“Only the fresh breeze over the river and the bright moon between the mountains: the ear receives the one as sound, the eye encounters the other as colour. No one forbids us to take them, and their use can never exhaust them. They are the Creator’s inexhaustible treasury, shared for the delight of you and me.” Something like that, perhaps.

Endurance. A choice may begin with a calling, but what happens after the calling fades? What is needed then is Si, a traceable coordinate system. The blog you are writing is an example. You wrote today, yesterday, and one year ago. These words gradually condense into a gentle system of coordinates. The system itself becomes a mild gravitational force drawing you forwards. It is not passionate, but it is stable. Once the passion of a calling has passed, the order of reality is needed to guide you on.

The fallow interval. This is the most dangerous part. Nothing shines; no one calls you. Such periods make it easiest to slide back into bed, into a rubbish relationship, or into a rubbish job. The answer is not to “pull yourself together and look for a calling.” That merely returns to the existentialist posture of struggle. The answer is clumsier: go and sit in places where a calling once appeared. Open the Markdown file, even if you write only the title. Sit on the rooftop, even if there is no wind. The aim is not to chase a calling but to make it easier for one to find you. It may not come today or tomorrow. It may not come for the whole week. Is that acceptable? A wise farmer understands that the field must sometimes lie fallow.

IV. Companions

After writing the preceding sections, I began to wonder whether anyone had travelled the same road. Let me summarize a few people below.

Fernando Pessoa: better not read him yet. He wrote The Book of Disquiet, worked as a clerk in Lisbon, never married, never left the city, and spent every evening in a rented room writing inner monologues under different names. His central condition is structurally identical to mine: live → observe oneself living → discover that observation has replaced living → write down that discovery too. He represents one possible future for me: a coordinate system built extraordinarily high and dense, but containing no one except myself. Clearly, that is one direction my future could take.

Dostoevsky’s Underground Man: he analyses every motive, finds an uglier one beneath it, and then discovers that the act of “finding the ugly motive” is itself another performance. He remains trapped in rumination. He once said, “I am not even a man—I am a pipe.”

Yukio Mishima. Sun and Steel may be more important to me than any work of philosophy. Mishima was a man with an overdeveloped brain. He lived among books and words from childhood, then suddenly began weight training and kendo at thirty and turned himself into a muscular man. He wrote, “Words dissolve the body.” The moment you analyse something through language, you are no longer inside it. You have leapt outside and taken up a position apart. He did not train for health. He discovered that only when his muscles ached and his heart rate rose beyond the threshold at which the analysing brain could operate did he temporarily escape the siege of words. He was not using the body to flee thought. He was fleeing the cycle in which thought replaced living.

Simone Weil. Gravity and Grace is not a thick book. It consists entirely of short fragments and maxims and is suited to opening at two in the morning. Her ground colour is very dark, and her honesty is cruel. She went one step further and did not protect herself with cold humour. “There is something like gravity in the world that drags the soul downwards, towards comfort and the self. There is also something human beings cannot produce by themselves—grace. It strikes them from outside and pulls them upwards.” But perhaps she did not quite know how to reassemble herself after being broken. She never had a real love affair, never kept a cat, and had few friends. She returned everything in advance to an absolute beyond gravity.

Ted Chiang. Trained in computer science, he uses scientific ideas—variational principles, entropy, free will—to write science-fiction stories, yet the final question in each is human. If we know everything, can we still love another person? If time does not flow, does free will still exist?

V. Epilogue

One year ago, Pavlov’s Twenty-Eight Dog complained about having no energy to work. A Brief Note lay dead in a dormitory and wrote that self-reflection was a sweet form of self-deception.

One year later, on the same computer, this article appeared.

Its relationship to V1 is this. V1 began with the question “How should I understand the world?”—compression and expansion, instrumentalism, the absent observer, the unity of knowledge and action. It placed epistemology at the bottom as its foundation. This article—or the part about values that I intend to call V2—turns that foundation over and replaces it with “What is calling me?”

This does not mean that I have left deficit-driven action behind forever. It remains alive. Yet deficit alone is plainly insufficient. It grows tired and loses power. A calling, however, is energy from outside. It may come from one particular person, one particular formula, or one particular sea breeze. I need only walk towards it.

Of course, the bed may defeat me again tomorrow. The fallow interval may last a long time. The next calling may appear next month, next year, or at some unknown point.

That is all right. The coordinate system is still turning. The Markdown file remains open. The barbecue stall has not closed. Everyone is still alive.

Sisyphus pushed his stone for a lifetime. One night, he looked up and saw a UFO in the sky. He could not tell whether it was an alien spacecraft or a hallucination brought on by exhaustion. But perhaps he would find that, beneath the UFO’s attraction, the stone in his hands had become a little lighter.

That is probably all.
