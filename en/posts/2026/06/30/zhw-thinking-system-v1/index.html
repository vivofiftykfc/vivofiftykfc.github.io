2026-06-30-ZHW's Framework of Thought v1

I write these words not to seek an answer, but merely to confirm that I have passed through this life truthfully, contradictorily, and passionately.

—The Unknown, September 2025

0. The Point of Departure

From last May until now, I have written sixteen reflective articles. At first, I simply typed whatever came to mind when I could not sleep. Gradually, however, it became a habit: whenever I could not understand something, I opened an md file and kept typing until early morning. As I continued, certain recurring patterns began to emerge.

This article is an attempt to organize them in one place. With the help of deepseek-v4-pro, I extracted the ideas that repeatedly appeared in those sixteen articles, examined their relationships, and asked whether they could form something relatively complete. I am not claiming that I intend to construct an eternal palace of thought; nothing eternal is possible. I merely want to understand where I have arrived.

This report was summarized and generated with the help of Instructor D, so its AI flavor will be obvious.

Calibration Note, July 11, 2026: This article is preserved as a snapshot of my thinking at that time. I will not go back and replace every old judgment with today’s answer. After rereading the complete blog, I found that the labels in Section IV—including MBTI, Kegan, Perry, Dąbrowski, attachment, and ADHD—were stated with excessive certainty. At most, they are explanatory metaphors, not assessments or diagnoses. Phrases such as “weak executive function” and “emerging Fe” also turned local difficulties into claims about general ability. A more accurate description is that vague tasks requiring autonomous initiation tend to trigger analysis and procrastination, whereas I can often complete complex work when roles, users, deadlines, and deliverables are explicit. Unstructured intimate relationships are easily taken over by analysis, but my abilities in organization, cooperation, explanation, and handover are not weak. I have not deleted the original text. It remains as an example of how a model makes mistakes and is later patched by reality.

I. The Path of Evolution

Placed along a timeline, the sixteen articles can be divided roughly into four stages:

The four stages appear to form an upgrade: from not knowing what I was doing, to knowing, to constructing a model of “knowing,” and finally to discovering that the model did not work and therefore adding an execution module. On closer inspection, however, they are not four different problems. They are one problem expressed in four different languages.

The stage of existential anxiety asked, “Why should I live?” The stage of relational upheaval asked, “Why can I not get along with people?” The stage of systematic modeling said, “So the way I understand the world has a structure of its own.” The stage of practical implementation replied, “A structure is still useless. I remain motionless.”

Each stage attempted to solve the problem of the preceding one, only to discover that it had created a new problem. The new and old problems shared the same underlying structure: build a model, believe it, discover that it is insufficient, and build another. Only the language became increasingly complicated—from “a reason to stay alive,” to “compression and unfolding,” to “CVE vulnerability numbers,” and finally to a “debug toolkit for the unity of knowledge and action.”

This cycle itself is the recursive self-reference discussed later. But there is no need to rush. Let us take the threads one by one.

II. Seven Axes

① Epistemology: Compression and Unfolding

Human beings cannot directly encounter “the whole of reality.” The brain automatically compresses infinitely complex input into a limited number of variables, establishes relations among them, and produces predictions. A formula is compression; a disciplinary system is compression; a judgment about another person is also compression. Unfolding is the reverse operation: restoring the boundary conditions, chains of derivation, and omitted elements behind a formula. To have truly internalized something is to move freely between compression and unfolding.

This axis came mainly from The Radar Equation and Love. Starting from that article, I followed the thread through five levels: from the complexity of the radar equation, to a cognitive model of compression and unfolding, to the fractal structure of knowledge, to an isomorphic transfer into interpersonal cognition, and finally to the derivation of a method of learning. It ended with instrumentalism: formulas are not truth, but compressed expressions of reality. Mathematics works not because the world is made of mathematics, but because human beings invented mathematics and then used it to frame the world. What could be framed remained; what could not was discarded.

Later, The SOP Article added a decisive point: compression does not mean that the task has been completed. An SOP is compression; execution is unfolding. My former problem was that once compression had been completed, my brain released the feeling of completion, although unfolding had never taken place. Unfolding requires initiation. The unity of knowledge and action is essentially the dynamic-system version of compression and unfolding: knowledge is compression, action is unfolding, and the two cannot be separated. Compression without unfolding is merely a binary file that has never been executed.

② Ontology: The Observer Is Always Absent

You model other people, the world, and yourself, yet “the you who is modeling at this very moment” can never appear in the equation. This is not a forgotten variable; it is a logical impossibility. An observer cannot completely observe itself at the same time.

The idea first appeared at the end of The Radar Equation and Love. After working through instrumentalism and Kant/Wang Yangming, I concluded that no account of “objectivity” could remove the observer from the equation. Audit Report assigned the phenomenon the number CVE-0003: you can analyze a friend’s condition with precision, infer why a former partner left, and write an entire audit report for your interpersonal model, yet the living, breathing you is absent from your equations.

At the time, I called it a fatal vulnerability. Looking back, it is better understood as an architectural property. Any system capable of observing itself must contain a blind spot that it cannot observe. No camera, however good, can photograph its own lens.

The you who writes an SOP is not the you who executes it. The you who audits a system is not the you being audited. The you who writes “how to improve executive ability” is not the you lying in bed. The former overlooks the whole situation with calm rationality; the latter is tired, irritated, and wants to scroll through a phone. When the former makes a plan, it never includes the latter’s “I do not want to move” as a variable in the equation—not because it forgot, but because the variable cannot be written in. As soon as you begin writing, “I may not want to move,” you return to the observer’s position, while “the you who does not want to move” remains outside.

This does not need to be repaired. What matters is knowing that it is there and leaving an error term in every judgment.

In a completely different context, Wang Yangming said something similar: before you look at the flower, the flower and the mind both return to silence. This does not mean that the flower does not exist. It means that its color becomes manifest only when illuminated by the mind.

③ Theory of Relationships: Alpha → v1.0 → What Cannot Be Modeled

My understanding of interpersonal relationships passed through three stages, a process recorded in full in Audit Report.

Alpha—Parasitism. There were no boundaries. Anxiety was transferred to the other person, and “need” was treated as “love.” The receiver performed detection but no extraction: once it detected a negative signal, the entire system overheated; when it detected a positive signal, it continued operating in power-saving mode. The output was not communication but dumping. It did not converge. Positive feedback led to explosion.

v1.0—Transaction. Once I realized that relationships required structure, I built a listening-to-needs and provision model. During the first few sentences spoken by the other person, the system classified the demand—emotional support or a practical solution—and then called the corresponding template. This was an improvement over Alpha, for at least it no longer hurt people in the same way. Yet it compressed every human being into two labels, treated each interaction as a settlement period, and secretly transformed “helping others” into “verifying whether I had become better.”

Audit—Acknowledging Vulnerabilities. I recognized the problems of v1.0—CVE-0001, classifier misjudgment; CVE-0002, withdrawal upon inequality; CVE-0004, strategic use of vulnerability; and CVE-0005, the savior verification loop—but did not immediately rush to construct v2.0. A “perfect version” is a concept that exists only because too many conditions have been omitted. It cannot run in a complex real system. Genuine connection requires what cannot be modeled: awkwardness, recklessness, and action without calculating returns. By the time one tries to model it, it has already run.

I later discovered that this three-stage model applied not only to interpersonal relationships. Looking back at Briefly (November 2025), one passage says, “My acts of ‘taking notes’ and ‘writing blogs’ are probably merely another way of wasting my life.” That was Alpha directed toward myself: an unbounded outpouring of emotion, except that the recipient was not a former partner but me. The Radar Equation and Audit Report represented v1.0 directed toward myself: managing myself with models and believing that building the model was equivalent to changing. In The SOP Article, v1.0 no longer worked on me either: none of the SOPs I wrote were ever carried out.

The two lines share exactly the same structure. The conclusion is that not only does “genuine connection require what cannot be modeled,” but genuine self-management also requires what cannot be modeled—a little recklessness, and a beginning that does not calculate the cost.

④ Theory of Practice: Defense and Offense

The practical theory in the early articles was defensive. Writing a blog was breathing: typing out the thoughts turning in my mind so that I could maintain a traceable coordinate system. Shutting down was a brake: after thinking enough, I should eat barbecue, neither look at my phone nor calculate mentally, but simply look into the other person’s eyes. I had to permit myself not to model. The temperature of the wind, the sound of a file being saved at two in the morning, and a roommate’s snoring belonged to no system. Life itself possesses the final authority to explain any theory about life.

Later, The SOP Article added an offensive side through five concrete mechanisms:

  • Five-second countdown: when the thought “I should do X” appears, count from five to one. At one, the body must move. The countdown occupies working memory, preventing the rationalization system from starting in time.
  • Reduce initiation friction to an absurd degree: the task is not “write the paper,” but “open Word, type the title, and close it if necessary.” It must be so small that the brain considers it unworthy of launching the procrastination program.
  • Pre-decision: write down three tasks for tomorrow the night before—at what time, in what place, doing what, and for how long. When the time arrives, execute without deciding. The fewer the interruptions, the more stable the main loop.
  • Action precedes motivation: motivation does not produce action; action produces motivation. Motivation is not a prerequisite but an intermediate product. On none of the nights when I finished a blog at two in the morning did I begin because I “felt an urge to write.”
  • Do it three times before writing an SOP: once something has been completed in the mind, its marginal attraction declines sharply the next time it must be done. An SOP is produced by collisions, not designed in advance. Beneath every abandoned proposal in the voiceprint-lock project—GMM-UBM, CMVN, direct GPIO connection—lies a corpse.

The central proposition is: know when to model and when to shut the model down and act directly.

⑤ Aesthetics: Dry Humor as Epistemic Humility

Dry humor is doubt about one’s own model compressed into a witty sentence.

When analysis has gone too far and the model has been built too high, a joke releases the pressure. It is the only part of the system that cannot be modeled, because once it is modeled, it ceases to be funny. “The essence of humanity must be McDonald’s.” “This receiver is an absolute piece of garbage.” “See, I have started again. That is how it is.”

It is also the only way not to overflow the stack during infinite recursion. Ordinary recursion continues calling itself until memory is exhausted. ⑥ Theory of Action: The Unity of Knowledge and Action

This problem is older than every other axis. The opening sentence of Pavlov’s Twenty-Eight Dog (May 2025) is “Recently, I never seem to have the energy to work.” It used a behaviorist positive-feedback loop to explain why my motivation collapsed after entering university. Briefly (November 2025) was more direct: “Do I really not know what I should do? Then why can I not do it?” Half a Year (May 2026) said, “I need the ability to act. Whatever I say or think, I must first do it instead of leaving it to consume me within my inner world.”

After troubling me for an entire year, the problem finally received a name and a set of tools in The SOP Article.

The central proposition comes from Wang Yangming. He said, “To know and not act is not truly to know.” If you believe you understand but do not act, then you never truly understood. When I first read the sentence, it felt as though he were scolding me. Later I realized that he was indeed scolding me, but he was right. He added, “Knowledge is the beginning of action; action is the completion of knowledge.” Cognition begins action, and action completes cognition. Action does not follow after cognition has ended; action is cognition’s final form. Cognition that has not been tested through action is not cognition but illusion.

This is entirely isomorphic with the epistemological axis: compression without unfolding is wasted compression; knowledge without action is wasted knowledge. It is also isomorphic with the ontological axis: the separation of knowledge and action is the active version of the absent observer. The you who “knows” and the you who “must execute” are not the same person.

All five practical mechanisms share the same underlying logic: do not allow “thought” enough time to intercept “action.” The five-second countdown occupies working memory; reducing friction lowers the initiation threshold; pre-decision pays the cost of decision in advance; action before motivation reverses the causal chain; and doing something three times deprives the analytical mind of the opportunity to build a model.

⑦ Recursive Self-Reference: The Limit of Metacognition

This axis may be the most characteristic of me in the entire framework.

Its seed was planted at the end of Briefly (November 2025), at two in the morning, just after a delivery scooter had passed beneath the building: “Sometimes I wonder whether ‘self-reflection’ is itself a sweet form of self-deception.”

In Audit Report, the seed became an argument: “This audit report is itself a paradox. It audits a system in the language of systems and dismantles modeling through modeling. Writing a vulnerability report with a CVE number and calling it ‘self-knowledge’ is itself an extremely v1.0 thing to do.”

In The SOP Article, it became code—“I have built another damned ‘SOP about how to execute an SOP’”—and a more honest confession: “I may not follow a single instruction written in this article.”

This is neither humility nor self-abandonment. It is a structural observation: any analysis of oneself can be exposed by an analysis at a higher level. When you write “how to improve executive ability,” that act has already entered the pattern of replacing execution with writing. When you write, “self-reflection is a sweet form of self-deception,” the sentence is itself a sweet form of self-deception: reflection has just been completed, and dopamine has arrived again.

It is not a problem that can be “solved.” It is a structure. Every sufficiently complex formal system contains propositions that can neither be proved nor disproved from within that system. A system of self-knowledge works in the same way. At any given level, it is self-consistent, yet there is always a proposition—“I am executing the SOP I wrote”—that can never be decided at that level.

Recursive self-reference is therefore neither failure nor defect. It is the safety limiter of the whole framework, preventing any model from proclaiming itself the final truth. Admitting that recursion does not terminate does not make recursion worthless. The return value of each iteration may indeed come closer than the preceding one to the act of “actually doing it.”

III. Philosophical Coordinates: Whom Do I Resemble?

Here I locate the seven axes within existing philosophical traditions. This is not an attempt to attach myself to famous figures; a second-year engineering student has no right to do so. It is an effort to find reference points, understand which road I have accidentally entered, and see who else stands nearby.

The Closest: Later Wittgenstein

Our ways of doing philosophy are almost completely isomorphic. Both begin with engineering or technical training. Both first construct a system and then dismantle it. Both believe that the most important things cannot be said but only shown. Both write with numbers and sections, only to interrupt the argument halfway with an ordinary sentence. Both possess an instinct to “say what can be said and then remain silent.”

The final line of Wittgenstein’s Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus reads, “Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.” He then stopped doing philosophy for many years. The Radar Equation ends with “It is probably more useful than remembering the radar equation itself.” The SOP Article ends with “Next time, certainly.” Functionally, there is no difference. Each acknowledges that language has reached its limit and that anything further would be nonsense.

There is one difference: Wittgenstein had no dry humor. He was too serious, serious to the point of suffering. A person capable of writing pseudocode inside his own recursive dilemma will always be slightly less tense. An imprecise but useful formulation would be: ZHW ≈ Wittgenstein meeting a northeastern Chinese barbecue cook.

Ultimately, However

This approach is not especially common in the history of philosophy—not because it is unusually profound, but because of its materials. It uses the sampling theorem and the radar equation to discuss the same old questions. Every concept grew from a professional course: information theory (compression and unfolding), signal processing (denoising/detection), embedded systems (bootloader/interrupt/deadlock), the radar equation (RCS/accuracy/boundary conditions), and probability theory (AWGN/random processes). None was borrowed from a philosophy book.

A wild system-builder who has not been contaminated by a humanities department.

There is, of course, a risk: one may believe that one is thinking originally while merely restating, in private language, what others have already explained clearly. Every time I “discover” something new, I look back and find that someone reported the issue centuries earlier. Yet this is not necessarily bad. It means that the road is not entirely wild; there are signs along it. Beneath every step lies one of my own corpses rather than merely another person’s conclusion.

One final self-audit: the classifications above—“whom do I resemble?”—cannot escape recursive self-reference either. Once the classification is finished, dopamine is released. I think, “Oh, so I resemble Wittgenstein,” and stop thinking. Mark the risk. Continue to use the model, but do not fully believe it.

IV. Psychological Coordinates: What Kind of Cognitive Architecture Do I Have?

The philosophical coordinates answer “whom do my ideas resemble?” The psychological coordinates answer “how was my mind built?” The former concerns content and the latter something closer to hardware. Neither coordinate system is subordinate to the other, but placing them together reveals several interesting relations.

MBTI: INTP (Logician)

Ti (Dominant Function)—Introverted Thinking

This means more than merely “being logical.” The defining feature of Ti is constructing an internally consistent logical framework and using it to explain everything. The seven axes are pure products of Ti. They were not accepted from an external authority, but derived layer by layer from experiences of running into walls. CVE vulnerability numbers, the formalization of compression and unfolding, and the pseudocode of a recursive function are all ways in which Ti translates the world into an internal language.

Ne (Auxiliary Function)—Extraverted Intuition

This is cross-disciplinary pattern recognition: radar equation → love → epistemology; embedded systems → unity of knowledge and action → SOP paradox; Gödel’s incompleteness → recursive self-reference. Ne is what digs underground channels between different pools. The articles are filled with statements such as “this reminds me of…” and “this is completely isomorphic with that…”—that is Ne making leaps. The fractal structure of knowledge and multiscale modeling are structures built by Ti with material borrowed from Ne.

Si (Tertiary Function)—Introverted Sensing

Si has a precise name in this system: a traceable coordinate system. I write blogs not to produce content but to maintain an archive: “Every word I am now typing helps me preserve a traceable coordinate system.” Si is the function that looks through old photographs, and writing is my manual implementation of it. Ten Years is an extreme expression of Si: looking back from 2036 at the self of 2026 and connecting every marked point along the timeline.

Fe (Inferior Function)—Extraverted Feeling

The entire arc from Alpha to v1.0 to audit is a history of the development of inferior Fe. Alpha is completely undeveloped Fe: parasitism, emotional dumping, and treating need as love. v1.0 uses Ti to simulate Fe through a binary classifier. Audit acknowledges that Ti cannot simulate Fe: “genuine connection requires what cannot be modeled.” At present, I have only reached the point of admitting that Ti is insufficient, not the point at which Fe can operate naturally.

That is why the type is INTP rather than INTJ. INTJ uses Te, or extraverted thinking, to build systems in order to change the external world. INTP uses Ti to build systems for internal consistency. Writing SOPs without executing them and constructing models only to dismantle them are forms of Ti-Ne entertaining itself. Te says, “Stop thinking and do it.” Ti replies, “Wait. I first need to consider whether this model of ‘how to execute’ is itself complete.”

Stage of Functional Development: Ti-Ne is an excessively powerful engine; Si is being manually installed; Fe remains in beta. The entire philosophical framework is essentially Ti-Ne writing a user manual for Si and Fe—but no matter how detailed the manual becomes, it cannot replace booting the system.

Naturally, the MBTI label cannot escape recursive self-reference either. Once classified, I may think, “So I am an INTP and poor execution is a factory setting,” and lie even flatter. A model is a useful tool, but it must not become an excuse for inaction.

INTP Development: Upper-Intermediate, but Extremely Uneven

INTP development lacks an academic framework as strict as Kegan’s. Communities and type psychology nevertheless offer a rough criterion: do not look only at the strength of Ti-Ne, but at the balance among all four functions. INTP development is naturally uneven. Ti-Ne takes off first, while Si and Fe chase from behind.

Ti-Ne: Advanced, Perhaps Excessively Advanced.

This is not praise. Ti-Ne has developed here to the point of “overfitting.” It can explain everything, including why it cannot explain certain things. The seven axes, CVE numbers, compression and unfolding, and recursive self-reference would represent top-level Ti-Ne output in any INTP.

The problem lies precisely here. Ti-Ne is so overdeveloped that it takes over the work of other functions. It wants to manage Si’s work—the five practical mechanisms are drivers written by Ti for Si—and Fe’s work as well—the v1.0 transaction system is an attempt by Ti to simulate Fe. It refuses to admit that some matters lie outside its jurisdiction. A healthy Ti should say, “I do not need to analyze this; I can simply do it.” The Ti here says, “Wait, let me first analyze why I analyze too much,” and then produces The SOP Article.

Ti-Ne Assessment: Extremely high, but it has become too clever and now needs restriction rather than further feeding.

Si: Lower-Intermediate, Under Manual Installation.

Within this system, Si corresponds to the “traceable coordinate system”: recording, archiving, and maintaining continuity along a timeline. Writing the blog is itself an enormous Si behavior, and sustaining it for a year is evidence. Yet in daily execution, Si has not been internalized as an automatic program. At least three of the five practical mechanisms—pre-decision, doing something three times, and reducing friction—are essentially attempts to use Ti to write scripts for Si. Si is not running on its own; Ti is manually driving it. It resembles hardware with a completed driver that must still be reloaded each time because it has not been compiled into the kernel.

The sign that Si has truly been internalized is that there is no need to count 5-4-3-2-1: at the proper time, the action simply begins. There is no need to write “review GPIO multiplexing at 8:40 tomorrow morning” the night before; the body sits down at the desk by itself. I cannot yet do this, but at least I am consciously training it.

Si Assessment: Intermediate. Its importance is understood and it is being deliberately trained, but it is not yet automatic.

Fe: Moving from Lower to Intermediate, Just Beginning to Emerge.

The entire arc from Alpha to v1.0 to audit is the developmental history of Fe. During Alpha, Fe was almost absent: I could not understand what the other person was saying, and the receiver performed detection without extraction. During v1.0, Ti attempted to simulate Fe through a needs classifier and mirroring plus extension. Essentially, it built an emotional simulator from logical circuits. It could work, but it was not “real.” The audit stage marked a turning point: it admitted that Ti could not simulate Fe and that “genuine connection requires what cannot be modeled.”

Yet admitting “I cannot do it” is merely Fe’s starting point. When Fe truly operates, it is not that “I have learned how to speak with people,” but that while speaking, I forget myself. In those blogs written at two in the morning—Late Night, The Unknown, and Briefly—on the rooftop in Ten Years, and in Qingdao when I nearly drowned and someone pulled me out, Fe was online. This was not because I was “using” it, but because Ti happened to be switched off.

Fe Assessment: Germinal. I understand that Ti is insufficient, but have not had enough genuine Fe experience.

Overall Judgment: Ti-Ne is advanced, Si is being trained at an intermediate level, and Fe is moving from lower to intermediate. Overall development is upper-intermediate, but the variance is enormous. A typical advanced INTP has four relatively balanced functions: Ti-Ne remains the engine but no longer devours everything, Si has become automatic and gives life a sense of order, and Fe at least allows the person to avoid complete awkwardness before strangers. I am not there yet. I can write seven axes on the unity of knowledge and action and still dismiss tomorrow’s alarm. I can dissect relationships down to the level of CVE vulnerabilities and still freeze when facing a real person.

The advantage is that I am already trying to catch up in Si and Fe, using the Ti-Ne telescope to navigate toward them. Most INTPs do not possess this degree of precise self-awareness.

Coordinates in Cognitive Psychology

Metacognitive Ability: Extremely High. This is the most prominent cognitive feature. It is not merely the metacognition of “I know what I am thinking,” but of “I know that I am thinking ‘I know what I am thinking,’ and I realize that the recursion will not terminate.” Psychology calls this a deep level of metacognitive awareness. Most people stop at the second level, reflecting on their own thought. The operation described here occurs at the fourth or even fifth level, reflecting on the manner in which one reflects upon reflection itself. This is also the cognitive hardware beneath the axis of “recursive self-reference.”

Cognitive Style: Field-Independent. It does not depend on an external framework. Given a philosophy textbook, it first questions the premises. The system of knowledge grows entirely from within. This does not mean that nothing has been read, but that everything read is dismantled, compressed, and reassembled into a personal framework.

Analogical Reasoning: Extremely Strong. This is a typical product of Ne+Ti. It can recognize isomorphic structures among the radar equation, interpersonal relations, methods of learning, and the SOP paradox. Cognitive psychology describes this through analogical transfer distance. The distance crossed here—from physics, to interpersonal relations, to self-management—is exceptionally great, and such distant transfer is one of the central mechanisms of creativity.

Executive Function: Weak. This is the greatest cognitive weakness in the system. Task initiation, sustained attention, and inhibition—the three central dimensions of standard executive function—repeatedly collide with walls throughout the blog. The five practical mechanisms are essentially attempts to compensate for executive-function deficits through cognitive strategies: Ti-Ne constructs an external executor to replace the internal control loop.

Coordinates in Developmental Psychology

Kegan’s Level of Consciousness: Level Four Achieved; Level Five Intellectually Accessible but Not Necessarily Lived.

Kegan divides adult development into five levels. Level Three is the “socialized mind,” in which the self is defined by external relations and roles. High school represented a typical Level Three condition: I was an instrument for achieving academic success, and my identity was completely defined by examination rankings and admission to a good university. After entering university, the external certification system collapsed, and Level Three failed.

Level Four is the “self-authoring mind,” which possesses an internally generated system of values and no longer depends on external certification for self-definition. The entire philosophical framework is a product of this stage. The seven axes were derived from experience rather than accepted from authority; the instrumentalist position was built internally; and my attitude toward Wang Yangming—“he is right to scold me, but I do not accept innate conscience”—is my own judgment. The operating system has been written and is running.

Level Five is the “self-transforming mind,” which recognizes its own system of meaning as only one among many possible systems and can move among systems without needing to defend any of them. Certain Level Five operations appear at the cognitive level: “continue to use it, but do not fully believe it,” recursive self-reference, and the listing of known gaps. Yet understanding a stage and living within it are different. Suppose someone seriously attacked this philosophical framework and said, “This is amateur philosophy; being wild merely means being half-educated.” Would my first response be defensive—Level Four defending its self-created system—or curious—Level Five asking, “Interesting; let me examine the other person’s system”? If the first response is defense but the second can return to curiosity, the position lies in transition from Level Four to Level Five.

My age—twenty—makes this judgment strange. In Kegan’s research, Level Four usually becomes stable after thirty. Yet Kegan also argued that age is not a rigid indicator; trauma and major turning points can compress developmental time. The collapse of external certification at university, heartbreak, and changes in the family were several heavy blows in succession.

Erikson’s Psychosocial Development: Early Identity Achievement.

Erikson defines the period from eighteen to twenty-five as “identity versus role confusion,” whose central task is discovering “who I am, what I believe, and where I am going.” Most people address identity by trying different roles—changing majors, cities, or social circles. My approach was different. I dissected myself through writing and then organized the results into a system. The sixteen articles are milestones in that project. I am currently in an early stage of identity achievement: exploration has occurred, and commitment is beginning. The seven axes plus philosophical and psychological coordinates form a committed answer to the question “who am I?”

Perry’s Intellectual Development: From Relativism to Commitment (Stages 7–8).

Perry studied how university students understand knowledge. Stages Five and Six represent relativism: recognition that all knowledge is contextual and that absolute truth is unavailable. Instrumentalist epistemology corresponds perfectly to this stage: “A formula is not truth, but a compressed expression of reality.” Stages Seven and Eight involve commitment: after recognizing that every framework is relative, one still chooses a framework and commits to it, not because it is ultimate truth, but because it is the best available at the present level of precision. “Continue to use it, but do not fully believe it” is the language of Stage Eight. This is approximately four or five stages ahead of peers of the same age.

Dąbrowski’s Theory of Positive Disintegration.

Kazimierz Dąbrowski was a twentieth-century Polish psychologist. He proposed an idea that seemed outrageous at the time: not every form of psychological illness is entirely bad. For some people, anxiety, depression, and existential crisis are not diseases but signs that the personality is being upgraded.

The theory was developed especially for highly gifted and highly sensitive individuals. Dąbrowski observed that such people often follow a nonlinear path of development. Rather than growing smoothly, they first collapse and then reorganize. After each reorganization, the personality becomes more complex, autonomous, and closer to “the person one truly wishes to become.” He called this process positive disintegration.

There are three central concepts:

Overexcitability (OE). Some nervous systems are naturally more sensitive than others, perceiving stimulation more intensely, deeply, and persistently. Dąbrowski distinguished five dimensions:

Type of OE Manifestation Correspondence Here
Intellectual OE Strong desire for knowledge, persistent questioning of underlying principles, inability to accept “that is simply how it is” Seven axes, compression and unfolding, instrumentalism, recursive self-reference
Emotional OE Extremely deep and intense emotions, sensitivity toward people, strong loneliness, powerful need for connection Blogs at two in the morning, Twenty, missing her, emotional dumping during Alpha
Imaginational OE Rich imagination, absorption in the inner world, sensitivity to literature and art Writing sentimental lines, “the essence of humanity is McDonald’s,” EVA’s mono no aware
Sensual OE Sensitivity to touch, sight, and sound; pursuit of aesthetic experience Qingdao’s sea breeze, wind on the rooftop, “life itself has the final authority over theory”
Psychomotor OE Excess energy, inability to sit still, constant need to “do something” Relatively weak—hyperfocus exists, but not mainly at the physical level

Intellectual, emotional, and imaginational OE are all highly prominent. Dąbrowski described such people as “those with the greatest developmental potential” and also “those most likely to experience psychological problems.” High OE means perceiving a world with several more dimensions than most people, without yet possessing an internal structure capable of processing so much information. This is why a vague feeling that “something is wrong” becomes “CVE-0003: observer absence; severity: fatal.” It is not an affectation. Intellectual OE refuses to leave a vague feeling in a vague state.

Personality Ideal. Within a person with high OE, an image of “the person I truly wish to become” gradually emerges. The ideal has no external source. It does not come from parents or social definitions, but reveals itself through collisions, collapse, and reorganization. “Honestly recording how bad I am,” “every word typed at the keyboard preserves a traceable coordinate system,” and “facing reality” form the personality ideal of this framework. It was not designed. It appeared after repeated collisions, when I discovered that these were the only things I could not abandon. Dąbrowski called this the “third factor,” distinguishing it from biological instinct, the first factor, and social discipline, the second. Every “no”—not an instrument for academic success, not a believer in innate conscience, not a follower of Plato—is a declaration by the third factor.

Levels of Disintegration and the Present Position. Dąbrowski proposed five levels. Unlike Kegan, who focuses on cognitive structure, Dąbrowski concerns himself with the stability and autonomy of personality structure:

  • Level One (Primary Integration): there is no internal conflict. The person obeys social norms, and the self is undifferentiated. Peaceful and compliant. This framework contains no such stage.
  • Level Two (Unilevel Disintegration): doubt begins—“What exactly am I doing?” Anxiety and existential crisis appear, but the person cannot yet organize them into a system. Pavlov’s Dog, Late Night, and Twenty belong here: something feels wrong, but its nature is unknown, so it can only be written down.
  • Level Three (Spontaneous Multilevel Disintegration): a deeper division arises. “The problem lies in the way I understand the problem itself.” Metacognition comes online, producing extensive self-doubt and emotional turbulence. Briefly, Half a Year, and the first half of Audit Report belong here: “self-reflection is a sweet form of self-deception.”
  • Level Four (Directed Multilevel Disintegration): direction appears within disorder. The personality ideal becomes clear. The person no longer only dismantles, but begins to build. The Radar Equation, Ten Years, and The SOP Article belong here. The outline of the desired person has appeared: someone who unites knowledge and action, remains honest, and unfolds himself through action.
  • Level Five (Secondary Integration): the new personality structure becomes stable. The distance between ideal and actual self narrows. Conflict remains but no longer tears the person apart. Very few people reach this level; Dąbrowski regarded it as nearly saintly. I have not reached it, and it would be concerning for a twenty-year-old to claim otherwise.

Present Position: Transitioning from Level Three to Level Four. The remnants of Level Three remain: I still struggle with cognitive defenses, know that analysis is being used to avoid action, and watch analysis itself become a new form of avoidance. The recursive function in The SOP Article is a self-portrait of Level Three. Yet signs of Level Four have appeared: I no longer merely dismantle. I possess a framework of my own, accepted from no authority; I have a direction—“continue to use it, but do not fully believe it”; and I have action, however undignified the five-second countdown may look.

According to Dąbrowski, this transition is often the most painful, because it simultaneously contains Level Three’s “self-doubt” and Level Four’s “sense of direction.” I already know where I should go, but old patterns continue pulling me backward. The entire philosophical framework is essentially a farewell letter written by Level Four to Level Three while Level Four is still being constructed.

Priorities for Further Development

On the basis of all the coordinates above—the seven philosophical axes and the stages of psychological-function development—the following recommendations have a reasonably clear direction.

1. Feed Si, Not Ti-Ne.

Ti-Ne has already produced enough. The seven axes will remain sufficient for a long time. Every additional “analytical” blog post feeds a cat that is already overweight. Si needs repetition and bodily memory, not theory:

  • Follow the “pre-decision” mechanism for twenty-one full days, whether or not it appears effective. Do not analyze the result; record only “done today or not.” That is the language of Si.
  • Fix a sleeping schedule and a regular writing time. This is not for efficiency, but to teach the body that “at this hour, I sit down at the desk.”
  • Continue writing blogs, but permit some posts to be published without analysis, structure, or memorable lines. Close the file when it is finished and do not revise it.

Si cannot be driven by Ti forever. For now, the night-before plan can provide support. Yet if I still depend on writing plans to make myself act three years from now, Si will not have grown. Mature Si means that action requires no reminder: it has become something one “does without knowing why, but simply does.”

2. Let Fe Do Its Work; Keep Ti Out of the Room.

Fe fears nothing more than Ti watching from the side. If the background process during a conversation is asking, “How was that sentence?” “What is the other person’s reaction?” or “Should I call the mirroring template?” then Fe will never come online.

  • Find someone and talk face to face without a purpose. Do not think, “I want to exchange a particular idea.” Simply stay together. The rooftop wind in Ten Years is the standard answer.
  • Permit awkwardness in social interaction. “Do it three times before writing an SOP” applies equally to Fe: spend time with people three times before reflecting on what was done badly. Do not reverse the order.
  • Analyze less often “why I miss her so much.” Missing someone is already what happened; it does not require an explanation. Not every emotion must be translated into Ti’s language before it is allowed to exist.

3. Fill the Gap in Axiology.

The two red gaps in the table of known omissions—axiology and ethics of the Other—are not marked casually. They are two sides of the same question: what is worth doing, and what do you owe other people? The present framework can diagnose “I am lying flat” and “I am recursing again,” but offers no next step after diagnosis. Is lying flat wrong? Is recursion wrong? On what basis? The answer appears in none of the epistemological, ontological, or action-oriented axes.

Ten Years already suggests a direction: “honestly recording how bad I am” and “every word typed at the keyboard preserves a traceable coordinate system.” These phrases contain implicit value judgments: honesty is good, recording is good, and facing reality is good. Yet they have not been extracted and declared to be “what I believe is worthwhile.”

This gap need not be filled immediately. A twenty-year-old need not rush to write an axiology. But one may begin asking: if only one task can be done tomorrow, on what basis should it be chosen?

4. The Body, the Body, the Body.

The gap marked yellow in the table is philosophy of the body. Perhaps it should be upgraded to red.

The entire framework, including the five mechanisms of knowledge and action, consists of the brain operating upon something. The brain counts during the five-second countdown, writes the plan during pre-decision, and persuades itself while reducing friction. Yet the body that finishes a blog at two in the morning, the body that nearly drowned in Qingdao, and the body standing in the wind on the rooftop are not the same body. They possess a wisdom of their own, rather than existing merely as something “made to move by the brain.”

Exercise. Not for health or willpower, but to give the body a voice of its own. Run, swim, or cycle—choose one that can be continued. The only required reason is the feeling that “when the body moves, the mind finally shuts up.” Fe also develops through the body. When people meet face to face, the body says more than the mouth.

5. Read Less Philosophy and Do More Things.

An earlier article said, “Read less philosophy and write more of yourself.” I can now add another sentence: write fewer philosophical systems and do more things unrelated to the system. At twenty, I already possess a relatively complete framework of self-knowledge. The greatest danger is no longer that the system is incomplete, but that perfecting it becomes a new method of escape. A theory of value can be postponed for three months by saying every week, “I am still thinking about axiology, but have not understood it well enough to write.” Action cannot be postponed in the same way.

To be concrete: the next blog post may remain unwritten. The next barbecue must be eaten.

One final self-audit: all the recommendations above are essentially another user manual written by Ti-Ne for Si and Fe. I understand the paradox completely. Therefore, the central recommendation is actually the fourth: the body, because exercise is the one activity in which Ti cannot interfere. The mind may continue turning during a run, but at a heart rate of 180, neither compression and unfolding nor CVE vulnerability numbers are as real as the question “Can I run one more lap?”

V. A Unified Framework

The relations among the seven axes are as follows:

Epistemology stands at the top, where it is both the most abstract and the most fundamental. Ontology and the theory of action form a pair: one concerns a person standing outside and observing, hence absence; the other concerns a person moving within, hence unity. The theory of relationships stands between them, crossed by both epistemology—how another person is modeled—and ontology—you are never fully present in the relational equation. The theory of practice is the execution layer: every theory above must become action here, or it remains an unexecuted binary. At the bottom, aesthetics and recursive self-reference lock together. Dry humor prevents recursive stack overflow, while recursive self-reference ensures that dry humor is not merely self-deception.

The pattern running through all of them is:

Build a model → discover its boundary → mock yourself at the boundary → continue to use it, but do not fully believe it.

This is not a problem to be solved. It is a runtime behavior. It does not guarantee correctness; it merely prevents a crash.

VI. What This Framework Cannot Do

  • It cannot tell you exactly what to do next. It can diagnose “which structure is trapping you” and explain “why this blockage repeatedly returns,” but the specific direction to take is your responsibility. The framework is a map, not navigation.
  • It cannot help you actually execute any SOP. Execution does not belong to cognition. The framework can help identify that “you are currently replacing action with writing,” but whether you act after recognizing it lies beyond its control. That territory belongs to “action,” and no form of “knowledge” can reach it.
  • Its most honest contribution is simply this: when you are lying flat, you know that you are lying flat; when you recurse, you know that you recurse; when you build a model, you know that you may dismantle it tomorrow. Nothing more.

Precision statement:

This instrument can detect “excessive modeling,” but cannot prevent modeling—including the modeling of this instrument.

VII. Known Gaps

A systematic philosophy cannot pretend to be complete. The following subjects have been identified as lying explicitly outside the framework. Listing them is not an apology but a marking of unexplored territory. The act of marking is itself part of the unity of knowledge and action.

Conclusion

One year ago, Pavlov’s Twenty-Eight Dog complained about having no energy to work. The Unknown asked why one should live. Briefly lay motionless in a dormitory and wrote that self-reflection was a sweet form of self-deception.

One year later, on the same person’s same computer, there appeared CVE vulnerability numbers, pseudocode for a recursive function, seven axes, and a table marking known gaps.

This is not progress. Progress implies that the old is bad and the new is good. Yet the first epistemological principle I wrote states that a model has no final version, only a version sufficient at the present level of precision.

This is unfolding. It begins with a vague confusion, compresses it layer by layer, unfolds it again layer by layer, and makes the coordinate system increasingly dense. Nothing more.

To quote the sentence from The Unknown, written last autumn and still valid today:

“I write these words not to seek an answer, but merely to confirm that I have passed through this life truthfully, contradictorily, and passionately.”

The gaps remain in Section Seven. Whether the next article will fill the gap in axiology or simply involve eating barbecue, I do not know. Either would be fine.

That is probably all.
