2026-05-21 Half a Year

Over the past six months, I finished organising New Generation, entered a relationship, and then watched a major upheaval strike my family during the winter holiday. The relationship gradually cooled and entered a period of irritation. My postgraduate-exam preparation slowed; I surrendered my own agency; I turned my former partner into an emotional pillar and exposed some of the ugliest parts of myself. Then came the breakup, the renewed doubt over whether my existence meant anything and whether it was necessary to go on existing, the audiovisual event, the loss of a large group of friends, and one academic failure after another. I brought much of this upon myself.

I suppose I have discovered quite a few things.

First, I do not know how to sustain an intimate relationship—or, to put it another way, how to love someone. This has been true for most of my life. I turned myself into a tool: a tool built to win academic distinction, and not even a particularly successful one. The social fear I experienced in middle and high school came from the same place. I did not know how an intimate bond was established, or how it was supposed to continue.

That was why, after losing the competition at university, I began looking elsewhere for a reason to live. It was also how I arrived at what has happened over the past few days.

Break the problem down further and it becomes a collection of much more concrete abilities: making small talk; expressing concern for another person; accepting concern in return; judging proportion and boundaries in an interaction; putting thoughts into words; hearing what someone actually says and detecting what remains unspoken. There are many more. Every one of them needs work.

Second, I am poor at managing my emotions and at separating one person’s responsibility from another’s. I can, in fact, recognise what I am feeling and dig down to understand why the feeling arose. Under the present circumstances, however, I have been unable to let it go or to separate emotion from behaviour. The result is that the emotion continues to torment me.

This returns to the question of how to regain command of my own life—how to hurry up and turn from a P into a J, I suppose. The practical measures are fairly plain: restore the normal order of daily life, divide complex tasks into manageable parts and categories, and approach things with a clearer sequence of thought. Yet the most important step is simply to begin.

And whatever happens, I must first learn to rely on myself. On myself. I have to stand up by myself and move forward one step at a time. Other people, even a partner, can provide only limited support; most of the work remains mine. I must look after my own small plot of ground rather than handing my emotions to someone else to process. That is childish and burdensome for the other person, and it may also produce a second injury—either to me or to them.

Third, I need the capacity to act. Whenever I say or think something, my first response should be to do it, rather than leaving it to wear itself out inside my private mental world. To be honest, I can barely recognise who I am at the moment. That may be the saddest part of all.

What comes next, then, is the recovery of a sense of order and of some command over daily life. I can begin by getting up early tomorrow, preparing and studying properly, and then making an honest effort to meet new people.

There are many other lessons from conversation, together with smaller techniques and goals, but those can wait. The main issues are my emotional stability and my ability to sustain intimacy.

And I am truly sorry to this group of friends. Above all, I am sorry to my former partner. I hurt that young woman again. I may owe her for the rest of my life.

I regret the end of our relationship as well. Had I been only a little more mature, perhaps it would not have ended this way. We met, yet were not meant to remain together. We met, yet were not meant to remain together.
