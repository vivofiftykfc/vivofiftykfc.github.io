Room to Choose: ZHW's Framework of Thought v4

I began with a question about making money: what can I actually do with the skills, resources, and relationships I have? Then I realised that career choices, intimacy, and friendship raise much the same question. Another one kept returning as well: if chance has a hand in the outcome, why should I still prepare, act, and take responsibility for my choices?

I have put these questions together under the provisional name room to choose: the paths a person can actually take, given the limits of their body, resources, institutions, other people, and time. Action changes those conditions. New paths may open, and old ones may close.

I. Where the Question Came From

I have written before about understanding the world, about being drawn to a particular thing, and about crossing the distance between wanting to act and acting. Now I want to know what keeps an action going once it has begun. When reality changes the plan, how do I decide whether to persist or change course?

Those lines of thought appear in earlier essays. The Radar Equation and Love and Outside the Map ask what a model can explain. The UFO versus Sisyphus asks how something can call forth a sense of value. The Final Three Kilometres from Knowledge to Action is about execution. Everyone, Feel asks what is lost when an experience is reduced to its outcome.

Some of the questions come from life itself. Something that once held my attention gradually recedes into memory, and an older emptiness shows through again. The past really happened; it simply no longer pulls at me in quite the same way. When I am on my own, what gives the world weight again? Seeing how other people live, and widening my understanding of the world, is one way of answering that question.

II. Limited Control and Fate

My present understanding of “fate” begins with something I said earlier:

All we can do is take care of what is in front of us, broaden our view as far as we can, and meet the requirements of the goal we are pursuing. The rest still depends on luck.

I later called fate “a mystical name for a chaotic system.” I can study, exercise, make things, meet people, and decide where to put my effort next. I cannot arrange changes in policy, the timing of an opportunity, or another person’s choice. My actions can affect my surroundings, but they cannot determine an outcome on their own. That is what I mean by limited control.

George Soros’s account of reflexivity offers a point of comparison: people’s judgements shape their actions, and their actions change social reality in turn. Many people take part at once. I cannot know all their judgements or calculate in advance what their actions will produce together. Fate is my name for the conditions I cannot fully see.

I still plan what to study, what to make, how to care for my body, and where to find information. I set deadlines for projects. A plan can organise what I do now; it cannot write the whole course of the decades ahead. As I put it more bluntly, I cannot pin myself to a life of “marriage, children, saving money, growing old, and dying.” Taking a temporary goal or identity as a rule for the rest of my life is the kind of attachment to self I mean here.

The thing in front of me is still worth doing seriously. I said I wanted to “keep experiencing and going deep, with everything I have”: choose something and work at it, while staying in touch with the world beyond the plan. To “follow fate’s lead and enjoy it” means doing the work in my hands and allowing an unexpected opportunity to change my direction. Outside the Map described how reality revises a model. A life plan needs room for the same revision.

Regret makes the unchosen road look like a version of this life in which I keep everything I have and correct just one mistake. Yet taking that road would also have changed the people I met, what I learned, and what it cost me. “Perhaps another choice would have turned out worse” was how I put it then. There is no complete report card for the road not taken, so I cannot compare its best imagined outcome with every cost of the life I actually have. Everyone, Feel asked what disappears when experience is compressed into an outcome. The same loss occurs when I compare past choices.

The past still calls for reflection, and I remain responsible for what I did. But after reflecting, I have to return to the present. When I said, “Don’t dwell, don’t be lost, don’t be anxious,” I was not asking myself never to feel those things again. Anxiety can point to a risk; regret can reveal what my judgement missed. What will I do with that information now? In Neither Escort nor Welcome; Respond without Storing, I borrowed a line from the Zhuangzi to write about this: prepare, respond, and let the past become experience instead of a permanent identity.

III. Projects, Feedback, and Social Standing

University entrance exams, postgraduate entrance exams, research tasks, and commercial orders differ greatly in content, but they share a structure. Within a limited time, someone gathers the conditions they have, produces a result, and lets reality test it. When I said that “a person’s life is made up of project after project,” I had this succession of efforts in mind. A project gives the work at hand a boundary:

$$P=(g,\mathbf K_0,\tau,y^*)$$

Here, g is the problem to be solved, K₀ the conditions available at the start, τ the time limit, and y* the result I hope to deliver. Put a goal, resources, time, and a deliverable together, and a vague wish begins to become something I can work on. K₀ will change as the work proceeds; the next section considers how it accumulates.

A project may take a long time, while the actions within it need shorter feedback. I wrote:

To keep doing these projects, I need to build positive feedback and close the loop. Being conscious of my image, and social standing itself, can be good rewards that keep the loop going.

Broken down, that loop is small and repeated:

$$a_t\longrightarrow y_t\xrightarrow{\text{检验}}r_t\longrightarrow a_{t+1}$$

An action aₜ produces a result yₜ. I test it, receive feedback rₜ, and decide what to do next. Can I solve the problem without help? Does the work run? Does a collaborator want to keep using it? These are results I can see before the whole project is finished. Meeting an expectation encourages me to continue; getting something wrong tells me what to change. The problem of knowledge and action discussed in The Final Three Kilometres from Knowledge to Action comes down, here, to actions I can actually check.

I care about how I am seen. I once said, “Showing off feels fucking good.” Recognition and a rise in standing are forms of feedback that make me happy. On a long project, visible rewards sometimes help me begin the next round. Beyond the reward, the work may also leave behind skills, something I made, trust, and new conditions for collaboration. The result of one project becomes the starting point of another. That is where the project cycle meets the accumulation of capital.

Rewards need a place within that cycle. In Everyone, Feel, I wrote about a project shrinking to its completion record and an experience shrinking to a metric. Of course I can enjoy having done something well. The Laozi‘s “act without resting on what one has done” might mean, here, remembering what I set out to do after the pleasure has passed. Social standing can get me started again; I still have to decide what the next work is for.

IV. From a Change of State to Capital Accumulation

Feedback within a project changes how I work. What the project leaves behind changes what I can do next. That second change is the beginning of capital accumulation. To see how it happens, I need to ask why an action is valuable to someone else.

Why would someone need another person to provide a product or service? They are in a state s₀ and want to reach a state s₁ they prefer, but lack something required to get there. It may be a skill, time, a tool, trust, or the ability to work with others. That gap gives rise to demand.

If I can fill it, something that was previously out of reach becomes possible. If the other person is willing to pay, a transaction may follow. This is the essence of making money. Yet solving a problem does not guarantee that I receive the corresponding income. Ownership, entry requirements, contracts, and platform rules also determine who can charge and how the proceeds are divided. Creating value, receiving income, and distributing income are three different processes. I have used “reducing friction” and “reducing entropy” to describe crossing an obstacle. Those metaphors concern how a goal is reached; people still choose the goal. Some seek security, and others seek adventure.

One transaction brings income. If, when it is over, I also retain a better-developed skill, a reusable tool, the other person’s trust, or a division of work that has already been tested, I need not start the next round from scratch. I call these conditions that survive into the next round and help me act again capital. Accumulation happens between rounds: the result of the last one becomes the starting condition of the next.

Money can buy time. Time can be spent learning. Skills can produce work, and that work may bring trust and collaboration. Repeated work of the same kind can turn individual experience into a process that several people carry out together. This gives one route from labour to operating a venture and accumulating capital. It suits work that can be repeated and shared; a singular piece of work or service has a different kind of value.

The change of state and the accumulation of capital can be written together:

$$\mathbf K_t=(F_t,H_t,S_t,R_t,O_t,T_t)$$

$$s_t\xrightarrow[\mathbf K_t]{a_t}s_{t+1},\qquad \mathbf K_{t+1}=\mathbf K_t+\mathbf G_t-\mathbf D_t.$$

In these expressions, s is a person’s state and a is an action. K represents the conditions available at the time: financial capital F, human capital H, social capital S, reputational capital R, organisational capital O, and disposable time T. G is what an action adds and leaves behind; D is what it consumes or what depreciates.

An action may move a person towards a new state, and it changes what they have available next time. Study improves a skill but uses time. A collaboration may build trust and organisational experience, or later circumstances may make them less useful. K is a set of conditions that can turn into one another. Its components cannot simply be added up as a person’s “total worth.”

This also helped me understand a pain I had not been able to explain. I said that my social, reputational, and organisational capital had recently taken a serious blow. What hurt more was “not knowing how to make these forms of capital grow again.” The money I have and the skills I can still use are relatively easy to recognise. Social capital, reputation, and a place in an organisation depend on particular people and circles: whom can I turn to, who is willing to trust me, and with whom can I get something done? I remain the same person, but some paths that used to be open suddenly are not. Losing my place in one circle does not mean everyone will judge me in the same way.

How can these forms of capital grow again? I have to return to concrete work. Finish something whose result others can see, and reputation gains new evidence. Build trust through dealings with people, and relationships can grow. Work with others, divide the work, and do it again, and organisational experience remains. An old judgement may persist, but the next action has a new starting point. I would rather ask: what did this round leave behind? Can I use it next time?

Timing matters as well. An early collaboration may lead to more opportunities, while a mistake may narrow a route that used to be open. Looking back, it is easy to attribute every difference to personal ability. For me, wealth is better understood through an effective set of choices: which things that I could once only imagine can I now actually do? Amartya Sen’s capability approach also distinguishes owning resources from being able to do something with them. I use it to examine the question, rather than attributing “wealth equals the effective set of choices” to Sen.

This model describes how the conditions for action grow, shrink, and change form. It can help me see which paths are open, but it cannot choose s₁ for me. Which path is worth taking still depends on values and the situation I am in.

V. Relationships: A Shared World and Separate People

The capital model also changed how I see relationships. Two people help one another and share time, information, and resources. Over time, they build trust, shared memories, and experience in handling difficulties together. These changes expand what each person can do.

At first I even said that I needed to “convince her this deal would not be a loss.” That sentence captures the question of trust and what each person brings when a relationship begins. It misses what two people may create by living together. I once borrowed Aristophanes’ story in Plato’s Symposium to say that “falling in love and forming a family is, in a sense, entering a more wonderful system.” The story of the creature with two heads and four arms directs my attention to a life formed together, beyond what each person put in at the start.

In symbols: A + B → A′, B′, AB. Each person changes, and a “we” comes into being as well. The understanding built through shared experience cannot easily be handed to a third person. One person’s joys and difficulties enter the other’s life. The new AB does not erase A or B: each retains friends, choices, and the right to refuse. A relationship can bring benefits, while the other person remains a particular human being.

Friendship usually has another shape. Friends live their own lives but can still turn to one another when needed. They may not have spoken for a long time and yet pick up where they left off. A friend can help, and can also say from a different position, “I think you’ve got this wrong.” Several friends who know and help one another form a network larger than any single pair.

Friends may be closer than partners, so these distinctions are only ways of seeing common patterns of cooperation. Relationships can generate social capital. Interpersonal System Audit Report and Outside the Map remind me that trusting and understanding someone still means allowing them their own judgement, including the freedom to say no.

VI. Values and Ideals

The capital model shows me what I can do; relationships show me the kinds of life I might share with others. As for what I want to do with my abilities, I put it bluntly at the time:

What I actually do depends on what I want to do: where the money is better, where I can work on something capable of advancing humanity—or even hastening its destruction—and where I might find love and hope.

Money, being seen, understanding the world, making things, love, and hope all attract me. I have also said that “being the centre of attention feels damn good.” Fame brings real pleasure, and it may open opportunities. Some things pay now; others are things I want to keep doing simply because I have done them and still care. I am willing to give time to both.

In The Moon and Sixpence, I tried to trace a path from “action → feeling → responsibility → relating it to the meaning of one’s life” towards an ideal. That sequence still helps me. Some interests have to be practised for a while before I know whether I am willing to bear the responsibilities that come with them. Understanding why I feel drawn to something does not make the feeling disappear. “What can be explained cannot necessarily be dissolved,” as I wrote in Outside the Map, is about this kind of experience too.

I am also drawn to technologies powerful enough to change many people’s lives. “Advancing humanity, or hastening its destruction” sounds provocative, but it names the appeal of power itself. If I actually take part in such a project, I have to look at whom it gives opportunities to and who bears its risks. In The Moon and Sixpence, I worried that an idealist can be driven along by his own goal. When it is my turn to act, that worry has to become a judgement about real consequences.

Do not pin myself to a single kind of life.
Do my best to build abilities, resources, and understanding, and do well the worthwhile work in front of me; keep my view open, so that I have more and more choices.
Accept the immense, incalculable role of chance in the world; call it fate if I like. When fate comes, respond to it. When it does not, carry on with my own work.
Fame, money, and status can all be pursued and enjoyed, without letting any one of them define who I am.
Experience things, go deeply into them, create, love, and take on questions large enough to matter.
Let what has happened stand. Do not punish my present self with a parallel world that does not exist.

I am in no hurry to decide what my final self will be. First I want to become capable of entering a wider and wider world, and then see where this life eventually takes me.

VII. Overview and Open Questions

Dimension Current view Related concepts Open question
Worldview Reality includes bodies, institutions, other people, and chance; action changes the conditions of the next choice. Realism, path dependence, reflexivity How much of an outcome came from preparation, and how much from timing?
Epistemology A model compresses reality and must be revised by experience, action, and counterexamples. Instrumentalism, fallibilism How do I keep a useful model from appearing universally valid?
Axiology Money, recognition, love, creation, and a wish to affect reality coexist; an ideal may form through practice. Value pluralism, value perception, calling How should I choose when these values conflict?
Freedom I can affect an outcome only to a limited extent, but I can prepare, commit, respond, and change course. Limited control, situated freedom When should I persist, and when should I revise a choice?
Action A project carries a bounded task; real feedback tests and corrects what I do. Unity of knowledge and action, Dewey on experience How can feedback help action without taking over the goal?
Wealth Resources matter insofar as they become real abilities and effective choices; institutions affect who receives the returns. Capabilities, conversion of capital, distribution Does having more choices necessarily make for a better life?
Relationships A relationship may enlarge each person’s possibilities and create a “we”; each remains a separate person. The Symposium myth, intersubjectivity How can I describe exchange and cooperation without reducing a person to capital?
Ethics The more my abilities affect others, the more I must attend to consent, refusal, consequences, and accountability. Ethics of the other, ethics of responsibility How can a private judgement of value be tested in public?

The terms in the table mark related questions, not theories I claim to have established. I cannot yet measure “room to choose” in any workable way. For now, it helps me organise experience and judgement; when it fails to explain something, I will have to revise it.

In The Topology of Thought, I tried to observe a person’s way of thinking through the connections that repeatedly appear among concepts. Here fate, projects, capital, and relationships have begun to connect as well. I want to become more capable of acting, and to understand what I want to do with that ability. The next real choice will show me whether this account is of any use.
